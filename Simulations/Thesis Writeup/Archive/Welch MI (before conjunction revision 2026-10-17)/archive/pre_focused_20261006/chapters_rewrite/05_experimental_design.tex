\chapter{Experimental Design}
\label{chap:design}

The simulation study evaluates whether Expanded Welch improves on Normal
Wald when comparing the MI of two populations. It measures false positives
when the population MI values are equal, detection when they differ, and how
often each method returns a valid p-value.
Each experimental setting starts with two joint probability tables,
\(P\) and \(Q\), whose margins and MI values are chosen before sampling.
Independent samples are then drawn from each population, and both tests are
applied to the resulting count tables. Because the population MI values are
known, we can assess the tests against the true difference rather than the
differences that arise by chance in individual samples.

\section{Constructing fixed joint distributions}

To vary association without changing category frequencies, we construct
each table with prescribed row and column margins and a known MI\@. A binary
example shows how the margins can remain fixed while \(X\) and \(Y\)
become dependent.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Constructing a non-independent \(2\times2\) joint distribution.}

To obtain one observation from \(P\), first draw \(X\) with category
probabilities \((0.7,0.3)\). With probability approximately 20.35\%, set
\(Y=X\); otherwise, draw \(Y\) independently with the same category
probabilities. Copying \(X\) makes matching categories more likely than
under independence. Both ways of choosing \(Y\) give it margins
\((0.7,0.3)\), so their mixture keeps the row and column margins fixed.

\begin{samepage}
For example, cell \((1,1)\) requires \(X=1\), which has probability 0.7.
Given \(X=1\), copying always gives \(Y=1\), while an independent draw
gives \(Y=1\) with probability 0.7. Combining the two possibilities gives
\begin{equation*}
 p_{11}\simeq0.7\{0.20347+(1-0.20347)(0.7)\}
 \simeq0.53273.
\end{equation*}
This is the first entry of \(P\) below; the other entries follow by
considering their corresponding category pairs.
\par
\end{samepage}

Construct \(Q\) in the same way, using category probabilities
\((0.8,0.2)\) and a copying probability of approximately 26.09\%.
The two copying probabilities are chosen to give \(I(P)=0.02\) and
\(I(Q)=0.03\) nats.

\begin{samepage}
The resulting joint probability tables are
\begin{equation}
 P\simeq
 \begin{pmatrix}0.53273&0.16727\\0.16727&0.13273\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 Q\simeq
 \begin{pmatrix}0.68175&0.11825\\0.11825&0.08175\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:design-example-pair}
\end{equation}
Their absolute MI difference, 0.01 nats, is the horizontal-axis value
used in the results figures.
\par
\end{samepage}

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Probability transfers in larger tables.}

For larger tables, begin with independence by multiplying the prescribed
row and column probabilities. The main construction adds the same amount
to cells \((1,1)\) and \((2,2)\), and removes that amount from cells
\((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\). Each affected row and column gains and loses the
same amount, so its total is unchanged; all other cells stay unchanged.
This four-cell transfer follows the sparse alternative of
\citet[Section~3(b)]{berrett2021}. For the \(2\times2\) example, this
transfer produces exactly the same table as the copying mechanism above.

For each population, the amount transferred is chosen to reach its target
MI while keeping every cell probability positive. Before sampling, both
tables are checked for positive cells, correct margins and target MI\@.
Appendix~\ref{app:construction} gives the equivalent matrix construction,
its feasible range and the numerical search used to select the amount.

The wider study uses three margin profiles.
For a dimension of size \(k\), the skewed margin used in the design is
\begin{equation}
 m_k(\rho)=\left(\rho,\frac{1-\rho}{k-1},\ldots,
                   \frac{1-\rho}{k-1}\right).
 \label{eq:skew-margin}
\end{equation}
The three principal profiles are specified separately for the two
populations in Table~\ref{tab:margin-profiles}. For a rectangular
\(r\times c\) table, the formula is applied with \(k=r\) to row margins and
\(k=c\) to column margins.
The margin profiles also determine whether equal MI gives identical tables.
At a zero MI difference, the same-margin profiles
produce \(P=Q\) under this construction; the different-skew profile has
\(I(P)=I(Q)\) but \(P\ne Q\).

In the usual effect direction, \(I(P)=I_0\) and
\(I(Q)=I_0+|\Delta|\). The reverse-direction imbalance experiment assigns
the higher target to \(P\).
The statistical convention remains \(\Delta=I(P)-I(Q)\), which is negative in
the usual direction. Figures use the nonnegative magnitude \(|\Delta|\).

The changed-cell experiments ask whether results depend on where association
sits in the table. The ``rare block'' moves the four changed cells from the
upper-left corner to rows and columns with smaller probabilities in skewed
populations. The ``spread'' pattern changes cells throughout the table while
keeping the row and column totals fixed. Each population retains its
specified margins and target MI. Comparing the patterns at these matched
values tests how the placement of association affects performance.

A separate comparison changes the construction rule. The log-linear method
also spreads association through the table but produces different cell
probabilities from the four-cell transfer at matched margins and MI\@.
Appendix~\ref{app:construction} gives the construction formulas.

\section{Sampling, tests and measured outcomes}

The study uses at least 10 observations per population.
For each unique configuration, 20,000 independent pairs of multinomial count
tables are drawn from its fixed \(P\) and \(Q\). Normal Wald and Expanded
Welch are applied to the \emph{same} count-table pair. Differences in their
test outcomes therefore arise from the methods rather than different draws.

Rejection is measured at the same two-sided nominal threshold,
\(\alpha=0.05\), throughout the statistical study. For each method, the
primary plotted rejection rate is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\#\{\text{sample pairs giving a valid p-value no larger than }0.05\}}
      {20{,}000}.
 \label{eq:unconditional-rate}
\end{equation}
At \(|\Delta|=0\) this estimates the false-positive rate. At
\(|\Delta|>0\) it estimates the probability of detecting the specified
difference at the fixed nominal threshold, often called power. Closer to
0.05 means better calibration under the null.
Under an alternative, higher detection is useful only alongside acceptable
null calibration and a usable test result.

An invalid calculation contributes no rejection to Equation
\eqref{eq:unconditional-rate}, but it does not count as evidence for the null.
The valid-result rate shows how often each test returns a usable
p-value. Reporting it alongside rejection distinguishes a low rejection
rate from a failure to calculate the test.

The reported 95\% intervals quantify the simulation uncertainty in those
rates at each
fixed population pair. Intervals for the difference between methods account
for applying both tests to the same sampled tables. Section~\ref{sec:simulation-uncertainty}
gives the interval calculations and rates restricted to valid results.

The ``minimum expected count'' diagnostic is
\(\min_{ij}n_Pp_{ij}\) for \(P\), or \(\min_{ij}n_Qq_{ij}\) for \(Q\).
The design imposes no minimum expected-count requirement, so empty observed
cells and rows remain possible.
To make low validity visible, a hollow marker warns that fewer
than 90\% of outputs of that method were valid; 90\% is a display convention,
not a validated safety threshold.

\section{Experiment families and computation}

Table~\ref{tab:design-families} records the question answered by each family.
The common population construction above applies unless the named factor
changes. Exact parameter sets are in Appendix~\ref{app:construction}.

The principal grid crosses square shapes \(2\times2,3\times3,5\times5,8\times8\)
with the three margin profiles in Table~\ref{tab:margin-profiles}. It fixes
\(I(P)=0.02\) nats and uses
\(|\Delta|\in\{0,0.001,0.002,0.005,0.01,0.02\}\) nats and equal
sample sizes \(n_P=n_Q\in\{10,20,50,100,250,500,1000\}\).
The other families change one or more stated factors from this principal
grid.

The design has 534 distinct population pairs \((P,Q)\). A pair combined
with sample sizes \((n_P,n_Q)\) defines a statistical configuration.
Reusing pairs at different sample sizes gives 2,760 unique configurations,
each sampled 20,000 times, for 55,200,000 table pairs.

excluding population construction and sampling. It varies table shape,
profile, sample size and null or alternative status. Each regime uses 200

interquartile ranges are reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

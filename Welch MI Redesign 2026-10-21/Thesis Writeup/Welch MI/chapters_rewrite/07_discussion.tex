\chapter{Implications and Limitations}
\label{chap:discussion}

\section{Accuracy of the variance approximation}

The experiment shows why the two moment approximations need separate
checks. The predicted sampling variance of \(\Vhat\) can be close to
its observed value while the mean estimate remains above the population
variance coefficient. In the \(4\times4\) different-skew example at
\(n=1{,}000\), the variance predictions are within about 3--4\%, but
the mean estimates are about 17--19\% too large. The moment-matching
formula squares the mean. Replacing that inflated sampling mean by the
smaller population value therefore lowers the population first-order
degrees of freedom, even when the sampling-variance approximation is
reasonably accurate. Mean estimated component degrees of freedom are
lower still at A, about 44\% of the moment target, compared with about
99\% at C.

Near independence, the population first-order variance is small and
quadratic sampling terms remain substantial. The calculation in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} accounts for their contribution
to the observed component means and degrees of freedom. This explains
why increasing sample size and moving away from independence can change
which approximation is responsible for the error. It also shows why
accurate population formulas do not by themselves ensure that substituting
sample probabilities gives accurate degrees of freedom.

A second-order mean correction could address part of this discrepancy.
The observed variation across MI and margins means that its coefficient
should be derived for the population table. A fixed coefficient based
only on table size would leave errors in some settings, and any estimated
correction would require evaluation of the resulting test.

\section{False-positive rates and power}

Expanded Welch brings false-positive rates closer to 5\% in the two
selected small-sample populations, both at common MI 0.02 nats.
Every rule returns a valid result
there, so the lower rejection rates reflect the cutoffs rather than
failed calculations. Across the broad grid, Expanded Welch also reduces
rates that are already below 5\%. The larger cutoff is useful when
it corrects excessive rejection and costly when it deepens conservatism.

The useful null behaviour does not establish that the estimated degrees
of freedom accurately match denominator moments. At A, the standard
error understates the observed variation of the MI difference, while
the low estimated degrees of freedom increase the rejection cutoff.
These effects can compensate in the null rejection rate. At B, the
variance ratio exceeds one and the difference is biased, so the same
explanation cannot be carried over unchanged. Separating these
contributions would require changing the individual estimates while
holding the others fixed.

Calibrated power also depends on the equal-MI population used to fit
the cutoff. At common MI 0.02, Expanded Welch gains power over Wald
at A and B. Moving calibration to the upper equal-MI population while
holding the alternative fixed reverses the gain at A and two null SDs,
and at B and four SDs. Cross-null evaluation shows why matching the
lower null is insufficient: the calibrated rules can still have different
false-positive rates at the upper null. The sample-dependent degrees
of freedom change the ordering of samples, but the observed power gain
is conditional on the calibration population. At C, the differences
remain practically small under both calibrations.

B retains its initial decline in rejection as the true MI difference
increases. Its positive null bias initially offsets the negative change
in the true difference, moving the estimated difference towards zero.
Adjusting the cutoff leaves that distortion in all the compared rules.
Neither a calibrated gain nor a lower nominal rejection rate resolves
the bias in the shared statistic.

\section{Estimation errors that a cutoff cannot correct}

The sparse \(16\times16\) different-skew comparison remains unreliable
under all four rules. Its positive difference bias is larger than the
true negative difference at the 0.01-nat alternative, so almost every
rejection gives the wrong estimated ordering. The calculation in
Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-alternative-bias-shift} explains the sign
reversal. The four-cell construction reproduces the failure, and the
fixed moment reference also rejects too often. Fitting denominator
moments cannot remove bias from the numerator.

The same-skew null illustrates a separate error. Identical population
tables at equal sample sizes cancel the expected difference bias, but
the estimated difference variance is almost twice its observed value
in the sparse copying case. All four rules are conservative. Unequal
sample sizes can also leave unequal residual biases in otherwise
identical populations. The allocation check shows how changed bias,
variance contributions and degrees of freedom affect the result when
one population receives fewer observations.

For an observed application, the MI difference gives estimated size and
direction, while the standard error describes estimated sampling
variation. Report these with the p-value and, for Expanded Welch, the
estimated degrees of freedom. The online-shopping example illustrates
that interpretation, but its unknown population MI values prevent a
direct check of false-positive rates. Simulations from plausible
application populations can examine sensitivity to rare cells, unequal
sample sizes and distinct equal-MI tables. Their conclusions depend on
the populations chosen for that assessment.

\section{Limits of the evidence}

The power settings were selected using earlier false-positive results
and evaluated on independent samples. Each curve keeps \(I(P)=0.02\)
and increases \(I(Q)\) along the copying family; other directions and
association patterns remain untested. The two calibration checks do not
bound rejection across the entire equal-MI null. The allocation check
is restricted to the specified \(4\times4\) populations and three
allocations.

Both constructions use equal row and column margins within each table
and align association locations between populations. Unequal margins
within a table and other locations of dependence remain untested.
The pointwise confidence intervals quantify simulation uncertainty for
the specified populations.

The fixed reference and empirical calibration require known simulation
populations. Replacing sample-dependent degrees of freedom by a fixed
value changes both their magnitude and their dependence on the statistic,
so that comparison does not isolate estimation error. The calibrated
power intervals include fitting and evaluation uncertainty for the fixed
constructed alternatives.

The derivation assumes independent categorical observations, fixed
categories, positive population cell probabilities and positive
first-order MI variance. Paired samples would require a covariance term
in the difference variance. Growing alphabets and continuous estimators
have different approximation errors. Exact independence needs the
second-order argument in Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}.

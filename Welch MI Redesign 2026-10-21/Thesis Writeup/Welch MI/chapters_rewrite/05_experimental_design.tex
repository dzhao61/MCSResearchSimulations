\chapter{Experimental Design}
\label{chap:design}

The experiment evaluates the moment approximations used to derive Expanded
Welch and the false-positive rates and power of the resulting test. It
first measures how the estimated MI variance behaves across repeated
samples from known populations. It then compares four rejection rules
using the same bias-corrected MI difference and standard error. This
separates errors in the derived moments from the performance of a
Student \(t\) distribution applied to the shared statistic. The design follows the aims, data-generating mechanisms,
targets, methods and performance measures framework of \citet{morris2019}.

\section{Research questions and statistical targets}

For a fixed population \(P\) and sample size \(n_P\), expectations and
variances below refer to repeated independent multinomial samples. The
estimator \(\Vhat(P)\) estimates the pointwise-MI variance \(V(P)\);
\(\Vhat(P)/n_P\) is the estimated contribution to the variance of the
MI difference. The sampling variance of \(\Vhat(P)\) is a different
quantity: it describes how that estimated contribution fluctuates across
samples.

The first-order calculation uses
\begin{equation}
 \E\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx V(P),\qquad
 \Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx\frac{\tau^2(P)}{n_P},
 \label{eq:redesign-moment-approximations}
\end{equation}
where \(\tau^2(P)\) is the population variance of the variance influence
function derived in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}. Substitution into the
moment-matching formula gives the population first-order value
\begin{equation}
 \nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}=\frac{2n_PV(P)^2}{\tau^2(P)}.
 \label{eq:redesign-first-order-df}
\end{equation}
The sampling-moment target instead uses the actual mean and variance of
the estimator:
\begin{equation}
 \nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}
 =\frac{2\{\E\Vhat(P)\}^2}{\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}.
 \label{eq:redesign-moment-df}
\end{equation}
The assumptions in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} ensure positive population
variance quantities for the tested populations. The two degrees-of-freedom
values can nevertheless differ at finite sample sizes. Dividing their
definitions before applying either approximation gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}}{\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}}
 =\left\{\frac{V(P)}{\E\Vhat(P)}\right\}^{2}
   \frac{\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}{\tau^2(P)/n_P}.
 \label{eq:redesign-df-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first factor measures the contribution of the mean approximation;
the second measures the contribution of the sampling-variance
approximation. A mean estimate above \(V(P)\) lowers the first-order
degrees of freedom relative to the moment target when the variance
prediction is otherwise accurate. The same calculations are made
separately for \(Q\).

For each population, the experiment estimates the two moments directly
and compares the population predictions in
Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-moment-approximations} with them. It also checks
the mean plug-in prediction \(\E(\widehat\tau^2)/n_P\), because the
implemented test estimates \(\tau^2(P)\) from each sample. Component
means, medians and percentile ranges of \(\widehat\nu_P\) describe the
additional effect of substituting empirical probabilities into the
first-order formula.

The second question concerns false-positive rates and power relative to
Normal Wald and a sample-size comparator. Even accurate denominator
moments do not determine the joint distribution of the MI difference and
its standard error. The moment checks and rejection comparisons therefore
answer distinct parts of the evaluation.

\section{Four rejection rules with a shared statistic}

Every rule uses the bias-corrected difference in
Equation~\eqref{eq:bias-corrected-difference} and the standard error in
Equation~\eqref{eq:shared-se}. Normal Wald compares their ratio \(T\)
with the standard normal distribution. Expanded Welch uses its existing
estimated degrees of freedom from Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}.

The sample-size comparator uses Hutcheson's assignment of sample size to
each component
\citep{hutcheson1970}, applied to the shared MI variance contributions.
It replaces the component degrees of freedom by \(n_P\) and \(n_Q\), giving
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\nu_{\mathrm{size}}=
 \frac{\{\Vhat(P)/n_P+\Vhat(Q)/n_Q\}^{2}}
 {\{\Vhat(P)/n_P\}^{2}/n_P+\{\Vhat(Q)/n_Q\}^{2}/n_Q}.
 \label{eq:redesign-size-df}
\end{equation}
This is a comparator for the degrees-of-freedom assignment: the numerator
and standard error remain the MI quantities from Chapter~\ref{chap:baselines}.
The component assignment is \(n_i\), whereas ordinary Welch's comparison
of sample means uses \(n_i-1\) \citep{welch1947}. The experiment asks
whether calculating the MI variance sensitivity provides a benefit over
the sample-size assignment.

A fourth rule uses a fixed moment-matched reference, estimated from
independent simulations of the corresponding equal-MI populations:
\begin{equation}
 \nu_{\mathrm{ref}}=
 \frac{2\{\E(\SEhat^2)\}^{2}}{\Var(\SEhat^2)}.
 \label{eq:redesign-combined-reference}
\end{equation}
Independence of the population samples gives
\begin{align}
 \E(\SEhat^2)&=\frac{\E\Vhat(P)}{n_P}+\frac{\E\Vhat(Q)}{n_Q},\\
 \Var(\SEhat^2)&=\frac{\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}{n_P^2}
                  +\frac{\Var\{\Vhat(Q)\}}{n_Q^2}.
 \label{eq:redesign-reference-variance}
\end{align}
Substitution of the component moment targets into the combination formula
at these expected variance contributions gives exactly
Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-combined-reference}. Thus the combination
identity introduces no further approximation at the level of moments.
Appendix~\ref{sec:combined-moment-identity} gives the cancellation
explicitly. Independent fitting samples estimate these moments by their
sample means and sample variances. The Student \(t\) distribution remains
an approximation to the shared statistic's distribution.

For each finite degrees-of-freedom rule, the nominal two-sided p-value is
\begin{equation}
 p=2\{1-F_\nu(|T|)\},
 \label{eq:redesign-t-pvalue}
\end{equation}
where \(F_\nu\) is the distribution function of the Student \(t\)
distribution and \(\nu\) is the rule's sample-dependent or fitted value.
A valid p-value at most 0.05 produces a rejection.

The moment reference requires known simulation populations and is held
fixed within an evaluation setting and across the alternatives belonging
to the same null. Expanded Welch versus this reference measures the
change in rejection when the sample-dependent degrees of freedom are
replaced by that fixed value. The reference can still reject incorrectly
when the shared difference is biased, its standard error has the wrong
scale, or their joint distribution is poorly described by the Student
\(t\) distribution.

\section{Constructing joint distributions with known MI}

The construction changes association while preserving the category
frequencies. For one population, draw \(X\) with category probabilities
\(a=(a_1,\ldots,a_k)\). With probability \(\lambda\), set \(Y=X\);
otherwise draw \(Y\) independently with the same probabilities \(a\).
Copying makes matching categories more likely than under independence.
Both branches give \(Y\) the same marginal probabilities, so changing
\(\lambda\) does not change the row or column totals.

An observation in a diagonal cell can arrive through either branch,
whereas an off-diagonal observation can arrive only through the
independent branch.
Combining these probabilities gives
\begin{equation}
 p_{ij}(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)a_i a_j
                  +\lambda a_i\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}},
 \qquad 0\leq\lambda<1,
 \label{eq:copying-construction}
\end{equation}
where the indicator is one when the two categories match and zero otherwise.
This construction is a special case of the mixture model described by
\citet{bocci2010}.
At \(\lambda=0\), the variables are independent and MI is zero.
Increasing \(\lambda\) increases MI, so the target MI determines the required
copying probability. Appendix~\ref{app:construction} gives the numerical
search and shows why the margins remain fixed.

\begin{samepage}
\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Constructing a \(2\times2\) joint distribution with positive MI.}

For \(P\), take \(a=(0.7,0.3)\) and a copying probability of
approximately 0.20347. Cell \((1,1)\) requires \(X=1\), which has
probability 0.7. Given \(X=1\), copying always gives \(Y=1\), whereas
an independent draw gives \(Y=1\) with probability 0.7. Thus
\begin{align*}
 p_{11}&\simeq0.7\{0.20347+(1-0.20347)(0.7)\}=0.53273,\\
 p_{12}&\simeq0.7(1-0.20347)(0.3)=0.16727.
\end{align*}
\end{samepage}
The second equation has no copying term because copying cannot produce
\(X=1,Y=2\). The remaining cells follow the same rule.
For \(Q\), use \(a=(0.8,0.2)\) and a copying probability of approximately
0.26091. These choices give \(I(P)=0.02\) and \(I(Q)=0.03\) nats:
\begin{equation}
 P\simeq\begin{pmatrix}0.53273&0.16727\\0.16727&0.13273\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 Q\simeq\begin{pmatrix}0.68175&0.11825\\0.11825&0.08175\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:design-example-pair}
\end{equation}
Both joint distributions have positive MI, and the true difference used
to assess power has magnitude 0.01 nats.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Table sizes and margin regimes.}

The copying construction is used for square tables of sizes
\(2\times2,4\times4,8\times8\) and \(16\times16\), containing
4, 16, 64 and 256 cells. Each doubling of the number of categories
multiplies the number of cells by four, so at a fixed sample size the
observations are distributed over more possible cells.

Three margin regimes vary the category frequencies: uniform frequencies
in both populations, the same uneven frequencies in both, or different
uneven frequencies between them. For uneven margins, the first category
has probability \(\rho\), and the remaining mass is shared equally:
\begin{equation}
 m_k(\rho)=\left(\rho,\frac{1-\rho}{k-1},\ldots,
                   \frac{1-\rho}{k-1}\right).
 \label{eq:skew-margin}
\end{equation}
Each population uses the same vector for its row and column margins.
Table~\ref{tab:margin-profiles} specifies the vectors for \(P\) and \(Q\).
The copying probabilities for \(P\) and \(Q\) are chosen separately to
achieve the targets \(I(P)\) and \(I(Q)\).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering

\label{tab:margin-profiles}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Regime & \(P\): rows and columns & \(Q\): rows and columns\\
\midrule
Uniform & \((1/k,\ldots,1/k)\) & \((1/k,\ldots,1/k)\)\\

Under equal MI, the uniform and same-skew regimes give identical joint
probability tables, \(P=Q\). In contrast, the different-skew regime gives
different tables with equal MI because the category frequencies differ
between populations. Comparing the regimes therefore tests false-positive
behaviour under both identical and distinct population tables.

Same skew provides a matched baseline for different skew in all three
studies and both supporting checks. With equal MI and equal sample sizes,
the same-skew samples have identical distributions, so their expected MI
estimation biases cancel in the difference. Different-skew populations can
retain unequal biases despite having equal MI. Under an MI difference or
unequal sample sizes, matching margins alone does not ensure that biases
cancel. The two regimes therefore distinguish failures that also occur
with matching category frequencies from those observed when the frequencies
differ.

Increasing table size under the uneven-margin rule spreads the remaining
mass over more categories, changing both the number of cells and their
probabilities. This comparison shows what happens under a common margin
rule as the table grows. Uniform versus same skew instead compares even
and uneven category frequencies at a fixed size; different skew allows the
two populations' frequencies to differ as well.


\section{Settings and controlled comparisons}

The equal-MI comparisons include identical joint tables under uniform
and same-skew margins, and distinct joint tables under different-skew
margins. This distinction is necessary because the null concerns equal
MI rather than equal cell probabilities. The experiment uses six
comparisons, with overlapping settings sampled only once:

The broad equal-MI study crosses four table sizes, three margin regimes
and ten sample sizes at common MI 0.02 nats. Its 120 settings assess
false-positive rates and approximation accuracy across population
structure and sampling density. The MI sweep uses \(4\times4\) tables,
all three margin regimes, seven positive MI targets and sample sizes
100, 1,000 and 10,000. After removing shared settings, these two studies
contain 174 distinct settings. Across all six comparisons there are 366
distinct evaluation settings and 186 distinct equal-MI fitting settings.
Appendix~\ref{app:construction} lists the complete grids.

The MI sweep compares population and plug-in predictions for the
sampling variance of \(\Vhat\), and checks the near-independence formulas
in Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}. Those formulas predict means
for each population separately. Observed means are therefore compared
with predicted means; medians describe the additional effect of skewed
sampling distributions. Increasing \(n\) while retaining the same
population distinguishes sampling effects from changes in population MI.
A large noncentrality parameter alone is not taken as a failure condition.

The scaled mean error \(n\{\E\Vhat-V\}\) is also compared with
\(d=(k-1)^2\). The near-independence prediction suggests a contribution
close to \(d/n\), whereas away from independence the second-order
coefficient can depend on the population table. The experiment measures
that dependence without assuming a universal correction.

Settings A and B were selected from earlier simulations because Expanded
Welch brought their false-positive rates closer to 5\%. Independent
simulations estimate their performance separately from those earlier
samples. Setting C provides a larger-sample comparison. The broad study
supplies the wider evidence needed to interpret these selected cases.
Four matched failure settings retain \(16\times16\) populations at
\(n=1{,}000\) and 10,000 under both skew regimes.

For each focus null, estimate
\(\sigma_0=\operatorname{SD}_0(\Deltahat)\) from an independent fitting
batch, centring at its sampling mean. Construct alternatives with
\begin{equation}
 I(P)=0.02,\qquad I(Q)=0.02+h\sigma_0,\qquad
 h\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5,3,3.5,4\}.
 \label{eq:redesign-power-grid}
\end{equation}
The signed difference is \(\Delta=-h\sigma_0\). Report each difference
in nats as well as null standard deviations, and hold the fitted
population tables fixed during evaluation.

The broader power study retains the existing differences from 0 to 0.2
nats at \(n=1{,}000\) and 10,000. It includes matched same-skew and
different-skew comparisons at every size, with uniform \(4\times4\)
populations as an additional comparison. The four-cell check matches
MI, margins and sample sizes to the copying construction. The allocation
check holds the total sample size at 2,000 and compares
\((1000,1000)\), \((400,1600)\) and \((1600,400)\) for null and
0.01-nat alternatives under both skew regimes. It assesses the whole
procedure under imbalance, including bias and estimated variance weights.

A separate, earlier bias-correction investigation is retained in
Appendix~\ref{sec:jackknife-difference-check}. It compares an analytic
correction with a delete-one jackknife on 20,000 pairs per setting and
does not add an arm to the four-rule experiment.

\section{Independent fitting, evaluation and calibration}

Every distinct evaluation setting uses 100,000 independently sampled
pairs of count tables. Population samples within a pair are independent;
repetitions are also independent. A separate batch of 100,000 pairs is drawn at each of the 186
null settings to fit the component moments and fixed reference. All four
rules receive the same evaluation pairs. Repeated settings share one
evaluation batch across displays. Separate random streams supply the two populations, each setting, and
the fitting and evaluation batches.

For each alternative, the reference is fitted under its corresponding
null with the same table size, margins, construction and sample allocation.
The baseline MI in both populations is 0.02 nats. Thus changes in power
are assessed against a fixed null reference rather than one refitted at
each alternative.

For the focused power comparison, the fitting batch also supplies
empirical null calibration thresholds. Each sample-dependent rule rejects
when its p-value lies strictly below its fitted fifth-percentile
threshold. Ties at the boundary are excluded, giving a fitting rejection
rate no greater than 5\%. Normal Wald and the fixed reference use the
same empirical cutoff for \(|T|\), since both order the samples by that
quantity. Their calibrated decisions consequently coincide. These
thresholds and the reference degrees of freedom remain fixed throughout
each alternative curve. The independent null evaluation batch measures
the achieved false-positive rate.

Nominal power uses each rule's stated 5\% cutoff. Calibrated power
compares the rules after their null rejection rates have been matched
empirically. The latter measures discrimination after adjustment for
null calibration; the fitted thresholds use known populations and are
not proposed as an additional data-analysis method.

\section{Performance measures and simulation uncertainty}

Primary rejection rates divide valid rejections by every attempted pair.
Invalid outputs are recorded separately and produce no rejection. Method-valid
and common-valid rates retain their stated denominators. Moment calculations
include finite zero variance estimates and finite estimates from invalid
tests. Component degrees-of-freedom summaries use positive finite values
and report their included counts. If exclusions occur, those conditional
summaries do not estimate an unconditional mean.

For each population, report \(\E\Vhat/V\), predicted over observed
sampling variance, first-order over moment degrees of freedom, and the
mean, median and fifth--95th percentile range of estimated component
degrees of freedom. These percentile ranges describe sampling spread.
They are distinct from confidence intervals for a summary.

For the shared difference, report bias divided by observed SD and mean
squared standard error divided by observed sampling variance. Correlations,
skewness and the two rejection tails provide additional diagnostics at
the focus and failure cases. Under alternatives, classify rejections by
whether \(\Deltahat\Delta\) is positive or negative, retaining both in
the two-sided rejection rate.

Wilson 95\% intervals describe uncertainty in rejection rates
\citep{wilson1927}. Paired differences subtract the comparator's rejection
indicator from Expanded Welch's indicator on each sampled pair. Their
Monte Carlo standard errors use the observed variance of these paired
differences. At a true rate of 5\%, 100,000 repetitions give an approximate
Monte Carlo standard error of 0.069 percentage points. Nominal reference
intervals condition on the fitted degrees of freedom; its fitting
uncertainty is reported separately.

Moment-ratio uncertainty uses the delta method applied to the joint
sample moments, as derived in Appendix~\ref{sec:moment-uncertainty}. Focused calibrated-power intervals use 1,000 bootstrap
repetitions of the fitting and evaluation outputs, preserving paired
method outcomes and the fitting batch shared across each curve. The
population tables and differences in nats remain fixed. A power difference
within one percentage point is the stated practical tolerance, assessed
with pointwise uncertainty intervals rather than a nonsignificant test
alone. Intervals quantify simulation uncertainty for the specified
populations; they do not provide simultaneous coverage over the design.

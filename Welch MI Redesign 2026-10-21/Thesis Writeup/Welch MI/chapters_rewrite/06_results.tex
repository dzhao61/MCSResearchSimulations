\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

This chapter first asks whether the mean and sampling-variance predictions
used by Expanded Welch agree with repeated sampling. It then examines
false positives and power. Each evaluation setting uses 100,000 sampled
pairs. The nominal rates divide valid rejections by all attempts, and
the plots show validity separately. Brackets around rates and shading
in rejection plots give pointwise 95\% intervals. Intervals for the fixed
moment reference condition on its independently fitted degrees of freedom.

\section{Accuracy of the variance approximations}

\subsection{Mean and sampling variance}

The mean approximation and sampling-variance approximation have different
errors. Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments} separates them for different-skew
populations at common MI 0.02 nats. A ratio of one indicates agreement.
Solid curves use the population quantities. Dashed curves use the mean
plug-in sampling-variance prediction in the middle row and the mean
estimated component degrees of freedom in the bottom row. The latter
are shown only when all component estimates are positive and finite. Shading gives
approximate 95\% Monte Carlo intervals for the population-prediction
ratios. The component mean remains inflated after the prediction of its
sampling variance has become reasonably accurate. At \(n=1{,}000\), the population
sampling-variance prediction divided by the observed value ranges from
0.710 to 0.996 across all sizes and margins, while the mean estimate
divided by \(V\) ranges from 1.023 to 4.571. The latter discrepancy is
particularly large for the larger tables.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=.78\textheight,keepaspectratio]{focused_redesign_moments_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Component approximation ratios under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:redesign-moments}
\end{figure}

At \(n=50{,}000\), the sampling-variance ratios across the same broad grid
lie between 0.974 and 1.009. Mean ratios range from 1.000 to 1.091, so
small residual mean errors can still affect the degrees of freedom.
Increasing sample size therefore improves both approximations, while
residual mean error can still lower the degrees of freedom. Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}
provides the uniform and same-skew versions of the figure.

The mean error also explains the difference between the two
degrees-of-freedom calculations. At \(4\times4\), \(n=1{,}000\) and MI 0.02, the
sampling-variance ratios are 0.970 for \(P\) and 0.967 for \(Q\).
Their mean ratios are 1.190 and 1.174. Consequently, the population
first-order degrees of freedom, 15.30 and 13.61, fall below the moment
targets, 21.03 and 18.14. The moment-matching formula squares the mean, so underestimating that
mean lowers the population first-order degrees of freedom even when the
variance prediction is close. Appendix~\ref{sec:moment-reference-details}
gives the algebra; Table~\ref{tab:variance-stability} gives the component
values.

In the table, mean ratio is \(\E\Vhat/V\); the next two columns divide
the population and mean plug-in sampling-variance predictions by the
observed sampling variance of \(\Vhat\). Mean df averages the positive
finite component estimates; all 100,000 estimates are included in each
shown row. Moment df uses the actual sampling moments, while FO df uses
the population first-order formula.

The estimated component degrees of freedom can fall further below the
sampling-moment target than the population first-order values suggest.
At A, the mean for the \(P\) component is about 6.52 against a moment
target of 14.81, or
44\% of the target. In the \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) same- and
different-skew cases, the corresponding ratios range from 55\% to 60\%.
All 100,000 component estimates are included in these comparisons.
At C, the ratios are about 99\%. The bottom-row dashed curves expose
this sample-estimation error alongside the population approximation
error; the moment target itself does not guarantee an exact Student
\(t\) distribution for the test statistic.

\subsection{Behaviour near independence}

Near independence, quadratic sampling terms contribute substantially to
\(\Vhat\). At MI 0.0001 and \(n=1{,}000\), the local calculation in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} predicts \(n\E\Vhat=9.2\)
for each population. The observed value for \(P\) is 9.158. Its mean
estimated component degrees of freedom are 4.86, close to the local
prediction 4.6, while the sampling-moment target is about nine. These
comparisons explain why the estimated degrees of freedom can remain
below the moment target even when the local calculation describes their
mean accurately.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=.78\textheight,keepaspectratio]{focused_redesign_a4_different_skew_1000.pdf}
\caption{Observed component moments and Appendix A.4 predictions across MI.}
\label{fig:redesign-a4}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4} shows how that agreement changes across MI.
The shaded band gives the fifth--95th percentile range of estimated
component degrees of freedom, with their mean and median shown separately.
The first-order prediction for the sampling variance is only about
5\% of the observed value at the smallest MI target. Meanwhile, the
mean plug-in prediction is 1.97 times the observed value for \(P\) and
2.14 times for \(Q\). The local calculation explains the scale of these
errors: the quadratic sampling contribution remains substantial even
though the population first-order degrees of freedom are below 0.1.
At MI 0.02, the population sampling-variance approximation is much more
accurate, while the mean error still lowers the first-order degrees of
freedom relative to the moment target. The mean plug-in variance
predictions remain about 7--8\% above the observed values. Thus the
accuracy of the population variance-sensitivity formula does not eliminate
the error from estimating that sensitivity in each sample.

The mean error also varies with the population table. The complete
comparison of its size across MI and margins appears in
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-mean-bias} in the appendix.

\section{False positives when MI is equal}

Figures~\ref{fig:redesign-null-uniform} and
\ref{fig:redesign-null-different} compare Normal Wald, the sample-size rule and Expanded Welch at common
MI 0.02 nats, with the fixed moment reference as a diagnostic. Expanded Welch reduces false positives in some small-sample
settings and lowers already-conservative rates in others. The sample-size
comparator often remains close to Normal Wald once sample sizes are
moderate. The moment reference also varies in its calibration, showing
that accurate denominator moments alone do not provide a correctly
calibrated Student \(t\) reference for every population.

The lower panels show the fraction of valid outputs for Normal Wald
and Expanded Welch. The sample-size and fixed-reference rules share
Wald's validity in these settings. Validity is lowest in the
smallest samples: across the 120 settings, the minimum valid fractions
are 0.958 for Normal Wald, the sample-size comparator and the fixed
reference, and 0.631 for Expanded Welch. Their conditional rates and
included counts are reported separately in the supplement.

Expanded Welch's small-sample improvements do not extend to uniformly
better calibration across the grid. In different-skew large tables,
all rules can reject too often; with identical populations, lowering
an already-conservative rate moves it further from 5\%.

\subsection{Small-sample improvements and sparse-table failures}

Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-rates} compares the selected small-sample
settings with the larger-sample and sparse-table cases. All four rules
are valid on every sampled pair in these seven settings. In the uniform
\(4\times4\) setting A, Expanded Welch gives 4.90\% rejection, compared
with 8.35\% for Wald and 7.39\% for the sample-size rule. In the
\(8\times8\) different-skew setting B, Expanded Welch gives 5.52\%,
compared with 7.47\% for Wald. Its rate remains above 5\%, although
closer than either main comparator.

The estimation errors differ between A and B. At A, difference bias is
negligible, but the mean estimated difference variance is only 0.827 of
its observed value, so Wald's standard error understates sampling
variation. At B, the difference bias is positive and the variance is
overestimated. Expanded Welch lowers false positives in both cases,
although the shared estimates have different errors. At the larger-sample
setting C, bias is negligible and the variance ratio is 1.010.
Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-diagnostics} gives the numerical diagnostics
and degrees-of-freedom comparisons in the appendix.

The \(16\times16\), different-skew case at \(n=1{,}000\) remains a
clear failure for all four rules. The difference bias is about 0.030 nats,
or 2.06 observed SDs. Rejection ranges from 24.8\% to 26.0\%.
The reference rate is 25.3\% despite its independently fitted moments.
Under the equal-MI null, the estimated difference is centred at
\begin{equation}
 \E(\Deltahat)\approx 0+0.02985=0.02985\text{ nats}.
 \label{eq:redesign-null-bias-shift}
\end{equation}
This shift is large relative to the observed SD of approximately 0.01450
nats. It moves most rejections into the positive tail even though the
population difference is zero.
With same skew, difference bias is negligible and the variance ratio is
1.93; all four rates instead lie between 0.53\% and 0.60\%.
The matched populations separate the large difference bias from
conservative behaviour that occurs when the biases cancel. At
\(n=10{,}000\), both skew regimes give much smaller estimation errors
and rates closer to 5\%.

\section{Power at the selected settings}

\subsection{Nominal power}

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-power} compares the three settings in
Table~\ref{tab:redesign-focus-settings}, with MI differences measured
in null standard deviations. Expanded Welch has lower nominal
power than the sample-size comparator at A and B. At A, two null SDs
correspond to about 0.310 nats: Expanded Welch rejects 35.9\% of pairs,
compared with 38.9\% for the sample-size rule and 41.0\% for Wald.
These comparisons include the different null rejection rates shown
at zero. Appendix~\ref{sec:calibration-sensitivity-results} gives the
numerical rates and paired contrasts.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_power.pdf}
\caption{Nominal power across differences measured in fitted null standard deviations.}
\label{fig:redesign-power}
\end{figure}

B also shows a falling-then-rising rejection curve. Expanded Welch's
rate falls from 5.52\% under the null to 1.99\% at one null SD, before
rising to 33.1\% at four SDs. The true signed difference becomes
negative as \(I(Q)\) increases, initially opposing the positive bias
observed under the null. The larger rejection cutoff does not remove
this distortion of the shared statistic. Direction counts and the
broader large-table curves provide supporting evidence in the appendix.

At C, nominal power differences are small. At two null SDs, or
about 0.00635 nats, Wald gives 45.2\% and Expanded Welch gives 44.9\%.
The paired loss is 0.30 percentage points, with 95\% interval
0.27--0.34. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-power-contrasts} reports paired
comparisons at two null SDs for all three focus settings. These intervals
use the same sampled pairs, so they measure the difference directly.
The calibrated comparison assesses how that difference changes after
matching null rejection.

\subsection{Power after empirical null calibration}

Calibrating at common MI 0.02 gives approximately 5\% rejection on
independent samples from that null population. The achieved rates range
from 4.93\% to 4.99\% across A--C and the four rules. Wald and the
fixed moment reference have identical calibrated decisions because
both order samples by \(|T|\). Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-calibrated-power}
uses these lower-null cutoffs throughout each alternative curve.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_power_calibrated.pdf}
\caption{Power after calibration at common MI 0.02; bands show bootstrap uncertainty.}
\label{fig:redesign-calibrated-power}
\end{figure}

Expanded Welch gains power under this calibration, but the gain depends
on the null population used to fit the cutoff. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-calibration-sensitivity}
holds each alternative fixed and compares lower- and upper-null
calibration. At A and two null SDs, the gain over Wald changes from
3.58 percentage points to a loss of 0.76 points. At B and four SDs,
a 3.92-point gain becomes a 0.39-point loss. A at four SDs retains a
small positive estimate whose interval includes zero, while B at two
SDs retains a much smaller gain. At C, both calibrations give differences
well within the one-percentage-point tolerance.

The table gives gains in percentage points with paired 95\% bootstrap
intervals including cutoff-fitting uncertainty. The lower null has
common MI 0.02; the upper null has the MI in the second column.
At A and two SDs, applying the lower-null calibration to upper-null
samples gives 11.2\% rejection for Wald and 13.7\% for Expanded Welch.
Using the upper-null cutoffs instead gives about 4.8--4.9\% there,
but only 1.63\% for Wald and 0.79\% for Expanded Welch at the lower
null. Thus the original gain compares rules matched at the lower null,
with different rejection rates at another equal-MI population.
The two checks do not establish control over every population satisfying
equal MI. Appendix~\ref{sec:calibration-sensitivity-results} reports
all cross-null rates and the full calibrated-power tables.

B's initial fall in rejection remains after calibration at common MI
0.02. Increasing \(I(Q)\) initially moves the negative true
difference against its positive estimation bias, reducing \(|T|\) and
rejection before the curve rises. This behaviour occurs for all the
compared rules: matching one null rate changes the cutoff but leaves
the biased estimated difference. The calibration sensitivity and the
falling-then-rising curve therefore limit the interpretation of its
apparent power gain.

\section{Association pattern and sample allocation}

Concentrating dependence in four cells reproduces the main sparse-table
failure. At \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) and different skew, the
null difference bias is 0.03155 nats, compared with 0.02985 under copying.
All four rules reject far too often. With same-skew populations, expected
difference bias cancels and rejection remains conservative.
Table~\ref{tab:redesign-construction} gives the matched comparisons.

Sample allocation affects both the standard error and the cutoff. In
the different-skew \(4\times4\) comparison, assigning only 400 of the
2,000 observations to \(Q\) increases the observed SD from 0.01173 to
0.01611 nats. At the 0.01-nat alternative, Expanded Welch's power falls
from 10.26\% under equal allocation to 3.42\%. Its median degrees of
freedom also fall, increasing the rejection cutoff. Reversing which
population has fewer observations changes the result because the two
populations have different variances and residual biases.
Appendix~\ref{sec:allocation-check} reports both allocation directions.

The earlier check in Appendix~\ref{sec:jackknife-difference-check}
replaces Wald's analytic MI bias correction with a delete-one jackknife,
retaining the existing standard error and standard normal distribution
cutoff. Its 20,000-pair samples provide a separate investigation of
bias correction, rather than an additional rejection
rule. It improves the estimated ordering in the sparse different-skew
alternative while leaving conservative null rejection.

\section{Application to online shopping data}
\label{sec:shoppers-application}

The Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention dataset provides a practical
comparison of month--purchase MI between new and returning visitors
\citep{sakar2018}.
A larger MI means that the month provides more information about whether
a session ends in a purchase. A second comparison uses the weekend
indicator instead of month to examine whether its MI with purchasing
differs between the same visitor groups.

The dataset contains 12,330 sessions, with each session belonging to a
different user according to the source documentation \citep{sakar2018}.
The analysis retains all 1,694 sessions labelled \texttt{New\_Visitor}
and 10,551 labelled \texttt{Returning\_Visitor}, excluding the 85
\texttt{Other} sessions. The outcome is the original Boolean
\texttt{Revenue} variable: true indicates a purchase and false indicates
no purchase. The tests treat the two disjoint visitor groups as independent
samples and the sessions within each group as independent observations.

Each group forms a \(10\times2\) month--purchase count table. The rows
are the ten months recorded in the dataset: February, March, May, June,
July, August, September, October, November and December. The columns
are no purchase and purchase, and each session adds one to its corresponding
cell. Both groups use these same categories, with no smoothing or removal
of zero cells. All ten months occur in both groups, although the new-visitor
February row contains only one session and no purchase. Replacing month
with the Boolean \texttt{Weekend} variable gives a \(2\times2\) table
for each group, with weekday and weekend rows; all four cells are observed.

Both methods use the bias-corrected MI estimates in
Table~\ref{tab:shoppers-application}. For the month tables, the leading
bias correction subtracts \((10-1)(2-1)/(2n)=9/(2n)\) from each plug-in
MI estimate \citep{miller1955,moddemeijer1989};
for the weekend tables, it subtracts \(1/(2n)\). Here \(n\) is the
number of sessions in the relevant visitor group. The estimated
difference is new-visitor MI minus returning-visitor MI for the same
predictor--purchase pair. Normal Wald
compares this difference divided by its standard error with the standard
normal distribution; Expanded Welch uses the same statistic with a
Student \(t\) distribution and estimated degrees of freedom. Both tests
are two-sided at the 5\% level.

Month has higher estimated MI with purchasing among returning visitors.
The corrected difference is \(-0.013563\) nats, with a standard error of
\(0.003333\) nats, giving
\begin{equation}
  T\approx\frac{-0.013563}{0.003333}\approx-4.069.
  \label{eq:shoppers-month-statistic}
\end{equation}
With 26.79 estimated degrees of freedom, the Student \(t\) distribution gives
a larger p-value than the standard normal distribution, but both tests
reject equal month--purchase MI for new and returning visitors. This
application was selected from an initial exploration
of six comparisons across three public datasets. Accounting for all six
comparisons with Holm's multiple-testing adjustment \citep{holm1979}
gives month p-values of 0.000189 for Normal Wald and 0.00149 for Expanded
Welch, so the conclusion remains unchanged.
Table~\ref{tab:application-holm} lists all six comparisons and their raw
and adjusted p-values.

The weekend comparison gives a different result: the corrected
weekend--purchase MI estimates for new and returning visitors are both
approximately 0.0007 nats. Their difference,
\(-0.000063\) nats, is small relative to the standard error of
\(0.001124\) nats, giving \(T\approx-0.056\). Although Expanded Welch
estimates only 2.18 degrees of freedom, both p-values remain close to one.
Neither test provides evidence that weekend--purchase MI differs between
the visitor groups, but this does not establish that their population MI
values are equal. The small statistic explains the agreement between the methods here.
Small estimated degrees of freedom are consistent with the weak
associations examined near independence in the simulations, even with
large samples. The population tables are unknown here, so their
sampling-moment target cannot be checked directly.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis developed a deterministic finite-sample reference for comparing
mutual information between two independent categorical populations. The
method begins with the same bias-corrected MI difference and first-order
standard error as normal Wald inference. It then estimates how much each
population's MI variance estimate would change across repeated samples,
converts that uncertainty into component degrees of freedom by Satterthwaite
moment matching, and combines the components in the usual Welch form.

The resulting expanded Welch--Satterthwaite test is mathematically grounded
in the influence function of the complete MI variance functional. It accounts
for changes in both the joint probabilities and the row and column margins.
Its computational complexity is $O(rc)$, matching the order of the normal
Wald and simple Welch alternatives.

Across 60 fixed equal-MI population pairs and 600,000 null replicates,
expanded Welch had the smallest overall calibration error and the coverage
closest to 95\%. At $\alpha=0.05$, mean absolute false-positive-rate error
fell from 0.01342 for normal Wald and 0.01194 for simple Welch to 0.00825. The
largest improvements occurred under skewness, rare expected cells, and
unequal sample sizes. In well-sampled tables, expanded Welch and normal Wald
were similarly accurate. The correction cost approximately one percentage
point of power in the tested alternatives and increased median runtime from
about 0.08 to 0.15 milliseconds per table pair.

The result is useful but bounded. Expanded Welch improves the calibration of
regular positive-MI comparisons; it is not an exact finite-sample test and
does not solve the nonregular independence boundary or arbitrary support
loss. The most extreme sparse and imbalanced configuration remained liberal.

The principal conclusion is therefore that MI-specific
Welch--Satterthwaite calibration is a viable analytic refinement for the
two-sample equal-MI problem. It offers a practical middle ground between an
uncorrected normal reference and computational resampling. Independent
confirmatory validation and higher-order centring are the next steps required
before broad applied adoption.

\chapter{Additional Results}
\label{app:additional-results}

This appendix reports the component diagnostics, calibration sensitivity,
rejection directions and multiple-testing adjustment used in the main
argument. Each simulation setting uses 100,000 evaluation repetitions. The
experimental supplement retains the full companion plots and supporting
comparisons.

\section{Component diagnostics and fitted null SDs}
\label{sec:component-diagnostics}

Table~\ref{tab:redesign-scaled-mean-error} shows how the mean error of
\(\Vhat\) varies across MI values, margin regimes and populations in
the \(4\times4\) sweep. The scaled error is \(n\{\E\Vhat-V\}\).
The near-independence calculation suggests a mean error of
\((k-1)^2/n=9/n\) for \(\Vhat\) in these tables. The observed scaled
errors vary, so subtracting \(9/n\) from \(\Vhat\) would leave
residual mean error.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\caption{Scaled mean error across the \(4\times4\) MI sweep.}
\label{tab:redesign-scaled-mean-error}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_redesign_scaled_mean_error}
\end{table}

At \(n=100\), the whole scaled-error range is below nine, so a
\(9/n\) subtraction would overcorrect the mean throughout the checked
grid. At the two larger sample sizes the ranges straddle nine: the same
correction would be too large for some populations and too small for
others. The wider scaled ranges do not imply larger absolute errors,
since the entries must be divided by \(n\) to recover the mean error
of \(\Vhat\). They show that its coefficient depends on MI and margins.

Table~\ref{tab:redesign-component-spread} gives the means, medians and
fifth--95th percentile ranges of component degrees of freedom. The
moment target uses the evaluation sampling moments. All 100,000
component estimates are positive and finite in each row; percentile
ranges describe sampling spread.

\begin{table}[H]

\caption{Sampling spread of component degrees of freedom.}
\label{tab:redesign-component-spread}

In Table~\ref{tab:redesign-component-spread}, the means at A and B exceed the medians and the upper percentile extends
farther from the median than the lower percentile. Large estimates in
the upper tail therefore affect the mean, which alone does not describe
a typical repetition. Both summaries remain below the moment targets.
At C, the mean and median nearly coincide and the
percentile range is much narrower relative to their size. The much
larger component degrees of freedom are also more stable in relative
terms, although their absolute percentile ranges are wider.

The \(P\) and \(Q\) rows of Table~\ref{tab:redesign-component-spread}
nearly match at A and the same-skew settings, as expected
for identical population tables. Under different skew, \(Q\) has lower
estimated component degrees of freedom and a lower moment target.
Increasing the \(16\times16\) sample size raises the estimates, narrows
their relative spread and brings their means closer to the targets in
both populations. The remaining gaps at the smaller sample size occur
with complete inclusion, so they are not produced by dropping invalid
component estimates.

Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-diagnostics} describes the shared difference
and standard error. Bias/SD divides difference bias by its observed SD;
variance ratio divides the mean squared standard error by the observed
difference variance. Rank corr. is Spearman's correlation between
\(|\Deltahat-\operatorname{mean}(\Deltahat)|\) and \(\SEhat\), using every repetition
where both quantities are finite without a test-validity restriction.
Skewness measures asymmetry in \(\Deltahat\): positive values indicate a
longer right tail and negative values a longer left tail. ``Median combined
df'' summarises \(\widehat\nu_{\mathrm{expanded}}\), rather than either
component's degrees of freedom. All 100,000 repetitions are included
in each row.

A near-zero signed correlation can follow from symmetry despite
magnitude dependence: the same-skew \(2\times2\), \(n=100\) null has
Pearson correlation between \(\Deltahat\) and \(\SEhat\) about
\(-0.001\), but rank correlation between the centred magnitude and
\(\SEhat\) of 0.547.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\caption{Shared-estimator diagnostics and combined degrees of freedom.}
\label{tab:redesign-case-diagnostics}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_redesign_cases_diagnostics}
\end{table}

The small skewness values in Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-diagnostics}
concern symmetry about the sampling
mean, whereas Bias/SD concerns displacement from the true difference.
The different-skew sparse case can therefore be approximately symmetric
while centred more than two observed SDs above zero. Its median combined
degrees of freedom are already large enough for the two rejection
cutoffs to be fairly close, yet both tests fail because the shared
statistic remains displaced.

Across the sample-size rows of Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-diagnostics},
increasing \(n\) from 1,000 to 10,000 gives both sparse regimes variance
ratios much closer to one, weaker magnitude dependence and higher combined
degrees of freedom. The different-skew bias also falls sharply and changes
sign. These changes accompany the recovery of the false-positive rates
in Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-rates}. The modest rank correlations in
these selected rows differ from the much stronger \(2\times2\)
dependence noted above; one average variance ratio cannot describe all
these sampling distributions.

Additional checks examine the peaks in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}.
For \(16\times16\) different-skew populations at common MI 0.02, the
cell contributions to \(\Vhat\) and \(\widehat\tau^2\) are partitioned
into cells observed once and cells observed more than once. The check uses
10,000 evaluation repetitions at each
\(n\in\{10,20,50,100,250,1000,10000\}\), for both populations.
Once-observed cells account for most of the mean estimates around the
peak, but much less at the largest checked sample size. Comparing the
linear change derived in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} with
\(\Vhat(P)-V(P)\) also shows substantial remaining variation around
the peak. Its covariance with the linear change is retained when
checking the total sampling variance.

For the MI sweep in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4} (\(4\times4\),
different skew, \(n=1{,}000\)),
the local approximations \(\Vhat\approx2\Ihat\) and
\(\widehat\tau^2\approx4\Vhat\) are checked against the sampled
values. Twice the plug-in MI increasingly understates the mean and
sampling variance of \(\Vhat\), while \(4\Vhat\) increasingly
understates the mean of \(\widehat\tau^2\). The observed variance
of \(2n\Ihat\) also exceeds its local noncentral
chi-squared prediction. Both parts of the local calculation therefore
contribute to the separation in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4}.

The uniform \(2\times2\) panel in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-null-uniform}
shows a further source of
conservatism: the centred magnitude of \(\Deltahat\) is positively
associated with \(\SEhat\), especially around the rejection-rate
trough. The magnitude correlation falls from 0.656 at \(n=50\) to
0.167 at \(n=1{,}000\). This check uses the same correlation defined
above; it concerns the joint behaviour of the estimates, which their
average variance ratio alone does not describe.

under calibration at common MI 0.02. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-power-contrasts}
gives nominal and calibrated paired contrasts at two null SDs, including
cutoff-fitting uncertainty for the calibrated intervals.

\begin{table}[H]

\label{tab:redesign-power-contrasts}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_redesign_power_contrasts}

In Table~\ref{tab:redesign-power-contrasts}, the nominal power loss is
largest at A and much smaller at B and C.
After calibration, the ordering reverses at A and B, while C's gain
remains small. Its interval excludes zero but stays well within the
one-percentage-point tolerance: a precisely estimated difference can
still be practically small. The following tables examine whether the
larger A/B gains persist under a different fitting null.

Along the alternative paths in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-power}, the
sampling SD also changes. At four
fitted null SDs, the observed SD of \(\Deltahat\) is approximately
\(1.41\sigma_0\) at A, \(1.70\sigma_0\) at B and
\(1.16\sigma_0\) at C. Difference bias remains positive at A and B
but negligible at C. These checks support the cross-panel interpretation
of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-power}: a common null-SD separation does
not give the same distance from zero relative to the alternative
sampling variation.

Table~\ref{tab:redesign-sensitivity-power-full} compares both calibrations
on the same fixed alternatives. The lower null has common MI 0.02;
the upper null sets both MI values to the alternative's larger value.
Appendix~\ref{sec:calibration-sensitivity-design} specifies the construction
and joint bootstrap. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-calibration-sensitivity}
in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} gives Expanded Welch minus Normal Wald
power differences with intervals.

\begin{table}[!htbp]

Across the two- and four-null-SD rows of Table~\ref{tab:redesign-sensitivity-power-full},
power increases for both tests under
each calibration, but changing the fitting null has different effects
across settings. At A, upper-null fitting substantially lowers both
power estimates at either separation. At B, the changes are smaller
and method-specific: at two SDs, Wald's power rises slightly while
Expanded Welch's falls. At C, both power levels and method differences
are nearly unchanged by the fitting null. Because each alternative
sample is held fixed between calibrations, these changes come from
the fitted thresholds.

In Table~\ref{tab:redesign-sensitivity-cross-null}, lower-null calibration
at A and \(h=2\) gives rates close to 5\% at
the lower evaluation null, but more than twice that target at the upper
null. Upper-null fitting reverses the pattern: rejection is close to 5\%
there and conservative at the lower null. The same pattern is stronger
at \(h=4\), where the two common MI values are farther apart.

At B, transfer errors are smaller than at A but differ between methods.
With lower-null fitting, Expanded Welch has higher rejection than Wald
at the upper null and exceeds 5\%; with upper-null fitting, it becomes more
conservative at the lower null. These differences grow at the larger
separation. At C, rates remain near 5\% across both fitting choices,
evaluation nulls and separations, consistent with the stable power
comparison. The sensitivity of power at A and B therefore accompanies
changes in their relative false-positive rates when calibration is
transferred. Neither calibration establishes control across the
complete equal-MI null.

\section{Sparse-table bias and estimated direction}

Table~\ref{tab:redesign-rejection-direction} counts wrong-direction
rejections at \(16\times16\), \(n_P=n_Q=1{,}000\) and
\(I(P)-I(Q)=-0.01\) nats. Each entry gives the number of wrong-direction
rejections divided by the total number of rejections. Almost every
rejection under different-skew margins indicates the wrong ordering;
the same-skew populations show far fewer such cases.

\begin{table}[!htbp]
\centering
\caption{Wrong-direction counts among rejections at the negative MI difference.}
\label{tab:redesign-rejection-direction}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_redesign_rejection_direction}
\end{table}

Expanded Welch reduces the number of rejections in both columns, but
under different skew almost every retained rejection still has the
wrong direction. Its larger cutoff removes some rejections without
repairing the sign error in the estimated difference.

At \(n=10{,}000\), neither test has a wrong-direction rejection at this alternative under either skew
regime. Under different skew, the difference bias at \(n=1{,}000\)
is 0.03071 nats. Thus
\begin{equation}
 \E(\Deltahat)\approx-0.01+0.03071=0.02071\text{ nats}.
 \label{eq:redesign-alternative-bias-shift}
\end{equation}
The positive centre explains why almost every rejection indicates the
wrong ordering. Two-sided power retains every rejection; these counts
show why its interpretation also requires the estimated direction.

\section{Holm adjustment for the application}
\label{sec:application-holm}

Table~\ref{tab:application-holm} gives raw and Holm-adjusted p-values for
six tests of equal predictor--outcome MI between groups. Adult compares
female and male records using education (16 categories) or occupation
(14 categories) and income above US\$50,000 \citep{becker1996}. Its
training and test partitions are combined without survey weights;
records with missing occupation are excluded only from that comparison.
Student Performance compares schools GP and MS using Portuguese-language
course records, study time (four levels) or maternal education (five levels),
and a final grade of at least 10 \citep{cortez2008}. Shopping compares new and returning visitors for
month--purchase and weekend--purchase MI.

Apply Holm's adjustment separately to each method's six p-values
\citep{holm1979}. Order the raw p-values and multiply them by 6, 5, 4,
3, 2 and 1. Moving through that order, replace any value below the
preceding adjusted value with the preceding adjusted value, and cap the
results at one.

Both Adult comparisons and the shopping month comparison remain below
0.05 after adjustment for either method. The other three comparisons
remain above 0.05. Expanded Welch gives larger raw p-values
throughout the table, but it selects the same three comparisons as
Normal Wald at this threshold. Holm's adjustment changes the reported
p-values without changing those decisions in this exploratory family.

sampled table pairs and the two-sided 5\% threshold.

The smaller tables again give low false-positive rates at intermediate
sample sizes. At \(n=100\), Wald rejects at 0.0128 under uniform
margins and 0.0114 under same skew, with still lower Expanded Welch
rates. In \(8\times8\) tables, uniform margins instead give rates
0.0737 and 0.0651 at this size, whereas same-skew margins give 0.0189
and 0.0121. The effect of the margin regime therefore changes with
table size. By \(n=50{,}000\), all four comparisons have rates
between 0.0489 and 0.0503 and full validity.

\section{Supporting estimation diagnostics}

Table~\ref{tab:association-diagnostics} gives the diagnostics behind
the association-strength figure. Est./obs. is the ratio of mean squared
standard error to observed sampling variance. Pop./obs. replaces the
estimated variance with the population first-order variance
\(V(P)/n+V(Q)/n\). DF denotes combined degrees of freedom; its bracketed
range gives the 5th and 95th percentiles. All repetitions have finite
diagnostics and valid test outputs in these settings.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Diagnostics for \(4\times4\) different-skew populations with equal MI.}
\label{tab:association-diagnostics}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_association_table}
\end{table}

At the smallest MI targets, population first-order variance accounts
for only a small fraction of the observed spread at either sample size.
Meanwhile, the plug-in variance estimate exceeds that spread. Using
estimated rather than population inputs therefore changes the error
as well as its size. Both comparisons become much closer to one as
association strengthens, especially at \(n=1{,}000\). This supports
reading the low rejection rates together with estimation error, rather
than attributing them solely to the Student \(t\) distribution.

Table~\ref{tab:large-sample-diagnostics} continues the main diagnostic
comparison to \(n=50{,}000\) at common MI 0.02. All outputs are valid.
At \(4\times4\) the difference bias has Monte Carlo standard error
approximately 0.000010 nats; at \(16\times16\) it is approximately
0.000012. The biases are small, the estimated/observed variance ratios
are near one, and the large degrees of freedom bring the two reference
distributions close together. The remaining population/observed
discrepancy at \(16\times16\) is larger than that at \(4\times4\).

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Variability of the estimated MI variance.}

Table~\ref{tab:variance-stability} checks the additional calculation
that supplies Expanded Welch's degrees of freedom. Observed variability
is the sample variance of \(\Vhat(P)\) across repetitions, with the
corresponding calculation for \(Q\). Pop./obs. divides
\(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) by this observed value; plug-in/obs. instead uses
the average \(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\). The predictions are defined
in Equation~\eqref{eq:variance-stability-predictions}. The six selected
settings use different-skew copying populations, and each row includes
all 20,000 finite outputs.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Predicted and observed variability of the estimated MI variance.}
\label{tab:variance-stability}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_stability_table}
\end{table}

At \(4\times4\), common MI 0.02 and \(n=10{,}000\), both
predictions are close to observed variability. At common MI 0.05 and
\(n=1{,}000\), the ratios are also close to one. Near independence
at common MI 0.0001, however, the population first-order prediction
accounts for about 5\% of observed variability, while the plug-in
prediction is approximately twice that variability. Thus the
approximation at the true table and substitution of empirical
probabilities produce different errors.

The larger table gives another intermediate case. At \(16\times16\)
and \(n=1{,}000\), the population predictions account for approximately
72--77\% of observed variability, while the plug-in predictions are
1.70--1.82 times that variability. At \(n=10{,}000\), the population
ratios are close to one, but the plug-in predictions remain about 10\%
too large. These comparisons assess the sampling approximation used to
calculate degrees of freedom. The usefulness of the resulting cutoff
also depends on the shared MI statistic, as the main results demonstrate.

\section{Detection over the full difference range}

Figures~\ref{fig:focused-detection-full} and
\ref{fig:focused-margin-detection-full} show the same settings as the
main detection figures, extending the horizontal range through 0.2 nats.
They retain the zero-difference points and Wilson interval bands.
The population targets are \(I(P)=0.02\) and
\(I(Q)=0.02+|\Delta|\); every result is valid in these settings.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_detection_sizes_full.pdf}
\caption{Full-range detection under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:focused-detection-full}
\end{figure}

At \(n=1{,}000\), detection of a 0.05-nat difference falls with table
size: Wald rates are 0.9642, 0.9079, 0.7699 and 0.0471 from
\(2\times2\) through \(16\times16\). The last curve first falls
from its high null rate, then rises to 0.6297 at 0.1 nats and 0.9997
at 0.2. Unequal bias opposes the small negative true differences there,
as discussed in the main results. At \(n=10{,}000\), both methods
detect every simulated 0.05-nat difference at all four sizes.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_detection_margins_full.pdf}
\caption{Full-range detection across \(4\times4\) margin regimes.}
\label{fig:focused-margin-detection-full}
\end{figure}

The margin regimes differ most at intermediate effects, before rejection
approaches one. At 0.2 nats, both methods reject every simulated sample
at both sample sizes in all three regimes. Near this upper limit,
a smaller difference between methods says little about their relative
performance for weaker effects; the enlarged main figures show those
effects more clearly.

targets. All outputs and diagnostics are finite and valid. The sampled

\label{tab:construction-check}

The same qualitative method comparison persists: at \(n=1{,}000\),
Expanded Welch lowers an already-low null rate and reduces detection;
at 10,000 its effect is smaller. The construction still changes the
numerical detection rate. For the 0.01-nat difference at \(n=10{,}000\),

of the method comparison at these settings, not identical performance
under every association pattern.

\thesisminiheading{Sparse \(16\times16\) comparison.}

Table~\ref{tab:large-construction-check} tests the larger-table failure
under the same two constructions. Both use different-skew margins
\(m_{16}(0.7)\) for \(P\) and \(m_{16}(0.8)\) for \(Q\), with
the MI targets and sample sizes matched. Every setting has 20,000
repetitions and full validity. Rejection brackets give Wilson 95\%
intervals. Bias and its Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE), and observed
SD, describe the corrected MI difference in nats. Est./obs. divides
mean squared standard error by observed sampling variance.
Opposite W/E reports the percentage of Wald and Expanded Welch
rejections whose estimated difference has the opposite sign to the
population difference. It applies only to the nonzero alternative.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Matched \(16\times16\) comparisons under two constructions.}
\label{tab:large-construction-check}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4.5pt}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_large_construction_table}

At \(n=1{,}000\), the four-cell null has difference bias
0.03145 nats and false-positive rates 0.3095 for Wald and 0.2977 for
Expanded Welch. Both rates exceed those under copying, where the
corresponding bias is 0.02960 nats. For the 0.01-nat alternative,
the four-cell bias of 0.03136 moves the mean estimated difference to
approximately \(-0.01+0.03136=0.02136\) nats. This is positive
although \(Q\) has larger population MI. Opposite-sign counts are
2,493 of 2,494 Wald rejections and 2,350 of 2,351 Expanded Welch
rejections; only one rejection for each method has the correct sign.
The sparse-table bias and reversed estimated ordering therefore persist
under both tested constructions.

At \(n=10{,}000\), the four-cell null rates are 0.0499 and 0.0491,
with much smaller difference bias. Every rejection at the alternative
has the correct estimated direction under either construction.
Detection nevertheless differs: Wald gives 0.6992 under copying and
0.6323 under the four-cell transfer. Their biases are similar, but the
observed SD rises from approximately 0.00433 to 0.00468 nats, making
the same population difference harder to detect under the transfer.
Changing association arrangement thus changes precision even when
table size, margins and MI targets match. The agreement concerns the
sparse estimation failure and the small effect of the cutoff adjustment,
rather than identical performance across constructions.

\section{Unequal sample sizes at a fixed total}
\label{sec:allocation-check}

Table~\ref{tab:allocation-check} compares three allocations of 2,000
observations between the same copying \(4\times4\) populations.
The row and column margins are \((0.7,0.1,0.1,0.1)\) in \(P\) and
\((0.8,0.2/3,0.2/3,0.2/3)\) in \(Q\). Both MI values are 0.02 nats
under the null; under the alternative, \(I(Q)=0.03\) and
\(\Delta=-0.01\) nats. Each allocation uses 20,000 independent
table pairs, and the balanced results are the corresponding main-study
comparisons. Different allocations use independent repetitions, whereas
the two methods receive the same samples within each allocation.

Rejection divides valid rejections by all repetitions; brackets give
pointwise Wilson 95\% intervals. Valid W/E gives the valid-result
percentages for Wald and Expanded Welch. Difference bias and observed
SD are in nats. Est./obs. compares mean squared standard error with
observed sampling variance, and DF is the combined degrees of freedom.
All estimates and test results are finite and valid in these comparisons.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\caption{Rejection and estimation diagnostics at fixed total sample size.}
\label{tab:allocation-check}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_allocation_table}
\end{table}

With equal MI, Wald rejects at 0.0394 for balanced samples and at 0.0463
and 0.0454 for the two unequal allocations. Expanded Welch lowers these
rates to 0.0331, 0.0306 and 0.0252, respectively. The imbalance therefore
does not create excessive Wald rejection in these settings, and the
adjustment makes already-low rates lower. The null median degrees of
freedom fall from 35.70 under balance to 14.42 and 10.97, increasing the
cutoff from the Student \(t\) distribution.

For the 0.01-nat difference, giving \(Q\) only 400 observations lowers
detection for both methods. The observed SD rises from 0.01167 nats
under balance to 0.01610; with \(P\) limited to 400, it is 0.01431.
Reversing the allocation also changes the remaining difference bias:
\(+0.00073\) nats when \(P\) has fewer observations and
\(-0.00143\) when \(Q\) has fewer. The null biases have the same
signs, with Monte Carlo standard error about 0.00010 in either case.
Mean squared standard error exceeds observed variance in each comparison.
Allocation therefore changes the shared estimates as well as the cutoff
adjustment.

\chapter{Experimental Details}
\label{app:construction}

This appendix specifies the population construction, target-MI search,
settings underlying the main results, sampling procedure and outcome
calculations. Together with the test formulas in
Chapters~\ref{chap:baselines}--\ref{chap:design},
these details define the statistical experiment.

\section{Copying construction and target selection}

For the copying construction in Equation~\eqref{eq:copying-construction},
summing a row gives
\begin{equation}
 \sum_jp_{ij}(\lambda)
 =(1-\lambda)a_i\sum_ja_j+\lambda a_i=a_i.
\end{equation}
Summing a column similarly gives \(a_j\), so both margins are preserved.
All cells are positive for \(0\leq\lambda<1\).

MI increases continuously from zero to the entropy of \(a\).
With the margins fixed, differentiating the joint terms gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\mathrm d^2 I\{P(\lambda)\}}{\mathrm d\lambda^2}
 =\sum_{i,j}\frac{(a_i\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}-a_i a_j)^2}
                     {p_{ij}(\lambda)}>0.
\end{equation}
The first derivative at zero is zero, because the change in probabilities
sums to zero and all pointwise MI values are zero there. The positive
second derivative makes the first derivative positive for
\(\lambda>0\). At the endpoint, \(Y=X\), so
\begin{equation}
 I\{P(1)\}=-\sum_i a_i\log a_i.
\end{equation}
These facts give a unique copying probability for each positive target
below the entropy endpoint.

For each population, calculate this endpoint and solve
\(I\{P(\lambda)\}-I_{\rm target}=0\) on \([0,1]\) using Brent's
method \citep{brent1973}. Absolute and relative root tolerances are
\(10^{-15}\) and \(10^{-14}\). The endpoint brackets the search;
sampled populations use the interior solution. Accept a table only when
every cell is positive, its total and marginal errors are at most
\(10^{-12}\), and its MI error is at most
\(10^{-12}+10^{-8}I_{\rm target}\). Every target is checked against its own population entropy endpoint.

\section{Parameter grids and sampling}

Table~\ref{tab:design-primary-grids} specifies the parameter grids, with
the null and power MI values given in
Equations~\eqref{eq:design-balanced-null} and
\eqref{eq:design-mi-gap-populations}. Solve separately for each
population's copying probability, since different margins can require
different copying probabilities even under equal MI.

For one repetition, draw the counts from a multinomial distribution:
\begin{equation}
 (N_{P,11},\ldots,N_{P,kk})\sim\operatorname{Multinomial}
      (n_P;p_{11},\ldots,p_{kk}),
\end{equation}
and draw the corresponding \(Q\) counts independently. Apply both tests
to each sampled pair of \(k\times k\) tables, using 100,000 independent
repetitions per distinct setting. The simulations use the PCG64
pseudo-random generator with master seed 20261021 for the approximation
checks and null settings with a shared MI of 0.02 nats, and 20261101 for
the other null settings and the power study. Separate streams distinguish settings and
the two populations, while repeated settings share the same repetitions
across displays.

All samples retain their declared table dimensions, including empty rows
and columns. Use natural logarithms and \(0\log0=0\), without adding
artificial counts to empty cells.
The bias correction for each plug-in MI is \((k-1)^2/(2n)\); do not truncate
negative corrected estimates. Calculate the standard error using
Equation~\eqref{eq:shared-se} and the component and combined degrees of freedom
using Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}.

\section{Calculation requirements and rejection rates}
\label{sec:validity-rules}

A calculation for the Wald test succeeds when it has a finite corrected difference, a
finite positive standard error, \(\Vhat(P)+\Vhat(Q)>10^{-14}\), and a finite p-value.
The threshold treats variances extremely close to zero as numerically
unusable. The Expanded Welch test additionally requires positive finite degrees
of freedom for both components and their combination, and a finite
p-value for that test. If either component's degrees of freedom are
not positive and finite, the calculation for the Expanded Welch test is unsuccessful.

The componentwise algorithm requires positive, finite degrees of freedom
for both populations. If a sampled table has
\(\Vhat=\widehat\tau^2=0\), the component formula in
Equation~\eqref{eq:nu-p-hat} requires \(0/0\). The calculation for the Expanded
Welch test is unsuccessful even if the other table makes the shared standard
error positive and finite.

The primary rejection rate divides rejections from successful calculations
by all repetitions, with unsuccessful calculations counted as non-rejections.
The study applies no minimum expected-count filter.

\section{Bias, variance and degrees of freedom}
\label{sec:estimation-diagnostics}

Let \(\widehat\Delta_s\) be the corrected difference in repetition
\(s\). Its bias estimate is the mean of the finite-valued MI difference
estimates minus the known \(\Delta\). Its observed sampling variance is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{s=1}^M
 \left(\widehat\Delta_s-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u=1}^M\widehat\Delta_u\right)^2,
 \label{eq:observed-sampling-variance}
\end{equation}
where \(M\) counts the finite-valued differences, including those whose
test calculation is unsuccessful. Centring at their sample mean separates
variance from bias.

For the estimated / observed variance ratio, restrict the calculations to
the same repetitions with a finite difference and finite squared
standard error. Retain finite zero variance estimates. Divide the mean
squared standard error by the observed variance on this matched subset.
The ratio is defined only when the observed variance is positive.

Degrees-of-freedom summaries retain positive finite estimates.
The mean-to-target ratios in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df}
are shown only when every component estimate is positive and finite.
Finite zero variance estimates remain in moment calculations even when
component degrees of freedom are undefined. These diagnostics do not isolate
each estimation error's causal contribution to rejection.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Sampling variability of the estimated pointwise-MI variance.}

For each selected population, measure the observed variability of
\(\Vhat(P)\) by replacing \(\widehat\Delta_s\) with
\(\widehat V_s(P)\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:observed-sampling-variance}.
Compare the observed value with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \text{population prediction}&=\frac{\tau^2(P)}{n_P},\\
 \text{mean plug-in prediction}&=
 \frac{1}{M}\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\widehat\tau_s^2(P)}{n_P}.
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:variance-stability-predictions}
\end{equation}
The population prediction uses the known probabilities, whereas the mean
plug-in prediction uses the empirical probabilities in each repetition.
Repeat both for \(Q\). For each population, use the same repetitions with finite
\(\widehat V_s\) and \(\widehat\tau_s^2\), including finite zeros
and outputs whose test calculation is unsuccessful. Divide both predictions
by the same observed variability, which must be positive.

The checks of cell contributions and the linear approximation accompanying
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments} use 10,000 of the approximation-study
repetitions for each \(16\times16\) different-skew population at shared
MI 0.02 nats and sample sizes
\(n\in\{10,20,50,100,250,1000,10000\}\).
Contributions to \(\Vhat\) and \(\widehat\tau^2\) are separated into
once-observed cells whose row and column totals also equal one, other
once-observed cells and cells observed more than once.

For the linear-approximation check, calculate the first-order change in
each repetition using the population sensitivity from
Equation~\eqref{eq:g-final}:
\begin{equation}
 L_s(P)=\sum_{i,j}\{\widehat p_{s,ij}-p_{ij}\}g_P(i,j),
 \qquad
 R_s(P)=\widehat V_s(P)-V(P)-L_s(P).
 \label{eq:variance-linear-remainder}
\end{equation}
Here \(\widehat p_{s,ij}\) is the empirical probability in repetition
\(s\), \(L_s(P)\) is the linear contribution, and \(R_s(P)\) contains
the remaining change. Since \(\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}g_P(i,j)=0\), the linear
contribution is also the sample average of \(g_P\) in
Equation~\eqref{eq:vhat-linearization}. Its sampling variance is therefore
\(\tau^2(P)/n_P\). The decomposition
\(\widehat V_s(P)-V(P)=L_s(P)+R_s(P)\) is exact by definition of the
remainder, even when the linear approximation is poor.

Taking variances and expanding the variance of this sum gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \Var\{\widehat V_s(P)\}
 ={}&\Var\{L_s(P)\}+\Var\{R_s(P)\}\\
    &+2\operatorname{Cov}\{L_s(P),R_s(P)\}.
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:linear-remainder-variance-decomposition}
\end{equation}
Subtracting \(V(P)\) does not change the variance because the population
table is fixed. The linear contribution and remainder come from the same
sample, so their covariance must be included. In particular, the
difference between the full variance and the linear prediction is
\(\Var(R_s)+2\operatorname{Cov}(L_s,R_s)\), which can be smaller than
\(\Var(R_s)\) when the covariance is negative.

Estimate the three terms from the same \(M=10{,}000\) repetitions using sample
variances and covariance with denominator \(M-1\). Their sum equals the
observed sample variance of \(\widehat V_s\). For example, at \(n=50\)
in population \(P\), the linear variance, remainder variance and twice
their covariance are respectively 18.7\%, 87.3\% and \(-6.0\%\) of
the observed variance. The covariance partly offsets the remainder, but
the linear variance is still only about one fifth of the observed
variance. The same decomposition is calculated separately for \(Q\).

\section{Simulation uncertainty}
\label{sec:simulation-uncertainty}

For a rate estimate \(\widehat r\) from \(M\) independent repetitions,
its approximate Monte Carlo standard error is
\(\{\widehat r(1-\widehat r)/M\}^{1/2}\).
Rejection and calculation success rates use pointwise 95\% Wilson intervals
\citep{wilson1927}. These intervals describe simulation uncertainty at fixed
populations, not simultaneous coverage over every setting.

\section{Component moment targets and uncertainty}
\label{sec:moment-target-details}
\label{sec:moment-uncertainty}

The population first-order degrees of freedom
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:population-component-df}
approximate the sampling-moment target \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\) in
Equation~\eqref{eq:component-df-moments}. The target uses the actual
sampling mean and variance of \(\Vhat(P)\), measured over repeated
samples from the same population at fixed sample size. Assuming positive
\(V(P)\), \(\tau^2(P)\) and sampling variance, divide the two
definitions and rearrange to obtain the exact identity
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}}{\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}}
 =\frac{\left\{V(P)/\E\Vhat(P)\right\}^{2}}
        {\{\tau^2(P)/n_P\}/\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}.
 \label{eq:redesign-df-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The numerator is the reciprocal of the squared mean ratio in
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}; the denominator is the middle-row
predicted / observed sampling-variance ratio. A sampling mean \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\) above \(V(P)\) lowers the first-order
degrees of freedom relative to the moment target when the variance
prediction is otherwise accurate. The same calculations are made
separately for \(Q\).

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Estimating the targets and their uncertainty.}

For a component, let \(X_s=\widehat V_s\) in repetition \(s\). Use
all finite values, including zeros, even if component degrees of freedom
are undefined. From \(M\) repetitions, calculate
\begin{equation}
 \bar X=\frac1M\sum_{s=1}^M X_s,\qquad
 S_X^2=\frac1{M-1}\sum_{s=1}^M(X_s-\bar X)^2.
\end{equation}
For \(S_X^2>0\), the estimated moment target is \(2\bar X^2/S_X^2\).
To evaluate Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-df-decomposition} for \(P\),
substitute \(\bar X\) for \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\) and \(S_X^2\) for
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\).

The Monte Carlo standard error measures how much this estimated target
would change if the whole set of \(M\) repetitions were repeated.
Write \(\mu=\E(X_s)\) and \(v=\Var(X_s)>0\), so the population
moment target is \(f(\mu,v)=2\mu^2/v\). The delta method propagates
the errors in \(\bar X\) and \(S_X^2\) through a first-order Taylor
expansion. For changes \(h_\mu\) and \(h_v\), the general formula is
\begin{equation}
 f(\mu+h_\mu,v+h_v)
 \approx f(\mu,v)
 +\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu}h_\mu
 +\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}h_v.
\end{equation}
The expansion point is \((\mu,v)\), the new values are
\((\bar X,S_X^2)\), and the changes are
\(h_\mu=\bar X-\mu\) and \(h_v=S_X^2-v\). Since
\(\partial f/\partial\mu=4\mu/v\) and
\(\partial f/\partial v=-2\mu^2/v^2\), substitution gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{2\bar X^2}{S_X^2}-\frac{2\mu^2}{v}
 \approx\frac{4\mu}{v}(\bar X-\mu)
       -\frac{2\mu^2}{v^2}(S_X^2-v).
 \label{eq:moment-target-taylor}
\end{equation}
Both errors must be propagated jointly because the same repetitions
estimate the mean and variance.

The mean error is exactly the average of \(X_s-\mu\). To express the
variance error in a similar form, expand
\(X_s-\bar X=(X_s-\mu)-(\bar X-\mu)\), square and sum. The cross
term uses \(\sum_s(X_s-\mu)=M(\bar X-\mu)\), leaving
\begin{equation}
 \frac{M-1}{M}S_X^2
 =\frac1M\sum_{s=1}^M(X_s-\mu)^2-(\bar X-\mu)^2.
 \label{eq:moment-variance-centring}
\end{equation}
Subtracting \(v\) and rearranging this identity separates the leading
average from the two corrections:
\begin{equation*}
 S_X^2-v
 =\frac1M\sum_{s=1}^M\{(X_s-\mu)^2-v\}
   -(\bar X-\mu)^2+\frac{S_X^2}{M}.
\end{equation*}
Since the repetitions are independent, \(\Var(\bar X)=v/M\), so the
standard deviation of the mean error \(\bar X-\mu\) is
\(\sqrt{v/M}\). Its typical size therefore decreases as
\(M^{-1/2}\), and squaring it gives size \(M^{-1}\). As \(M\) grows
at fixed sample size, \(S_X^2\) estimates the finite variance \(v\),
so the last term \(S_X^2/M\) also has size \(M^{-1}\).
Only finitely many count tables are possible at fixed sample size, so the
centred squared deviations also have finite variance. Averaging these
independent contributions gives the first term a standard deviation
proportional to \(M^{-1/2}\). The two corrections therefore become smaller
relative to this leading sampling error.
Omitting them at first order gives
\begin{equation}
 S_X^2-v\approx\frac1M\sum_{s=1}^M\{(X_s-\mu)^2-v\}.
\end{equation}
Substituting this expression and
\(\bar X-\mu=M^{-1}\sum_s(X_s-\mu)\) into
Equation~\eqref{eq:moment-target-taylor} gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{2\bar X^2}{S_X^2}-\frac{2\mu^2}{v}
 \approx\frac1M\sum_{s=1}^M
 \left[\frac{4\mu}{v}(X_s-\mu)
       -\frac{2\mu^2}{v^2}\{(X_s-\mu)^2-v\}\right].
 \label{eq:moment-target-average-error}
\end{equation}
The first term in brackets carries the mean error and the second carries
the variance error. Replacing the unknown \(\mu,v\) by
\(\bar X,S_X^2\) gives the estimated contribution from each repetition.
Define
\begin{equation}
 U_s=X_s-\bar X,\qquad W_s=(X_s-\bar X)^2-S_X^2.
\end{equation}
The estimated contribution is
\begin{equation}
 Z_s=\frac{4\bar X}{S_X^2}U_s
       -\frac{2\bar X^2}{(S_X^2)^2}W_s.
 \label{eq:moment-df-mc-contribution}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:moment-target-average-error} represents the target error
as an average of independent repetition-level contributions. The standard
deviation of their average is their individual standard deviation divided
by \(\sqrt M\). With \(\bar Z=M^{-1}\sum_s Z_s\), its estimate is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\mathrm{MCSE}}
 =\left\{\frac{1}{M(M-1)}\sum_{s=1}^M(Z_s-\bar Z)^2\right\}^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:moment-df-mcse}
\end{equation}
The two terms within \(Z_s\) need not be independent: an unusually
large \(X_s\) can affect both the estimated mean and variance.
Calculating the standard deviation after combining the terms retains
their covariance.

For the other diagnostic ratios, apply the same Taylor formula to
\(h(a,b)=a/b\), with derivatives \(1/b\) and \(-a/b^2\):
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\widehat a}{\widehat b}-\frac{a}{b}
 \approx\frac{1}{b}(\widehat a-a)
       -\frac{a}{b^2}(\widehat b-b).
 \label{eq:diagnostic-ratio-taylor}
\end{equation}
The mean ratio \(\bar X/V(P)\) has a known denominator, so only the
numerator error contributes. The population variance-prediction ratio
\(\{\tau^2(P)/n_P\}/S_X^2\) has a known numerator, so only the
denominator error contributes. For the sample-based variance prediction,
let \(Y_s=\widehat\tau_s^2(P)/n_P\) and
\(\bar Y=M^{-1}\sum_sY_s\). Both inputs to \(\bar Y/S_X^2\) are
estimated from the same repetitions, giving estimated contributions
\begin{equation}
 Z_{s,\mathrm{ratio}}
 =\frac{Y_s-\bar Y}{S_X^2}
  -\frac{\bar Y}{(S_X^2)^2}W_s.
 \label{eq:plugin-variance-ratio-mc-contribution}
\end{equation}
Use their sample standard deviation divided by \(\sqrt M\), retaining
the pairing between the numerator prediction and the squared deviation
in each repetition. The ratio \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}/\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\)
also has a known numerator, so its error follows by propagating only
the estimated target's error through Equation~\eqref{eq:diagnostic-ratio-taylor}.

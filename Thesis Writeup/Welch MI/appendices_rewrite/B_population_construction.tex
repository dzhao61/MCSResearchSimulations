\chapter{Experimental Details}
\label{app:construction}

This appendix specifies the population formulas, target-MI search, complete
parameter grids, sampling procedure and outcome calculations. Together with
the test formulas in Chapters~\ref{chap:baselines}--\ref{chap:design},
these details define the statistical experiment.

\section{Copying construction and target selection}

For a positive marginal vector \(a\), the joint distribution is
\begin{equation}
 p_{ij}(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)a_i a_j+\lambda a_i\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}.
\end{equation}
Summing a row gives
\begin{equation}
 \sum_jp_{ij}(\lambda)
 =(1-\lambda)a_i\sum_ja_j+\lambda a_i=a_i.
\end{equation}
Summing a column similarly gives \(a_j\). The copying branch contributes
only to its matching category, while the independent branch contributes
across every category. Thus the construction preserves both margins and
has positive cells whenever \(0\leq\lambda<1\).

MI increases continuously from zero to the entropy of \(a\).
With the margins fixed, differentiating the joint terms gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\mathrm d^2 I\{P(\lambda)\}}{\mathrm d\lambda^2}
 =\sum_{i,j}\frac{(a_i\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}-a_i a_j)^2}
                     {p_{ij}(\lambda)}>0.
\end{equation}
The first derivative at zero is zero, because the change in probabilities
sums to zero and all pointwise MI values are zero there. The positive
second derivative makes the first derivative positive for
\(\lambda>0\). At the endpoint, \(Y=X\), so
\begin{equation}
 I\{P(1)\}=-\sum_i a_i\log a_i.
\end{equation}
These facts give a unique copying probability for each positive target
below the entropy endpoint.

For each population, calculate this endpoint and solve
\(I\{P(\lambda)\}-I_{\rm target}=0\) on \([0,1]\) using Brent's
method \citep{brent1973}. Absolute and relative root tolerances are
\(10^{-15}\) and \(10^{-14}\). The endpoint brackets the search;
sampled populations use the interior solution. Accept a table only when
every cell is positive, its total and marginal errors are at most
\(10^{-12}\), and its MI error is at most
\(10^{-12}+10^{-8}I_{\rm target}\). Every target is checked against its own population entropy endpoint,
including the alternatives scaled by the fitted null standard deviation.

\section{Four-cell supporting construction}

For the supporting \(4\times4\) and \(16\times16\) comparisons,
begin with the independence probabilities \(a_i a_j\) and transfer
an amount \(t\) within the first two rows and columns. The resulting
table \(R(t)\) has cell probabilities
\begin{equation}
 r_{ij}(t)=
 \begin{cases}
 a_i a_j+t,&(i,j)=(1,1)\text{ or }(2,2),\\
 a_i a_j-t,&(i,j)=(1,2)\text{ or }(2,1),\\
 a_i a_j,&\text{all other cells}.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:additive-construction}
\end{equation}
Every affected row and column gains and loses \(t\), preserving its
total. The decreasing cells have starting probability \(a_1a_2\), so
\begin{equation}
 0\leq t<t_{\max}=a_1a_2.
 \label{eq:design-tmax}
\end{equation}
Solve \(I\{R(t)\}=I_{\rm target}\) by the same root search on
\([0,t_{\max}]\). The target must lie below the endpoint MI, and the
constructed table passes the same checks as the copying table.

In a binary table the copying construction is exactly this transfer:
\begin{align}
 P(\lambda)
 &=(1-\lambda)
 \begin{pmatrix}a_1^2&a_1a_2\\a_1a_2&a_2^2\end{pmatrix}
 +\lambda\begin{pmatrix}a_1&0\\0&a_2\end{pmatrix}\\
 &=\begin{pmatrix}a_1^2&a_1a_2\\a_1a_2&a_2^2\end{pmatrix}
   +\lambda a_1a_2\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:binary-copy-equivalence}
\end{align}
The second equality uses \(a_1+a_2=1\), giving
\(a_1-a_1^2=a_1a_2\) and \(a_2-a_2^2=a_1a_2\).
Hence \(t=\lambda a_1a_2\). This equivalence does not extend to the
larger tables: copying changes cells throughout the table, whereas
Equation~\eqref{eq:additive-construction} changes only four.

\section{Validity and sparse-sample measures}
\label{sec:validity-rules}

Normal Wald requires a finite corrected difference, a finite positive
standard error, \(\Vhat(P)+\Vhat(Q)>10^{-14}\), and a finite p-value.
The threshold treats variances extremely close to zero as numerically
unusable. Expanded Welch additionally requires positive finite degrees
of freedom for both components and their combination, and a finite
expanded p-value. A failed component makes Expanded Welch invalid under
this rule, including the zero-component case in
Appendix~\ref{sec:zero-component}.

The fixed reference requires the base conditions and a positive finite
fitted reference value. No component-degree requirement is added to the fixed rule.

The primary rate divides valid rejections by all repetitions. A
method-valid rate uses only that method's valid repetitions; a
common-valid rate uses only pairs where both tests and the fixed
reference are valid. Report
the denominator for either conditional rate, which is undefined when
the denominator is zero. Invalid outputs contribute no rejection to
the primary rate.

Record the number of zero-count cells, empty rows and empty columns
separately for each population. These are sampling diagnostics rather
than eligibility rules. The minimum expected cell count is
\(\min_{ij}np_{ij}\), with the corresponding definition for \(Q\).
The study applies no minimum expected-count filter.

\section{Bias, variance and degrees-of-freedom diagnostics}
\label{sec:estimation-diagnostics}

Let \(\widehat\Delta_s\) be the corrected difference in repetition
\(s\). Its bias estimate is the mean of the finite-valued MI difference
estimates minus the known \(\Delta\). Its observed sampling variance is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{s=1}^M
 \left(\widehat\Delta_s-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u=1}^M\widehat\Delta_u\right)^2,
 \label{eq:observed-sampling-variance}
\end{equation}
where \(M\) counts those finite-valued estimates. Centre at their sample
mean, rather than the true difference, so this measures variance
separately from bias. Include finite-valued MI differences even when the test
output is invalid. For \(P\) and \(Q\), the individual corrected MI biases
are the mean finite corrected MI estimates minus \(I(P)\) and \(I(Q)\),
respectively.

For the estimated/observed variance ratio, restrict both averages to
the same repetitions with a finite difference and finite squared
standard error. Retain finite zero variance estimates. Divide the mean
squared standard error by the observed variance on this matched subset;
the ratio is undefined if the observed variance is zero. Report its
included fraction. The population comparison instead uses
\(V(P)/n_P+V(Q)/n_Q\) and the primary observed variance.

The Monte Carlo standard error of estimated bias is the observed SD
divided by \(\sqrt M\). Component degrees-of-freedom summaries use positive finite component
values, with their included fraction reported. Combined summaries use
positive finite combined values. Finite zero variance estimates remain
in the moment calculations even when component degrees of freedom are undefined. Percentile ranges describe the spread of those outputs,
not confidence intervals for their median. These summaries expose
estimation problems but do not isolate the causal contribution of
each problem to rejection. The figures show a mean-estimate-to-moment-target
ratio only where all component degrees of freedom are defined. Conditional
means elsewhere retain their inclusion counts in the numerical summaries.

The dependence diagnostic uses Spearman's rank correlation between
\(|\widehat\Delta_s-\overline{\Deltahat}|\) and \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_s\),
where the mean is taken over repetitions with both quantities finite.
No validity restriction removes finite pairs; included and excluded
counts accompany the summaries. Centring removes the mean shift so
the diagnostic measures whether larger deviations accompany larger
estimated standard errors. With identical populations and equal sample
sizes, exchanging the two samples reverses the signed difference but
leaves the standard error unchanged. A zero signed correlation can
therefore occur by symmetry even when their magnitudes are dependent.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Variability of the estimated MI variance.}

For each selected population, measure the observed variability of
\(\Vhat(P)\) by replacing \(\widehat\Delta_s\) with
\(\widehat V_s(P)\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:observed-sampling-variance}.
This measures how much the variance estimate itself changes between
samples. Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} predicts that variability to first
order. Compare the observed value with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \text{population prediction}&=\frac{\tau^2(P)}{n_P},\\
 \text{mean plug-in prediction}&=
 \frac{1}{M}\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\widehat\tau_s^2(P)}{n_P}.
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:variance-stability-predictions}
\end{equation}
The population prediction uses the known probabilities, whereas the mean plug-in
prediction uses the empirical probabilities in each repetition. Repeat both for
\(Q\). For each population, use the same repetitions with finite
\(\widehat V_s\) and \(\widehat\tau_s^2\), including finite zeros
and outputs whose test is invalid. Divide each prediction by the observed
variability: a ratio of one means agreement, and the ratio is undefined
when observed variability is zero.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Estimated direction among rejections.}

For a nonzero population difference, classify each valid two-sided rejection
by the sign of \(\Deltahat\). The estimate has the opposite sign when
\(\Deltahat\Delta<0\); it has the correct sign when
\(\Deltahat\Delta>0\). Report the number of rejections and the
number with opposite signs for each method. The fraction with opposite
signs divides the opposite-sign count by the rejection count and is undefined
if there are no rejections. These counts supplement the estimate of power,
which includes every valid rejection regardless of direction.

\section{Simulation uncertainty}
\label{sec:simulation-uncertainty}

For a rate estimate \(\widehat r\) from \(M\) independent repetitions,
its approximate Monte Carlo standard error is
\(\{\widehat r(1-\widehat r)/M\}^{1/2}\).
Wilson intervals use \(z=\Phi^{-1}(0.975)\) and have centre and
half-width
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\widehat r+z^2/(2M)}{1+z^2/M},
 \qquad
 \frac{z\sqrt{\widehat r(1-\widehat r)/M+z^2/(4M^2)}}{1+z^2/M}.
\end{equation}
These intervals are used for both rejection and validity rates
\citep{wilson1927}.

For the paired method contrast, let \(D_s\) equal the Expanded Welch
valid-rejection indicator minus the comparator
valid-rejection indicator on pair \(s\). The approximate interval for the average paired difference
\(\bar D\) is
\begin{equation}
 \bar D\ \pm\ 1.96
 \left\{\frac{1}{M(M-1)}\sum_{s=1}^M(D_s-\bar D)^2\right\}^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Use the same common-valid subset when reporting a conditional paired
contrast. Cross-construction comparisons use independent simulation
streams, as do different sample allocations, so their difference uncertainty is not calculated from paired
indicators. All intervals describe simulation uncertainty at fixed
populations, not simultaneous coverage over every setting.


\section{Moment targets and the fixed reference}
\label{sec:moment-reference-details}

The first comparison in Chapter~\ref{chap:design} measures the mean and
sampling variance of \(\Vhat(P)\) separately. Expectations and variances
refer to repeated samples from the same fixed population at the same
sample size. Substituting the predictions in
Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-moment-approximations} into the moment-matching
formula gives
\begin{equation}
 \nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}=\frac{2n_PV(P)^2}{\tau^2(P)}.
 \label{eq:redesign-first-order-df}
\end{equation}
The subscript FO identifies the population first-order formula from
Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}. The sampling-moment target instead uses
the actual mean and variance of the estimator:
\begin{equation}
 \nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}
 =\frac{2\{\E\Vhat(P)\}^2}{\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}.
 \label{eq:redesign-moment-df}
\end{equation}
The assumptions in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} ensure positive population
variance quantities for the tested populations. The two degrees-of-freedom
values can nevertheless differ at finite sample sizes. Dividing their
definitions before applying either approximation gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}}{\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}}
 =\left\{\frac{V(P)}{\E\Vhat(P)}\right\}^{2}
   \frac{\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}{\tau^2(P)/n_P}.
 \label{eq:redesign-df-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The first factor measures the contribution of the mean approximation;
the second measures the contribution of the sampling-variance
approximation. A mean estimate above \(V(P)\) lowers the first-order
degrees of freedom relative to the moment target when the variance
prediction is otherwise accurate. The same calculations are made
separately for \(Q\).

The fixed diagnostic rule uses a moment-matched reference, estimated from
independent simulations of the corresponding equal-MI populations:
\begin{equation}
 \nu_{\mathrm{ref}}=
 \frac{2\{\E(\SEhat^2)\}^{2}}{\Var(\SEhat^2)}.
 \label{eq:redesign-combined-reference}
\end{equation}
Independence of the population samples gives
\begin{align}
 \E(\SEhat^2)&=\frac{\E\Vhat(P)}{n_P}+\frac{\E\Vhat(Q)}{n_Q},\\
 \Var(\SEhat^2)&=\frac{\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}{n_P^2}
                  +\frac{\Var\{\Vhat(Q)\}}{n_Q^2}.
 \label{eq:redesign-reference-variance}
\end{align}
Substitution of the component moment targets into the combination formula
at these expected variance contributions gives exactly
Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-combined-reference}. Thus the combination
identity introduces no further approximation at the level of moments.
The next section gives the cancellation explicitly. Independent fitting samples estimate these moments by their
sample means and sample variances. The Student \(t\) distribution remains
an approximation to the shared statistic's distribution.

The fixed reference uses the two-sided p-value formula in
Equation~\eqref{eq:expanded-p}, substituting its fitted degrees of freedom. A valid p-value at most 0.05 produces a
nominal rejection.

The moment reference requires known simulation populations and is held
fixed within an evaluation setting and across the alternatives belonging
to the same null. For an alternative, the fitting populations have equal
MI of 0.02 nats and the same table size, margins, construction and sample
allocation as that alternative. Expanded Welch versus this reference measures the
change in rejection when the sample-dependent degrees of freedom are
replaced by that fixed value. The reference can still reject incorrectly
when the shared difference is biased, its standard error has the wrong
scale, or their joint distribution is poorly described by the Student
\(t\) distribution.

\section{Combining component moment targets}
\label{sec:combined-moment-identity}

The combined moment reference follows exactly from the component moments
when the two samples are independent. This calculation assumes positive
component means, finite component variances and a positive variance of
the summed contributions. Substitute the expected variance contributions
and component moment degrees of freedom into the combination formula:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\{\E\Vhat(P)/n_P+\E\Vhat(Q)/n_Q\}^{2}}
 {\dfrac{\{\E\Vhat(P)/n_P\}^{2}}
              {2\{\E\Vhat(P)\}^{2}/\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}
 +\dfrac{\{\E\Vhat(Q)/n_Q\}^{2}}
              {2\{\E\Vhat(Q)\}^{2}/\Var\{\Vhat(Q)\}}}.
 \label{eq:combined-component-moment-substitution}
\end{equation}
For either component, the square of the mean cancels:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\{\E\Vhat(P)/n_P\}^{2}}
 {2\{\E\Vhat(P)\}^{2}/\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}
 =\frac{\{\E\Vhat(P)\}^{2}}{n_P^2}
  \frac{\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}{2\{\E\Vhat(P)\}^{2}}
 =\frac{\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}{2n_P^2}.
\end{equation}
Applying the same cancellation to \(Q\) reduces
Equation~\eqref{eq:combined-component-moment-substitution} to
\begin{equation}
 \frac{2\{\E\Vhat(P)/n_P+\E\Vhat(Q)/n_Q\}^{2}}
 {\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}/n_P^2+\Var\{\Vhat(Q)\}/n_Q^2}
 =\frac{2\{\E(\SEhat^2)\}^{2}}{\Var(\SEhat^2)}.
\end{equation}
The final equality uses independence to remove the covariance between
\(\Vhat(P)/n_P\) and \(\Vhat(Q)/n_Q\). Thus matching each component
and combining at its expected contribution yields the same target as
matching the sum directly. This identity concerns moments; it does not
assert that the sum has a scaled chi-squared distribution. Using random
sample contributions instead of their expectations produces a different,
sample-dependent degrees-of-freedom rule.

\section{Moment estimation and Monte Carlo uncertainty}
\label{sec:moment-uncertainty}

For a component, let \(X_s=\widehat V_s\) for repetition \(s\), with
population sampling mean \(\mu=\E(X_s)\) and sampling variance
\(\sigma^2=\Var(X_s)>0\). From \(M\) repetitions, calculate
\begin{equation}
 \bar X=\frac1M\sum_{s=1}^M X_s,\qquad
 S_X^2=\frac1{M-1}\sum_{s=1}^M(X_s-\bar X)^2.
\end{equation}
These moments retain finite zero values. Estimate the moment degrees of
freedom by \(2\bar X^2/S_X^2\). The diagnostic decomposition uses
these evaluation moments, whereas the reference cutoff uses the
independent fitting moments. An undefined component degree of freedom
does not remove its finite variance estimate from either calculation.

To quantify uncertainty in the moment degrees of freedom, apply the
multivariate delta method to
\(f(m,v)=2m^2/v\). The first-order Taylor formula for a function of two
arguments is
\begin{equation}
 f(m+\Delta m,v+\Delta v)
 \approx f(m,v)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial m}(m,v)\Delta m
                +\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}(m,v)\Delta v.
\end{equation}
Here the expansion point is \((\mu,\sigma^2)\), the estimated point is
\((\bar X,S_X^2)\), and the differences are \(\bar X-\mu\) and
\(S_X^2-\sigma^2\). The derivatives are
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial f}{\partial m}=\frac{4m}{v},\qquad
 \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}=-\frac{2m^2}{v^2}.
\end{equation}
Substitution gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{2\bar X^2}{S_X^2}-\frac{2\mu^2}{\sigma^2}
 \approx\frac{4\mu}{\sigma^2}(\bar X-\mu)
       -\frac{2\mu^2}{(\sigma^2)^2}(S_X^2-\sigma^2).
\end{equation}
The mean and variance estimation errors come from the same repetitions,
so their covariance must be retained. Their first-order contributions
for one repetition are estimated by
\begin{equation}
 U_s=X_s-\bar X,\qquad W_s=(X_s-\bar X)^2-S_X^2.
\end{equation}
Using the fitted derivatives, the contribution for the degrees-of-freedom
estimate is
\begin{equation}
 Z_s=\frac{4\bar X}{S_X^2}U_s
       -\frac{2\bar X^2}{(S_X^2)^2}W_s.
 \label{eq:moment-df-mc-contribution}
\end{equation}
The sample SD of \(Z_s\), divided by \(\sqrt M\), estimates the Monte
Carlo standard error. Taking the variance of the combined contribution
preserves the covariance between the estimated mean and variance. The
combined reference uses the corresponding weighted contributions from
both independent populations. Mean and variance ratios use the same
delta-method calculation; the plug-in prediction and observed variance
are kept paired when both are estimated from the same outputs.

Nominal reference-rate intervals condition on the fitted degrees of
freedom. Its fitting Monte Carlo standard error is reported separately.
For the calibrated comparison, refitting the fixed degrees of freedom
would not change the ordering by \(|T|\); the calibration bootstrap
therefore resamples its empirical absolute-statistic cutoff directly.

\section{Empirical calibration and power uncertainty}

For a sample-dependent rule, assign an invalid output p-value one for
calibration and order the fitting p-values. The threshold is the
\((\lfloor0.05M\rfloor+1)\)th smallest value, and rejection requires a
strictly smaller evaluation p-value. Every tie at the boundary is
excluded. Normal Wald and the fixed reference instead share the equivalent
empirical upper-tail threshold for \(|T|\), with invalid fitting values
placed below all valid absolute statistics. This convention gives at most
5\% rejection in the fitting batch. Independent null evaluation measures
the achieved rate, including any effect of discreteness.

For focused calibrated power, resample the fitting outputs and refit
these thresholds in each of 1,000 bootstrap repetitions. Apply a common
resampled fitting batch across all points of a curve. At each evaluation
point, resample the joint decisions of both tests and the fixed moment
reference, preserving their pairing. Use the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for pointwise intervals of
rates and paired rate differences. Compare the paired intervals with the
practical tolerance of minus one to plus one percentage point.
The fixed reference and Normal Wald
remain identical after this calibration, so refitting their moment degrees
of freedom cannot change the calibrated decisions. The population tables
and effect sizes in nats stay fixed throughout the bootstrap.


\subsection{Sensitivity to the calibration population}
\label{sec:calibration-sensitivity-design}

For each focus setting and \(h=2,4\), retain the alternative
\((I(P),I(Q))=(0.02,0.02+h\sigma_0)\). The lower fitting null is
\((0.02,0.02)\); construct the upper null by setting both MI values to
\(0.02+h\sigma_0\). For each population, solve for its copying
probability using its original margin vector, retaining the dimensions
and sample sizes in Table~\ref{tab:redesign-focus-settings}.

Generate 100,000 new fitting pairs and 100,000 independent validation
pairs for each of these six upper nulls. Compare lower- and upper-null calibration
on the same original alternative pairs, then apply both sets of cutoffs
to the original lower-null and new upper-null validation pairs. Invalid
outputs remain non-rejections in every comparison. Validation concerns
these two null populations.

For uncertainty in power and its contrasts, use 1,000 joint bootstrap
repetitions. In each repetition, resample the lower fitting pairs once
per focus setting and share that resample across its two alternatives.
Independently resample each upper fitting batch and refit all cutoffs.
For a fixed alternative, jointly resample the decisions of both tests
and the fixed moment reference under both calibrations. This retains
pairing between rules and between

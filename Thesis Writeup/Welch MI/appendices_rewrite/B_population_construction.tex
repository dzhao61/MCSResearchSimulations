\chapter{Experimental Details}
\label{app:construction}

This appendix specifies the population construction, target-MI search,
settings underlying the main results, sampling procedure and outcome
calculations. Together with the test formulas in
Chapters~\ref{chap:baselines}--\ref{chap:design},
these details define the statistical experiment.
The final section records the application comparison family used for
multiple-testing adjustment.

\section{Copying construction and target selection}

For the copying construction in Equation~\eqref{eq:copying-construction},
summing a row gives
\begin{equation}
 \sum_jp_{ij}(\lambda)
 =(1-\lambda)a_i\sum_ja_j+\lambda a_i=a_i.
\end{equation}
Summing a column similarly gives \(a_j\), so both margins are preserved.
All cells are positive for \(0\leq\lambda<1\).

MI increases continuously from zero to the entropy of \(a\).
With the margins fixed, differentiating the joint terms gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\mathrm d^2 I\{P(\lambda)\}}{\mathrm d\lambda^2}
 =\sum_{i,j}\frac{(a_i\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}-a_i a_j)^2}
                     {p_{ij}(\lambda)}>0.
\end{equation}
The first derivative at zero is zero, because the change in probabilities
sums to zero and all pointwise MI values are zero there. The positive
second derivative makes the first derivative positive for
\(\lambda>0\). At the endpoint, \(Y=X\), so
\begin{equation}
 I\{P(1)\}=-\sum_i a_i\log a_i.
\end{equation}
These facts give a unique copying probability for each positive target
below the entropy endpoint.

For each population, calculate this endpoint and solve
\(I\{P(\lambda)\}-I_{\rm target}=0\) on \([0,1]\) using Brent's
method \citep{brent1973}. Absolute and relative root tolerances are
\(10^{-15}\) and \(10^{-14}\). The endpoint brackets the search;
sampled populations use the interior solution. Accept a table only when
every cell is positive, its total and marginal errors are at most
\(10^{-12}\), and its MI error is at most
\(10^{-12}+10^{-8}I_{\rm target}\). Every target is checked against its own population entropy endpoint.

\section{Parameter grids and sampling}

Table~\ref{tab:design-primary-grids} gives the approximation, broad null
and power grids. The power study uses Equation~\eqref{eq:design-fixed-gap-populations}
for every table size, margin regime and sample size in its grid. The same
copying formula and root search apply throughout.

The stronger-common-MI checks in Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy}
retain the margins and sample sizes of the selected null cases A and B.
Their common MI targets are 0.329933611140 nats for A and
0.280551105996 nats for B. Solve separately for the copying probability
of each population at its specified target.

For one repetition, draw
\begin{equation}
 (N_{P,11},\ldots,N_{P,kk})\sim\operatorname{Multinomial}
      (n_P;p_{11},\ldots,p_{kk}),
\end{equation}
and independently draw the corresponding \(Q\) counts.
Reshape these counts into their \(k\times k\) tables and apply both tests
to those same two sampled tables. Repeat independently 100,000 times
per distinct evaluation setting. The simulations use the PCG64
pseudo-random generator, with master seed 20261021 for the approximation
and null studies and 20261101 for the fixed-gap power study. Separate
streams distinguish settings and the two populations. Repeated settings
use one set of repetitions in every relevant display.

All samples retain their declared table dimensions, including empty rows
and columns. Use natural logarithms, \(0\log0=0\), and no smoothing.
The bias correction for each plug-in MI is \((k-1)^2/(2n)\); do not truncate
negative corrected estimates. In the equal-sample studies, the two bias corrections cancel in their
difference.
Calculate the standard error using
Equation~\eqref{eq:shared-se} and the component and combined degrees of freedom
using Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}.

\section{Validity and sparse-sample measures}
\label{sec:validity-rules}

Normal Wald requires a finite corrected difference, a finite positive
standard error, \(\Vhat(P)+\Vhat(Q)>10^{-14}\), and a finite p-value.
The threshold treats variances extremely close to zero as numerically
unusable. Expanded Welch additionally requires positive finite degrees
of freedom for both components and their combination, and a finite
expanded p-value. A failed component makes Expanded Welch invalid under
this rule.

For positive component degrees of freedom, substituting
Equation~\eqref{eq:nu-p-hat} and its \(Q\) analogue into
Equation~\eqref{eq:expanded-df} gives the equivalent combined form
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\nu_{\mathrm{expanded}}
  =
  \frac{
  \left\{\Vhat(P)/n_P+\Vhat(Q)/n_Q\right\}^2
  }{
  \widehat\tau^2(P)/(2n_P^3)
  +
  \widehat\tau^2(Q)/(2n_Q^3)
  }.
  \label{eq:expanded-df-direct}
\end{equation}
This direct form remains defined when one table has
\(\Vhat=\widehat\tau^2=0\) and the other gives a positive denominator.
The zero contribution drops out, leaving the other table's component degrees
of freedom. For the zero component, however, Equation~\eqref{eq:nu-p-hat} requires
\(0/0\). The specified componentwise algorithm requires both component
degrees of freedom to be positive and finite, so it records this case as
invalid even though the direct combined expression remains defined.


The primary rate divides valid rejections by all repetitions. A
method-valid rate uses only that method's valid repetitions; a
common-valid rate uses only repetitions where both tests are valid. Report
the denominator for either conditional rate, which is undefined when
the denominator is zero. Invalid outputs contribute no rejection to
the primary rate.

Record the number of zero-count cells, empty rows and empty columns
separately for each population. These are sampling diagnostics rather
than eligibility rules. The minimum expected cell count is
\(\min_{ij}np_{ij}\), with the corresponding definition for \(Q\).
The study applies no minimum expected-count filter.

\section{Bias, variance and degrees of freedom}
\label{sec:estimation-diagnostics}

Let \(\widehat\Delta_s\) be the corrected difference in repetition
\(s\). Its bias estimate is the mean of the finite-valued MI difference
estimates minus the known \(\Delta\). Its observed sampling variance is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{s=1}^M
 \left(\widehat\Delta_s-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u=1}^M\widehat\Delta_u\right)^2,
 \label{eq:observed-sampling-variance}
\end{equation}
where \(M\) counts the finite-valued differences, including those whose
test output is invalid. Centring at their sample mean separates variance
from bias. For \(P\) and \(Q\), the individual corrected MI biases
are the mean finite corrected MI estimates minus \(I(P)\) and \(I(Q)\),
respectively.

For the estimated / observed variance ratio, restrict the calculations to
the same repetitions with a finite difference and finite squared
standard error. Retain finite zero variance estimates. Divide the mean
squared standard error by the observed variance on this matched subset.
Report the included fraction. The ratio is defined only when the observed
variance is positive. The population comparison instead uses
\(V(P)/n_P+V(Q)/n_Q\) and the primary observed variance.

The Monte Carlo standard error of estimated bias is the observed SD
divided by \(\sqrt M\). Component and combined degrees-of-freedom
summaries retain positive finite values and report their included fractions.
Finite zero variance estimates remain in moment calculations even when
component degrees of freedom are undefined. Figures divide the mean
estimate by its moment target only where every component estimate is
defined; conditional summaries
elsewhere retain their inclusion counts. These diagnostics do not isolate
each estimation error's causal contribution to rejection.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Sampling variability of the estimated pointwise-MI variance.}

For each selected population, measure the observed variability of
\(\Vhat(P)\) by replacing \(\widehat\Delta_s\) with
\(\widehat V_s(P)\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:observed-sampling-variance}.
This measures how much the variance estimate itself changes between
samples. Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} predicts that variability to first
order. Compare the observed value with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \text{population prediction}&=\frac{\tau^2(P)}{n_P},\\
 \text{mean plug-in prediction}&=
 \frac{1}{M}\sum_{s=1}^{M}\frac{\widehat\tau_s^2(P)}{n_P}.
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:variance-stability-predictions}
\end{equation}
The population prediction uses the known probabilities, whereas the mean plug-in
prediction uses the empirical probabilities in each repetition. Repeat both for
\(Q\). For each population, use the same repetitions with finite
\(\widehat V_s\) and \(\widehat\tau_s^2\), including finite zeros
and outputs whose test is invalid. Divide both predictions by the same
observed variability. A ratio below one indicates underprediction, a ratio
above one indicates overprediction, and one indicates agreement. The
observed variability must be positive; a zero prediction gives a zero ratio.

\section{Simulation uncertainty}
\label{sec:simulation-uncertainty}

For a rate estimate \(\widehat r\) from \(M\) independent repetitions,
its approximate Monte Carlo standard error is
\(\{\widehat r(1-\widehat r)/M\}^{1/2}\).
Rejection and validity rates use pointwise 95\% Wilson intervals
\citep{wilson1927}.

For the paired method contrast, let \(D_s\) equal the Expanded Welch
valid-rejection indicator minus Normal Wald's valid-rejection indicator
in repetition \(s\). The approximate interval for the average paired difference
\(\bar D\) is
\begin{equation}
 \bar D\ \pm\ 1.96
 \left\{\frac{1}{M(M-1)}\sum_{s=1}^M(D_s-\bar D)^2\right\}^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Use the same common-valid subset when reporting a conditional paired
contrast. All intervals describe simulation uncertainty at fixed
populations, not simultaneous coverage over every setting.


\section{Component moment targets and uncertainty}
\label{sec:moment-target-details}
\label{sec:moment-uncertainty}

The population first-order degrees of freedom
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:population-component-df}
approximate the sampling-moment target \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\) in
Equation~\eqref{eq:component-df-moments}. The target uses the actual
sampling mean and variance of \(\Vhat(P)\), measured over repeated
samples from the same population at fixed sample size. Assuming positive
\(V(P)\), \(\tau^2(P)\) and sampling variance, divide the two
definitions and rearrange to obtain the exact identity
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}}{\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}}
 =\frac{\left\{V(P)/\E\Vhat(P)\right\}^{2}}
        {\{\tau^2(P)/n_P\}/\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}.
 \label{eq:redesign-df-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The numerator is the reciprocal of the squared mean ratio in
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}; the denominator is the middle-row
predicted / observed sampling-variance ratio. A sampling mean \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\) above \(V(P)\) lowers the first-order
degrees of freedom relative to the moment target when the variance
prediction is otherwise accurate. The same calculations are made
separately for \(Q\).

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Estimating the targets and their uncertainty.}

For a component, let \(X_s=\widehat V_s\) in repetition \(s\). Use
all finite values, including zeros, even if component degrees of freedom
are undefined. From \(M\) repetitions, calculate
\begin{equation}
 \bar X=\frac1M\sum_{s=1}^M X_s,\qquad
 S_X^2=\frac1{M-1}\sum_{s=1}^M(X_s-\bar X)^2.
\end{equation}
For \(S_X^2>0\), the estimated moment target is \(2\bar X^2/S_X^2\).
The diagnostic decomposition uses these evaluation moments.

The delta method uses a first-order Taylor expansion of
\(f(\mu,v)=2\mu^2/v\), where \(\mu\) and \(v\) are the sampling
mean and variance of \(\Vhat(P)\). Its derivatives are
\(\partial f/\partial\mu=4\mu/v\) and
\(\partial f/\partial v=-2\mu^2/v^2\). Evaluating them at
\(\bar X,S_X^2\) gives the two coefficients below. The mean and variance
errors are propagated jointly because both estimates use the same
repetitions. Define
\begin{equation}
 U_s=X_s-\bar X,\qquad W_s=(X_s-\bar X)^2-S_X^2.
\end{equation}
The first-order contribution to the target's estimation error is
\begin{equation}
 Z_s=\frac{4\bar X}{S_X^2}U_s
       -\frac{2\bar X^2}{(S_X^2)^2}W_s.
 \label{eq:moment-df-mc-contribution}
\end{equation}
Its sample SD divided by \(\sqrt M\) estimates the Monte Carlo standard
error. Calculating the SD after combining the two terms retains the
mean--variance covariance.

Mean and variance ratios use the same joint delta-method calculation,
keeping the plug-in prediction and observed variance paired.

\section{Application comparison family and Holm adjustment}
\label{sec:application-holm-family}

The shopping comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:shoppers-application} were
selected from the six comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:application-holm-family}.
For Adult, the comparisons use education or occupation versus an indicator
of annual income above \$50{,}000, comparing female and male records
\citep{becker1996}. For Student Performance, they use study time or maternal
education versus an indicator of final grade at least 10 in the Portuguese
course, comparing records from schools labelled GP and MS
\citep{cortez2008}. The shopping variables and visitor groups are defined
in Section~\ref{sec:shoppers-application}.

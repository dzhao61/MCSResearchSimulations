\chapter{Experimental Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results}

The figures plot how often each test rejects across 20,000 sampled table
pairs. A point at a true MI difference of zero measures false positives;
points to its right measure detection when the population MIs differ.
The horizontal coordinate is fixed by the population construction, not
estimated from the random samples. Blue solid circles show Normal Wald and
magenta dashed squares show Expanded Welch. The dotted line marks the
target 0.05 null rejection rate. Every plotted rate divides valid
rejections by all 20,000 replicates; a hollow marker warns that fewer
than 90\% of calculations were valid.

The comparisons proceed from null calibration to detection, sparse-sample
validity, possible causes of error and larger-sample behaviour. Additional
matched figures are in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}; Appendix~
\ref{app:evidence-index} identifies the per-configuration results.

\section{Calibration across table size and margins}

The first comparison keeps both populations uniform in a \(2\times2\)
table. Figure~\ref{fig:main-2x2-uniform} shows three equal sample sizes
and asks whether the lower Expanded Welch rejection rate is closer to
the target 0.05 at each size.

\mainlandscapefigure{2x2}{\(2\times2\)}{uniform}{both \(P\) and \(Q\) have uniform row and column margins \((1/2,1/2)\)}{uniform}{At zero difference, both methods reject below 0.05 at \(n=100\) and 1,000; Expanded Welch is lower still. At \(n=10\), Wald is slightly above 0.05 and Expanded Welch below it. Validity is high in all three panels. Rejection grows most clearly with MI difference at \(n=1{,}000\), where the two curves are close. At the smaller sizes, low rejection should not be mistaken for better detection.}
\FloatBarrier

At \(n=10\), Wald starts slightly above 0.05 and Expanded Welch below
it. At \(n=100\) and 1,000, Wald is already below the target, so the
further reduction is not an improvement in calibration. The next
question is whether larger tables change this balance. Figure~
\ref{fig:main-8x8-uniform} keeps uniform margins and the same true MI
targets and displayed sample sizes, but uses \(8\times8\) tables.

\mainlandscapefigure{8x8}{\(8\times8\)}{uniform}{both \(P\) and \(Q\) have uniform row and column margins \((1/8,\ldots,1/8)\)}{uniform}{At \(n=10\) and 100, the zero-difference rates exceed 0.05 for both methods; Expanded Welch reduces the excess but does not always reach the target. These plotted calculations are valid, so the difference is not caused by missing outputs. At \(n=100\), rejection changes little over the displayed alternatives; at \(n=1{,}000\), it grows strongly. Even with uniform margins, table size and sample size alter the trade-off.}
\FloatBarrier

With \(8\times8\) uniform margins, both methods reject too often at
the \(n=10\) and 100 null points, and Expanded Welch reduces the
excess without always reaching 0.05. These calculations are valid, so
the change is not caused by missing p-values. Yet equal MI does not
require identical population tables. Figure~\ref{fig:main-8x8-different_skew}
asks whether the conclusion persists when \(P\) and \(Q\) have
different margins at the zero-difference point.

\mainlandscapefigure{8x8}{\(8\times8\)}{different_skew}{\(P\) has row and column margins \(m_8(0.7)=(0.7,0.3/7,\ldots,0.3/7)\), while \(Q\) has \(m_8(0.8)=(0.8,0.2/7,\ldots,0.2/7)\)}{different skew}{At zero difference, both methods are too liberal at \(n=10\) and 100, although Expanded Welch rejects less. Its \(n=10\) markers are hollow, so validity contributes to that reduction. At \(n=100\), rejection falls rather than grows over the displayed alternative range; at \(n=1{,}000\), detection rises but stays lower for Expanded Welch. This is the clearest reminder not to infer power from the null rate alone.}

\FloatBarrier

In that different-skew comparison, both methods still reject too often
at the \(n=10\) and 100 null points. The hollow \(n=10\) markers show
that validity contributes to Expanded Welch's lower rate. At \(n=100\),
rejection falls as the true difference increases over the displayed
range; null calibration alone cannot predict the alternative curve.
Across the 24 main null regimes with \(n=10\) or 20,
Expanded Welch is numerically closer to 0.05 in
\(\MainTenTwentyExpandedCloserCount\), whereas Wald is closer in
\(\MainTenTwentyWaldCloserCount\). At \(n=500\) or 1,000, Wald is
closer in all \(\MainLateNullCount\) main null regimes. The \(n=5\)
regimes need separate treatment below because Expanded Welch never
rejects there. Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} shows the
remaining shape--margin plots.

Table~\ref{tab:null-paired-examples} makes the two directions of the
calibration trade-off concrete in null settings where both methods
return a p-value on every sample.

In the \(8\times8\) row, Expanded Welch moves Wald's above-target
rate closer to 0.05. In the \(2\times2\) row, it moves an already-low
rate farther below the target. Invalid outputs cannot explain either
difference. The reason for this one-sided trade-off is built into the methods:
on the same valid table pair, Expanded Welch uses the same statistic
as Wald but a Student reference with a no-smaller two-sided p-value.
It can lower rejection, not raise it. This makes detection under
unequal MI the next question.

\section{Detection as the true MI difference grows}

The main grid shows only differences up to 0.02 nats. Figure~
\ref{fig:broad-focus} extends a uniform \(2\times2\) comparison
to 0.2 nats to check whether the low rejection over small effects
persists as the true MI difference grows.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{broad_2x2_uniform.pdf}
\caption[Wider MI differences, uniform \(2\times2\)]{Wider MI differences in a uniform \(2\times2\) population pair.
The single panel has \(P\) and \(Q\) row and column margins \((0.5,0.5)\),
first-cell changes, \(n_P=n_Q=100\), \(I(P)=0.02\), and
\(I(Q)=0.02+|\Delta|\). The horizontal grid is
\(|\Delta|=0,0.001,0.002,0.005,0.01,0.02,0.05,0.1,0.2\) nats;
the rejection axis is 0--1. Each point has 20,000 replicates.}
\label{fig:broad-focus}
\resultinterpretation{At zero difference, both methods are below the 0.05
line; Expanded Welch is lower, and validity is high. Rejection then rises markedly as the true MI
difference reaches the larger targets: by 0.2 nats both methods reject more
than 90\% of samples. Expanded Welch remains below Wald at intermediate
differences, but the gap narrows when detection is already high. This wider
axis shows that the low rejection over the main grid's small differences is
not a permanent ceiling on detection.}
\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

At 0.2 nats, both methods detect the difference in more than 90\% of
samples. Their low rejection over the main grid's smaller differences
is therefore not a permanent ceiling. But the starting association
may also matter. Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-focus} repeats a \(3\times3\)
different-skew comparison with three starting MI values while keeping
the other displayed settings fixed.

All three baseline-MI null comparisons return valid calculations,
yet both methods reject too rarely and Expanded Welch is lower.
The local calculation in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} explains why
its degrees of freedom can become small near independence: the
Student cutoff becomes more demanding where the first-order MI
approximation is also less reliable. Alternative rejection is measured at the common nominal 5\%
threshold. Because the methods have different null rejection rates,
the curves should not be read as a comparison at matched achieved
false-positive rates. They show the practical trade-off under the
procedures as implemented: in these examples, the extra caution
reduces detection as well as false positives.

\section{Validity under sparse sampling}

An observed table can omit many cells even when every population
cell has positive probability. Figure~\ref{fig:extreme-focus}
pushes the margins toward one dominant category while holding
\(n_P=n_Q=100\). It asks whether near-zero rejection in very sparse
tables means good calibration or simply a failure to calculate a test.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{extreme_3x3.pdf}
\caption[Extreme-skew \(3\times3\) tables]{Extreme-skew \(3\times3\) tables. Across columns the dominant
row and column probabilities for \((P,Q)\) are
\((0.9,0.95)\), \((0.99,0.995)\), and \((0.999,0.9995)\). Each remaining
margin entry splits the leftover probability equally between the two other
categories. Both use first-cell changes, \(n_P=n_Q=100\),
\(I(P)=0.00001\), and \(I(Q)=I(P)+|\Delta|\), for
\(|\Delta|=0,0.00001,0.00005,0.0001,0.0002\) nats.
Top: 0--1; bottom: 0--0.15. Each point has 20,000 replicates; hollow
markers flag validity below 90\%.}
\label{fig:extreme-focus}
\resultinterpretation{For dominant margins 0.9 and 0.95, the null rates are
0.0701 for Wald and 0.0088 for Expanded Welch: the adjustment moves an
excessive rate past 0.05. At the most extreme margins, 0.999 and 0.9995,
both rates are zero, but only 0.0118 of Wald and 0.00005 of Expanded Welch
null calculations are valid. Rejection does not meaningfully grow over the
displayed alternatives there. The hollow markers show why near-zero
unconditional rejection cannot be read as good calibration in sparse data.}
\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

At the most extreme margins, both null rejection rates are zero, but
only 0.0118 of Wald and 0.00005 of Expanded Welch calculations are
valid. The low rejection mainly reflects a failure to return a
p-value, not good calibration.

There is a second, distinct small-sample effect. Expanded Welch
rejects in none of the \(\MainFiveNullCount\) main-grid null
regimes with \(n=5\), including uniform \(3\times3\), \(5\times5\)
and \(8\times8\) regimes where most calculations are valid. Thus its
zero rejection cannot always be explained by missing outputs. In
one \(8\times8\) different-skew alternative at \(n=5\), Wald
rejects \(\EightSparseFiveAlternativeWaldRate\) and Expanded Welch
rejects \(\EightSparseFiveAlternativeExpandedRate\), while their
valid-result rates are \(\EightSparseFiveAlternativeWaldValid\)
and \(\EightSparseFiveAlternativeExpandedValid\). Rejection and
validity must be reported together.

Wald needs a positive usable total estimated variance. The expanded
implementation additionally requires positive finite component
sensitivities and degrees of freedom. Some count tables therefore
produce a Wald p-value but no Expanded Welch p-value. The primary
rate counts such failures as non-rejections for the procedure; they
are not evidence that the population MIs are equal. The exact
software rule is recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}.

\section{Checks on population and sampling choices}

The preceding figures isolate the central trade-off. Additional
matched checks ask whether it depends on where the association sits,
how the populations are constructed, the sample allocation or the
table shape. Their plots and exact specifications are collected in
Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}. Moving association between
early, rare and spread cells changes the underlying table even when
margins and MI are fixed (Figure~\ref{fig:patterns-focus}). An
ordinal log-linear construction also changes rates under matched
margins and MI (Figure~\ref{fig:construction-focus}).
Reversing which population has the larger sample or higher MI changes
the alternative curves (Figures~\ref{fig:imbalance-q} and
\ref{fig:imbalance-p}). Rectangular tables show that the conservative
pattern is not exclusive to square shapes
(Figure~\ref{fig:rectangular-focus}). These checks limit how far any
single plotted example can be generalised; none removes the need to
judge null calibration and validity for the particular population
pair.

The exact-independence check in
Figure~\ref{fig:independence-focus} is kept with those supporting
plots because it addresses a different mathematical boundary:
the first-order MI variance becomes zero at independence.
Appendix~\ref{app:independence-boundary} explains why a one-table
independence test has a different reference distribution.

\section{Limits of the Student reference}
\label{sec:mechanism-results}

Both methods use the same estimated MI difference and the same
plug-in standard error. Changing the reference distribution leaves
errors in either quantity untouched. In larger different-skew null
tables, the two populations can also have different remaining
finite-sample MI biases even though their leading equal-sample bias
corrections cancel. That can move the numerator away from zero;
the present plots do not isolate how much of the excess rejection
it explains.

A supplementary diagnostic makes the shared-standard-error issue
concrete. For a \(3\times3\) different-skew null with
\(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\) and \(n=100\), ordinary Wald rejects
0.0166 of independently simulated table pairs. Keeping the same
estimated MI differences but dividing each by one fixed finite-sample
standard deviation estimated from an independent pilot gives 0.0551.
Using the fixed population first-order standard deviation instead
gives 0.0976. The confirmatory Wald rate is 0.0171; its small
difference from 0.0166 is Monte Carlo variation between runs.

Across the evaluated pairs, the average squared plug-in standard
error is about 1.17 times the finite-sample variance of the MI
difference estimated in the pilot. However, fixing the denominator
changes two things at once: its size no longer varies across samples,
and its relationship with the estimated MI difference is removed.
The rejection-rate contrast therefore cannot be divided into
separate causal shares. Appendix~\ref{sec:mi-variance-covariance}
shows a positive first-order covariance between each table's MI
estimate and its variance estimate for small positive MI on the
fixed-margin path. The relationship for the two-sample statistic
depends on both populations. A Student cutoff based on variance
fluctuations alone does not model that joint behaviour or make the
statistic exactly Student distributed.

This explains why the negative result is informative. The correction
addresses uncertainty in one estimated variance, but some important
finite-sample errors arise elsewhere in the shared test statistic.
The fixed-denominator calculations are diagnostics, not proposed
replacement tests. The supplementary results report contains their
full pilot records.

\section{Larger samples and computational cost}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-focus} keeps each population pair fixed
under equal MI and increases the sample size. This tests whether the
poor null rates are temporary small-sample effects for the chosen
populations, and whether the answer changes with their common MI.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{convergence.pdf}
\caption[Large-sample null behaviour]{Large-sample null behaviour. Rows compare \(2\times2\) and
\(8\times8\) tables; columns have common true MI 0.0001 and 0.02 nats.
For both shapes, \(P\)'s row and column margins are \(m_k(0.7)\) and
\(Q\)'s are \(m_k(0.8)\), with \(k=2\) or 8. Both populations use the
first-cell block. The horizontal axis is the equal sample size
\(n_P=n_Q=1{,}000,2{,}500,10{,}000,50{,}000\) on a labelled log scale;
the vertical axis is null rejection from 0 to 0.10. Each point has 20,000
replicates.}
\label{fig:convergence-focus}
\resultinterpretation{The horizontal axis is sample size, and every point
is a zero-difference null; there are no alternative-detection points. All
displayed calculations are valid. In the
displayed \(2\times2\) and \(8\times8\) settings, rates move toward 0.05
more clearly when common MI is 0.02 than when it is 0.0001. Across all 12
first-block shape--margin combinations at \(n=50{,}000\), the 0.02-MI
null rates range from 0.0470 to 0.0527 for Wald and 0.0466 to 0.0526 for

\end{figure}

\FloatBarrier

At common MI 0.02, the null rates approach 0.05 in the tested
populations as sample size grows. At MI 0.0001, they remain low even
at \(n=50{,}000\). This is consistent with the first-order
approximation becoming useful more slowly near independence, but the
four displayed panels do not establish a universal sample-size
threshold for other populations.

Expanded Welch also requires more computation. A full method call
has the same \(O(rc)\) asymptotic complexity as Wald, but its extra
sensitivity and degree-of-freedom calculations take longer in the
measured implementation. Table~\ref{tab:runtime-by-shape} shows
representative timings; all 60 regimes are indexed in
Appendix~\ref{app:evidence-index}.

Across the 60 timing regimes, the median call time is about
85 microseconds for Wald and 150 for Expanded Welch, a ratio of
about 1.75. These timings measure calls after tables are available;
they exclude population construction and sampling. The extra cost
is modest beside the statistical issue. Expanded Welch is useful
where Wald is demonstrably too liberal and Expanded Welch remains
valid, but the figures do not support replacing Wald across all
regimes. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} states the contribution and
limits of that conclusion.

\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_redesign_reporting_values}

This chapter checks the approximations used to choose Expanded Welch's
degrees of freedom, then compares its false-positive rates and power with
Normal Wald. Supporting simulations and the online shopping application
examine how the findings extend to other settings.

One simulation repetition consists of independently drawing one count table
from \(P\) and one from \(Q\). Normal Wald and Expanded Welch are both
applied to the same two sampled tables in that repetition. Each evaluation
setting uses 100,000 repetitions.

\section{RQ1: Accuracy of variance approximations}

For a fixed population table \(P\) and sample size \(n_P\), each
repetition produces a new \(\Vhat(P)\). We examine its average value,
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\), and its variation between repetitions,
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\). These are the two moments needed for the component
degrees of freedom. Table~\ref{tab:results-variance-quantities} distinguishes
them from the variation of the MI difference examined later.

\midrule

\subsection{Mean and sampling-variance predictions}

Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-moment-approximations} predicts
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx V(P)\) and
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx\tau^2(P)/n_P\), where \(\tau^2(P)\)
measures the variability of the observation-level sensitivities derived
in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}. We compare each prediction with the
corresponding moment observed across repetitions, and repeat the checks
for \(Q\).

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments} shows the different-skew populations
at common MI 0.02 nats. The top row divides \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\)
by the population benchmark \(V(P)\), and likewise for \(Q\).
The middle row divides \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) by the observed
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\). The bottom row uses the average
\(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\) as its numerator and the same observed
variance as its denominator. Both variance rows use the same vertical
scale, so their differences show the effect of substituting sample
proportions. A ratio of one indicates agreement. Shading in the first
two rows shows approximate 95\% Monte Carlo
intervals from 100,000 repetitions; their half-widths are below 1.3\%
of the plotted values, making them barely visible on this scale.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_moment_predictions_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Mean and sampling-variance ratios under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:redesign-moments}
\end{figure}

Top-row ratios above one show that \(V(P)\) underestimates
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\), and likewise for \(Q\). The larger tables show
greater relative mean error over much of the sample-size range. For \(16\times16\)
population \(P\) at \(n=1{,}000\), the observed mean is about
\(3.24V(P)\). The initial rise of the larger-table curves shows that mean error
first increases with sample size before declining.

Middle-row curves below one show that \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\)
underestimates \(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\). In the same \(16\times16\)
comparison at \(n=1{,}000\), the ratio is 0.756: the prediction is
about 24\% below the observed sampling variance. Substituting sample
proportions can reverse the error. The bottom-row ratio is 1.67,
so the average sample-based prediction is about 67\% above the same
observed variance. The population calculation therefore
underestimates variation, whereas the sample-based calculation overestimates it.

At larger sample sizes, both variance rows approach one while the
mean approximation can retain a larger error. For \(16\times16\)
population \(P\) at \(n=50{,}000\), the observed mean is still about
7\% above \(V(P)\), even though both predictions of
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\) are close to its observed value. The calculation
therefore describes the variation of \(\Vhat(P)\) between samples more
accurately than its average value. The experimental supplement provides
the companion uniform and same-skew plots.

\subsection{How moment errors affect degrees of freedom}

Equation~\eqref{eq:design-moment-target} defines
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\) from \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\) and
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\). We compare this target with \(\nu_P\),
calculated using the population first-order approximations, and
\(\widehat\nu_P\), estimated from each sampled table. Their differences show how the moment approximations and the
substitution of sample proportions affect the degrees of freedom.

For the same different-skew populations,
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df} uses the moment target,
estimated from the simulations, as the common denominator. The top row shows
\(\nu_P/\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\); the bottom row shows
\(\E(\widehat\nu_P)/\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\), and likewise for \(Q\).
Both rows use the same vertical scale. Ratios below one mean too few
degrees of freedom; ratios above one mean too many. Bottom-row curves
require all component estimates to be positive and finite. Shading in
the top row gives approximate 95\% Monte Carlo intervals.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_component_df_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Component degrees-of-freedom approximations relative to their moment targets.}
\label{fig:redesign-component-df}
\end{figure}

Underestimating \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\) lowers \(\nu_P\) relative to
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\). For different-skew
\(4\times4\) populations at \(n=1{,}000\) and MI 0.02, the
predicted / observed sampling-variance ratios are 0.970 for \(P\) and
0.967 for \(Q\).
The observed means are \(1.190V(P)\) and \(1.174V(Q)\). The first-order
calculation substitutes \(V(P)\) for the larger sampling mean
\(\E\Vhat(P)\), and likewise for \(Q\). Because the moment-matching
formula squares the mean, this substitution lowers the degrees of freedom
even though the sampling-variance prediction is close. The resulting
first-order values, 15.30 and 13.61, fall below the moment targets,
21.03 and 18.14. Table~\ref{tab:variance-stability} gives the component
values; Appendix~\ref{sec:moment-target-details} gives the algebra.

In the table, ``Mean ratio'' is the observed mean of \(\Vhat(P)\)
divided by \(V(P)\), or the corresponding ratio for \(Q\).
``Var. ratio (pop.)'' and ``Var. ratio (sample)'' divide
\(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) and the average \(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\),

\(\widehat\nu_P\) or \(\widehat\nu_Q\) over positive finite estimates;
all 100,000 are included in each shown row. ``Moment df'' gives
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\) or \(\nu_{Q,\mathrm{mom}}\), while ``FO df''
gives \(\nu_P\) or \(\nu_Q\).

Mean estimated component degrees of freedom can also fall below their
moment targets (Table~\ref{tab:redesign-component-spread}). At A (uniform
\(4\times4\), \(n_P=n_Q=20\)), the \(P\) target is 14.81 against
an average estimate of 6.52, giving an estimate / target ratio of 0.44.
For the \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) same- and different-skew cases,
these ratios range from 0.55 to 0.60. All 100,000 component estimates
are included in these comparisons. At C (different-skew \(4\times4\),
\(n_P=n_Q=10{,}000\)), the ratios are about 0.99.
The bottom row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df} shows the discrepancy when
population probabilities in the first-order formula are replaced by
sample proportions. The moment target itself does not guarantee an
exact Student \(t\) distribution for the test statistic.

\subsection{Behaviour near independence}

As MI approaches zero, \(V(P)\) and \(V(Q)\) become small. Terms
quadratic in the sampling error can then contribute substantially to
\(\Vhat(P)\) and \(\Vhat(Q)\), so the first-order predictions need
not remain accurate. The near-independence calculation in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} retains these quadratic terms
to approximate the component moments and estimated degrees of freedom.

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4} shows \(4\times4\) different-skew
populations at \(n=1{,}000\). The first two rows compare the observed
moments of \(\Vhat\) with their near-independence predictions. The
bottom row compares the mean estimated degrees of freedom, its
near-independence prediction, the sampling-moment target and the
population first-order value.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=.78\textheight,keepaspectratio]{focused_redesign_near_independence_different_skew_1000.pdf}
\caption{Component moments and degrees of freedom near independence.}
\label{fig:redesign-a4}
\end{figure}

At common MI 0.0001, the near-independence calculation predicts
\(n\E\{\Vhat(P)\}=9.2\) for each population; the observed value for
\(P\) is 9.158. The mean estimated component degrees of freedom for
\(P\) are 4.86, close to the prediction 4.6, while the sampling-moment
target is about nine. Thus the near-independence calculation describes
the mean estimated degrees of freedom accurately even though that mean
remains below the moment target. The experimental supplement retains
the median, percentile range and near-independence prediction of the
moment target in the full diagnostic plots.

Near independence, Table~\ref{tab:variance-stability} shows opposite
errors for the first-order population and sample-based predictions. At the smallest MI target in
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4}, \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) and \(\tau^2(Q)/n_Q\)
are only about 5\% of the observed sampling variances. The mean
sample-based predictions are instead 1.97 times the observed variance
for \(P\) and 2.14 times for \(Q\), indicating overprediction.
The quadratic sampling contribution remains substantial, while the
population first-order degrees of freedom are below 0.1.

At MI 0.02, \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) and \(\tau^2(Q)/n_Q\) are much more
accurate. The sample-based predictions still overestimate variation:
their predicted / observed ratios are 1.080 for \(P\) and 1.072 for \(Q\). The remaining mean error still lowers
the first-order degrees of freedom relative to the moment target.
Appendix~\ref{sec:component-diagnostics} reports the mean error across
MI and margins.

\section{RQ2.1: False-positive rates under equal MI}

We now set \(I(P)=I(Q)\), so every rejection is a false positive.
Both tests use \(T=\Deltahat/\SEhat\), but Expanded Welch uses a
cutoff from the Student \(t\) distribution in place of Wald's standard
normal cutoff. This larger cutoff reduces rejection. Whether that
reduction improves accuracy depends on whether Wald's rate exceeds or
falls below the nominal 5\% level.

\subsection{Overall false-positive patterns}
\label{sec:null-rejection-patterns}

Expanded Welch improves false-positive rates in selected settings where
Normal Wald rejects too often. In others, it further lowers already
conservative rates. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-null-grid} summarises the
120 settings at common MI 0.02 nats.

We classify Expanded Welch as improving a setting when its false-positive
rate is closer to 5\% than Wald's by more than
\RedesignGridTolerance{} percentage points. It worsens a setting when its
rate is farther from 5\% by more than \RedesignGridTolerance{} percentage
points. Differences within this tolerance are grouped separately. All
\RedesignToward{} improvements occur with Wald above 5\%;
\RedesignAwayBelow{} of the \RedesignAway{} deteriorations occur with
Wald below 5\%.

\subsection{Bias and standard-error accuracy}
\label{sec:null-estimator-accuracy}

To explain these rejection rates, we examine the two shared estimates.
Difference bias measures how far the average \(\Deltahat\) lies from
\(\Delta\). The variance ratio for \(\Deltahat\) divides the average
\(\SEhat^2\) by the observed \(\Var(\Deltahat)\). A ratio below one
means that the squared standard error underestimates sampling variance;
a ratio above one means that it overestimates sampling variance.
These diagnostics distinguish a shifted estimated difference from an
inaccurate standard error. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-shared-estimates}
compares the focus settings and the sparse \(16\times16\) cases at
\(n=1{,}000\). The full diagnostics, including the larger-sample
\(16\times16\) cases, remain in Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-diagnostics}.

\end{table}

Expanded Welch brings rejection close to 5\% at A. At B it also
reduces rejection, although the rate remains above 5\%.

variation of \(\Deltahat\). At B, \(\Deltahat\) is biased upwards

Changing the degrees of freedom does not resolve the sparse-table
failure. At \(16\times16\), different skew and \(n=1{,}000\), the
difference bias is about 0.030 nats, or 2.06 observed SDs. Both tests
reject far too often; changing the rejection cutoff leaves the bias
unchanged.
Under the equal-MI null, the estimated difference is centred at
\begin{equation}
 \E(\Deltahat)\approx 0+0.02985=0.02985\text{ nats}.
 \label{eq:redesign-null-bias-shift}
\end{equation}
This shift is large relative to the observed SD of approximately 0.01450
nats. It moves most rejections into the positive tail even though the
population difference is zero.

Under equal MI, the same-skew populations have identical tables. With
equal sample sizes, their expected MI estimation biases therefore cancel
in the difference. The average \(\SEhat^2\) exceeds the observed
\(\Var(\Deltahat)\), and both rejection rates are far below 5\%.
The matched populations distinguish rejection driven by a large
difference bias from conservative behaviour when that bias cancels.
At \(n=10{,}000\), both skew regimes have much smaller estimation errors
and rates closer to 5\%.


The sparse-table failure also occurs under the four-cell construction.
At \(16\times16\), common MI 0.02 nats and
\(n_P=n_Q=1{,}000\), replacing copying by a probability transfer
within four cells preserves each population's margins and MI.
Under different-skew margins, the estimated difference remains
substantially biased and both false-positive rates are near 30\%.
The experimental supplement reports the full construction comparison.

\section{RQ2.2: Nominal and calibrated power}

The power comparison uses A (uniform \(4\times4\), \(n_P=n_Q=20\)),
B (different-skew \(8\times8\), \(n_P=n_Q=100\)) and
C (different-skew \(4\times4\), \(n_P=n_Q=10{,}000\)). For each
setting, \(\sigma_0\) is the independently fitted SD of \(\Deltahat\)
under \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\) nats. We hold \(I(P)=0.02\) and increase
\(I(Q)\), so \(\Delta=I(P)-I(Q)\) is negative under the alternatives.
The horizontal axis is \(-\Delta/\sigma_0\): moving right increases
the MI advantage of \(Q\) in fitted null SD units.
Nominal power uses each test's usual 5\% cutoff; calibrated power uses
cutoffs fitted to target 5\% rejection at a specified equal-MI population.

\subsection{Nominal power}

Expanded Welch has lower nominal power than Normal Wald at the selected
small-sample settings A and B (Table~\ref{tab:redesign-focus-settings}).
At A, two null SDs correspond to about 0.310 nats: Expanded Welch rejects
35.9\% of repetitions, compared with 41.0\% for Wald.
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-power} shows all three focus settings with MI
differences measured in fitted null standard deviations. The nominal
comparison includes their different null rejection rates at zero.
Appendix~\ref{sec:calibration-sensitivity-results} gives numerical rates
and differences between methods evaluated on the same simulated data.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_power.pdf}
\caption{Nominal power across differences measured in fitted null standard deviations.}
\label{fig:redesign-power}
\end{figure}

As the population MI values move farther apart at B, rejection initially
falls before rising. Expanded Welch's rate falls from 5.52\% under the null to
1.99\% at one null SD, before rising to 33.1\% at four SDs. As
\(I(Q)\) increases, the true signed difference becomes negative,
initially offsetting the positive estimation bias observed under the
null. The estimated difference moves towards zero, reducing \(|T|\)
and rejection before the curve rises. This distortion of the shared
statistic remains when the rejection cutoff is increased.
Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} reports the estimated directions
among rejections in the sparse-table comparisons.

At C, nominal power differences are small. At two null SDs, or about
0.00635 nats, Wald gives 45.2\% and Expanded Welch gives 44.9\%.
Expanded Welch's loss relative to Wald is 0.30 percentage points,
with a 95\% interval of 0.27--0.34. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-power-contrasts}
reports comparisons at two null SDs for all three focus settings.
Each interval uses both methods' decisions from the same simulation
repetitions to estimate their power difference. These are paired
comparisons: the pairing is between methods applied to the same data.
The calibrated comparison then assesses power after matching null rejection.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Power after matching false-positive rates}

At settings A and B, Expanded Welch's lower nominal power accompanies a
lower false-positive rate than Normal Wald's. We now ask whether its
power remains lower after adjusting both tests to have approximately the
same false-positive rate.

Empirical calibration fits a separate p-value threshold for each test
using independent equal-MI simulations to target 5\% rejection. These
replace the usual threshold of 0.05. We hold them fixed, check their
false-positive rates on
independent equal-MI samples, and apply them to the same alternative
samples used in the nominal comparison. The MI difference \(\Deltahat\),
standard error \(\SEhat\) and calculated p-values remain unchanged.

We first fit the thresholds at \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\) nats. On independent
null evaluation samples, both tests reject between 4.93\% and 4.99\%
across A--C, so their false-positive rates are closely matched there.
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-calibrated-power} shows rejection under these
thresholds: at zero on the horizontal axis it measures false positives;
to the right, it measures power against increasing MI differences.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_power_calibrated.pdf}
\caption{Power after calibration at common MI 0.02; bands show bootstrap uncertainty.}
\label{fig:redesign-calibrated-power}
\end{figure}

\begin{samepage}
At A and two null SDs, Normal Wald has calibrated power of 32.57\%,
compared with 36.15\% for Expanded Welch: a gain of 3.58 percentage
points. Thus the nominal power ordering reverses at this alternative
after matching false-positive rates at common MI 0.02.
\par
\end{samepage}

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Does the calibration population change the comparison?}

The first comparison matches false-positive rates at common MI 0.02.
We next ask whether Expanded Welch's power advantage persists when the
thresholds are fitted at a different common MI.

For an alternative with \(I(P)=0.02\) and \(I(Q)>0.02\), we compare:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Lower-null calibration:} fit both tests at
  \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\), the alternative's smaller MI.
  \item \textbf{Upper-null calibration:} fit both tests with their
  population MI values equal to the alternative's larger \(I(Q)\).
\end{itemize}
Both calibration populations retain the alternative's margins and sample
sizes. Applying both sets of thresholds to the same alternative samples
ensures that only the thresholds change the power comparison.

Table~\ref{tab:redesign-calibration-sensitivity} reports Expanded Welch
power minus Normal Wald power at two and four null SDs. Gains are in
percentage points: positive values favour Expanded Welch and negative
values favour Wald. The upper-MI column gives the common MI used for
upper-null fitting. Brackets give 95\% bootstrap intervals accounting
for the shared evaluation data and uncertainty from fitting thresholds.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\caption{Expanded Welch minus Wald power under two null calibrations.}
\label{tab:redesign-calibration-sensitivity}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_redesign_calibration_sensitivity}
\end{table}

Changing the fitting population reverses the gain in two comparisons.
At A and two null SDs, the 3.58-point gain becomes a 0.76-point loss.
At B and four SDs, a 3.92-point gain becomes a 0.39-point loss.
Under upper-null calibration, A at four SDs retains a small positive
estimate whose interval includes zero, while B at two SDs retains a much
smaller gain. At C, both calibrations give differences well within the
one-percentage-point tolerance.

The calibrated gains therefore depend on where we match false-positive
rates. Appendix~\ref{sec:calibration-sensitivity-results} reports the
rates from applying each calibration to both null populations and the
full power tables. Matching at one population does not ensure matching
elsewhere. These two checks also do not establish that either test keeps
its false-positive rate at or below 5\% for every equal-MI population.

B's initial fall in rejection also remains after lower-null calibration
for both tests. Calibration leaves the biased \(\Deltahat\) unchanged,
so a calibrated power gain can coexist with distortion of the power curve.

\section{Application to online shopping data}
\label{sec:shoppers-application}

The Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention dataset records website sessions
and whether each session ends in a purchase \citep{sakar2018}. We use it
to address two application questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Month:} Does month provide the same amount of information
  about purchasing among new and returning visitors?
  \item \textbf{Weekend:} Does weekend status provide the same amount of
  information about purchasing among new and returning visitors?
\end{enumerate}

The dataset contains 12,330 sessions, with each session belonging to a
different user according to the source documentation \citep{sakar2018}.
The analysis retains all 1,694 sessions labelled \texttt{New\_Visitor}
and 10,551 labelled \texttt{Returning\_Visitor}, excluding the 85
\texttt{Other} sessions. The outcome is the original Boolean
\texttt{Revenue} variable: true indicates a purchase and false indicates
no purchase. The tests treat the two disjoint visitor groups as independent
samples and the sessions within each group as independent observations.

For each question, \(X\) is the session attribute and \(Y\) is the
purchase outcome. The two populations are defined by visitor type:
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Variables and populations for the two application questions.}
\label{tab:shoppers-questions}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
\toprule
 & Month question & Weekend question\\
\midrule
\(X\) & Month of the session & Weekday or weekend\\
\(Y\) & \multicolumn{2}{l}{Purchase outcome: no purchase or purchase}\\
\(P\) & \multicolumn{2}{l}{Joint distribution of \((X,Y)\) among new visitors}\\
\(Q\) & \multicolumn{2}{l}{Joint distribution of \((X,Y)\) among returning visitors}\\
\(H_0\) & \multicolumn{2}{l}{\(I(P)=I(Q)\) for each choice of \(X\)}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}
For month, each group forms a \(10\times2\) count table. The rows are
the ten recorded months: February, March, May, June, July, August,
September, October, November and December. The columns are no purchase
and purchase. Both groups use these categories, with no smoothing or
removal of zero cells. All ten months occur in both groups, although the
new-visitor February row contains only one session and no purchase.
Replacing month with the Boolean \texttt{Weekend} variable gives a
\(2\times2\) table for each group, with weekday and weekend rows;
all four cells are observed.

The leading MI bias correction subtracts
\((10-1)(2-1)/(2n)=9/(2n)\) from each month plug-in estimate
\citep{miller1955,moddemeijer1989}, and \(1/(2n)\) from each weekend
estimate, where \(n\) is the relevant group size. The corrected
difference \(\Deltahat\) is new-visitor MI minus returning-visitor MI.
Table~\ref{tab:shoppers-application} gives these differences alongside
their shared standard errors, \(\SEhat\), and the resulting
\(T=\Deltahat/\SEhat\). MI estimates, differences and standard errors
are in nats.

Both tests reject equal month--purchase MI. Expanded Welch's larger
p-value reflects the heavier tails of the Student \(t\) distribution
with 26.79 degrees of freedom.

For weekend, both corrected MI estimates are approximately 0.0007 nats.
Their difference is only about 0.056 standard errors from zero, so both
p-values are near one. Neither test provides evidence of different
weekend--purchase MI between the groups; this does not establish equality.

Expanded Welch's 2.18 degrees of freedom for weekend reflect substantial
estimated sampling uncertainty relative to the sizes of \(\Vhat(P)\)
and \(\Vhat(Q)\), even with large samples
(Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4}). The unknown population tables prevent a
direct check of the sampling-moment target.

These two shopping comparisons were selected from an initial exploration
of six comparisons across three public datasets. Holm's adjustment for
that original family \citep{holm1979} gives month p-values of 0.000189
for Normal Wald and 0.00149 for Expanded~Welch, so both tests still reject
equal month--purchase MI. Table~\ref{tab:application-holm} reports all raw
and adjusted p-values, with the adjustment applied separately to each
method's six tests.

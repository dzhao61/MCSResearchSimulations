\label{chap:results}

This chapter checks the approximations used to choose Expanded Welch's
degrees of freedom, then compares its false-positive rates and power with
Normal Wald. Supporting simulations examine additional settings, and the
online-shopping application illustrates interpretation of the test outputs.

Each evaluation setting uses 100,000 independent repetitions, each drawing
one count table from \(P\) and one independently from \(Q\). Normal Wald
and Expanded Welch are both applied to the same two sampled tables in
each repetition.

\section{RQ1: Accuracy of variance approximations}

For a fixed population table \(P\) and sample size \(n_P\), each
repetition produces an estimate \(\Vhat(P)\) of the pointwise-MI variance
\(V(P)\). We examine its average value,
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\), and its variation between repetitions,
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\), because both moments enter the component
degrees-of-freedom calculation. Table~\ref{tab:results-variance-quantities} in
Chapter~\ref{chap:baselines} distinguishes these quantities from the
sampling variance of the MI difference.

\subsection{Mean and sampling-variance predictions}

Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-moment-approximations} predicts
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx V(P)\) and
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx\tau^2(P)/n_P\), where \(\tau^2(P)\)
measures the variability of the observation-level sensitivities derived
in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}. We compare each prediction with the
corresponding moment observed across repetitions, and repeat the checks
for \(Q\).

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments} shows the different-skew populations
at common MI 0.02 nats, with row and column margins \(m_k(0.7)\) for
\(P\) and \(m_k(0.8)\) for \(Q\).
The top row divides the simulated mean of
\(\Vhat(P)\) by the population benchmark \(V(P)\), and likewise for \(Q\).
The middle row divides \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) by the observed
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\). The bottom row uses the average
\(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\) as its numerator and the same observed
variance as its denominator. Both variance rows use the same vertical
scale, so their differences show the effect of substituting sample
proportions. A ratio of one indicates agreement. Shading in the first
two rows shows approximate 95\% Monte Carlo
intervals from 100,000 repetitions. Their half-widths are below 1.3\%
of the plotted values, making the bands barely visible on this scale.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_moment_predictions_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Mean and sampling-variance ratios under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:redesign-moments}
\end{figure}

In the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}, larger tables have
higher peaks in the mean ratio and take more observations to approach
one. The \(2\times2\) curve falls towards one without a
pronounced peak, whereas the \(4\times4\), \(8\times8\) and
\(16\times16\) curves first rise and then fall. The \(4\times4\)
peak is smaller and earlier than those for the two larger tables.
The rise in these curves occurs as rare combinations that were initially
unobserved begin to appear in sampled tables. Cells observed only once
can have estimated pointwise MI far from the table's plug-in MI,
producing large squared deviations in \(\Vhat\). For example, if a
cell and its row and column each contain one observation, its empirical
pointwise MI is \(\log n\). Decomposing the cell contributions in
10,000 repetitions at each of seven selected sample sizes from 10 to
10,000 for the \(16\times16\) different-skew populations shows that
once-observed cells contribute most of the mean of \(\Vhat\) around
the peak in both populations.
Their contribution falls as repeated counts give better estimates of
the cell probabilities. Larger tables spread probability over more rare
combinations under these margin rules, so this distortion persists to
larger sample sizes. Both populations show these patterns, but the
larger-table peaks are higher in \(P\) than in \(Q\), and the
\(16\times16\) peak occurs later in \(Q\). For each of these larger
table sizes, the peak mean of \(\Vhat\) is greater in \(P\) than in
\(Q\), while \(V(P)<V(Q)\). Both the larger numerator and the smaller
denominator therefore raise \(P\)'s peak ratio. Equal MI fixes the population mean of pointwise MI, whereas its variance
\(V\) also depends on the cell-specific pointwise-MI values and their
probability weights, which change with the margins.

In the middle row, the \(2\times2\) curves have shallow troughs,
while the larger tables have progressively deeper troughs and take
longer to approach one. Ratios below one mean that \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\)
understates the observed \(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\), with the same
interpretation for \(Q\). The dip develops because, for a fixed
population, the predicted variance decreases exactly as \(1/n\), while
the observed sampling variance does not initially follow that decrease.
At small sample sizes, many rare combinations remain unobserved. As
\(n\) increases, some sampled tables contain a given rare cell while
others still miss it. An unobserved cell contributes zero to \(\Vhat\),
whereas a single observation can give a large squared deviation of its
pointwise MI from the table's plug-in MI. The \(1/n\) change in a cell
proportion can be large relative to its true probability, so its effect
on \(\Vhat\) can be poorly represented by linearisation around the
population table. The observed variance can therefore
initially rise, or fall more slowly than \(1/n\), while the predicted variance decreases, so the ratio then falls. In the same \(16\times16\)
repetitions, subtracting the linear change from \(\Vhat-V\) leaves
a remainder whose variance exceeds that of the linear change around the
trough. Including their covariance in the total variance confirms that
the linear change alone misses much of the observed variation.
With larger samples, repeated
counts make the estimated cell probabilities and their log ratios more
stable. The variation missed by the linear approximation becomes smaller
relative to \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\), and the observed variance approaches
this prediction, so the ratio rises back towards one. Under these margin
rules, larger tables contain more rare combinations and require more
observations before this approximation becomes accurate. The observed
variance measures spread about the sampling mean, so it can be predicted
accurately while that mean remains above the population benchmark, as
in the top row. The same ordering by table size appears in \(P\) and
\(Q\), but the \(16\times16\) trough occurs later in \(Q\), where the
rarer row and column categories have lower probabilities and accumulate
observations more slowly.

In the bottom row, the \(4\times4\), \(8\times8\) and
\(16\times16\) sample-based predictions first underestimate the
observed sampling variance, then overestimate it before approaching
agreement. Their ratios begin below one, rise through one to a peak,
and then fall towards one, although the \(16\times16\) curves initially
dip further before rising. The \(4\times4\) overshoot is smaller and
earlier than those for the two larger tables, while \(16\times16\)
has the largest overshoot and takes longest to settle near one.
The \(2\times2\) curves begin above one and settle near it sooner.
Substituting sample proportions changes the sensitivities used to
estimate \(\tau^2\). Around the overshoot, cells observed only once
contribute strongly to \(\widehat\tau^2\) as well as to \(\Vhat\),
so the averaged sample-based prediction can exceed the observed
sampling variance even while the middle-row population prediction
remains below it. Sample substitution can therefore reverse the
direction of the prediction error. Both populations show this
progression, but for the three larger table sizes the initial
underprediction is stronger in \(Q\), and the crossing above one
occurs later than in \(P\). The subsequent peaks are higher in \(P\)
than in \(Q\).

\subsection{How moment errors affect degrees of freedom}

We now examine how the moment errors in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}
affect the component degrees of freedom for \(P\) and \(Q\), using the
three quantities and inputs distinguished in Table~\ref{tab:component-df-roles}. At each setting, the observed
mean and sampling variance of \(\Vhat(P)\) across 100,000 repetitions
give the moment target \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\) in
Equation~\eqref{eq:design-moment-target}. The population first-order value
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\) instead uses \(V(P)\) and \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\),
while the sample-based comparison calculates \(\widehat\nu_P\) from
each sampled table before averaging these estimates.


Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df} uses the different-skew populations
at common MI 0.02 nats, with margins \(m_k(0.7)\) for \(P\) and
\(m_k(0.8)\) for \(Q\). Sample size increases along the horizontal axis,
and the lines represent table sizes. The top row divides
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\) by \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\); the bottom row divides
\(\E(\widehat\nu_P)\) by the same target, and likewise for \(Q\).
On the shared vertical scale, one indicates agreement, while values below
or above one indicate fewer or more degrees of freedom than the target. A bottom-row value is shown only when all 100,000 component
estimates are positive and finite. Shading in the top row gives approximate
95\% Monte Carlo intervals.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_component_df_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Component degrees-of-freedom approximations relative to their moment targets.}
\label{fig:redesign-component-df}
\end{figure}

In the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df}, the first-order
values remain below the moment targets over most of the sample-size range.
The \(2\times2\) curves approach one first, followed by \(4\times4\);
the \(8\times8\) and \(16\times16\) curves remain lower for longer.
This pattern arises because the two moment errors enter different parts
of the degrees-of-freedom calculation. Replacing the enlarged sampling
mean of \(\Vhat(P)\) by the smaller \(V(P)\) lowers the squared numerator.
A variance prediction below the observed sampling variance lowers the
denominator and raises the resulting degrees of freedom; a prediction
above the observed variance lowers them. Over most of the range, the
reduction from the squared mean outweighs the upward effect of an
underestimated denominator. The larger tables have greater mean errors in
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}, so their first-order degrees of freedom
remain farther below the target. As the moment approximations improve,
both effects diminish and the ratios approach one. Although the same table-size ordering appears in \(P\) and \(Q\),
the larger-table ratios are generally closer to one in \(Q\), whose
smaller normalised mean
overshoot produces a smaller downward error in the squared numerator.

In the bottom row, the \(16\times16\) curves first appear above the target,
fall below it, and then recover towards one. The \(4\times4\) and
\(8\times8\) curves have smaller excursions, while the \(2\times2\)
curves are already close to one when they become visible. This change
from the top row occurs because each sampled table supplies both the
squared numerator \(\Vhat(P)^2\) and the variance prediction
\(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\). At the first shown \(16\times16\) points, the variance prediction is
too small on average, tending to raise the estimated degrees of freedom.
As the prediction subsequently becomes too large, it tends to lower
the estimated degrees of freedom before recovering towards the observed
sampling variance. The two inputs come from the same table, so their joint variation also
affects the mean degrees of freedom when we average the sampled ratios
rather than insert average inputs into a single ratio.
With larger samples, both inputs become more stable and the mean estimated
degrees of freedom approach the target. They can recover sooner than the
population first-order values because their numerators use the sampled
\(\Vhat\), rather than the smaller fixed \(V\). Both populations show these patterns, but for the three larger table
sizes the curves first become
visible later in \(Q\) because the requirement of complete component
validity is met at a larger sample size. Missing earlier points indicate
failed component calculations in some repetitions, rather than zero degrees
of freedom.

A numerical example shows why a fairly accurate variance prediction can
still give degrees of freedom below the target. For different-skew
\(4\times4\) population \(P\) at \(n=1{,}000\) and MI 0.02, the
sampling mean of \(\Vhat(P)\) is about 19\% above \(V(P)\), whereas
\(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) is only about 3\% below the observed sampling variance.
The first-order calculation squares \(V(P)\), whereas the moment target
squares the larger \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\), so the mean error's downward
effect on the numerator is magnified. The slightly underestimated variance denominator
offsets only part of this effect, leaving first-order degrees
of freedom of 15.30 compared with a moment target of 21.03.
Matching these moments chooses
the degrees of freedom of the scaled chi-squared approximation; it does
not guarantee an exact Student \(t\) distribution for the test statistic.

\subsection{Behaviour near independence}

Near independence, the pointwise-MI weights in the linear sampling error
become small, so products of cell-probability errors can contribute
substantially to sampling variation. These quadratic terms allow sampled
tables to show apparent association even when population MI is almost
zero. The near-independence calculation in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} retains them to predict the mean
and sampling variance of \(\Vhat\) and the mean estimated component
degrees of freedom.

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4} holds the sample size at \(n=1{,}000\) and
the table size at \(4\times4\), and increases the common population MI
along the horizontal axis. The margins are \(m_k(0.7)\) for \(P\) and
\(m_k(0.8)\) for \(Q\). The top row multiplies the observed sampling
mean of \(\Vhat\) by \(n\); the middle row multiplies its observed
sampling variance by \(n^2\). Their dashed near-independence predictions
use the same scalings and depend on the common table size, sample size
and MI, so they are identical in the two columns. The bottom row compares
the mean estimated degrees of freedom, its near-independence prediction,
the sampling-moment target and the population first-order value. Across the simulation repetitions, we calculate the observed means and
variances and average the degrees of freedom estimated separately from
each sampled table.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=.78\textheight,keepaspectratio]{focused_redesign_near_independence_different_skew_1000.pdf}
\caption{Component moments and degrees of freedom near independence.}
\label{fig:redesign-a4}
\end{figure}

In the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4}, the observed scaled mean
and its near-independence prediction nearly coincide at the smallest MI
values, then both rise as association strengthens, with the observed mean
eventually exceeding the prediction. The positive starting level occurs because
sampling noise produces positive plug-in MI and \(\Vhat\) even when
population association is almost absent. The local calculation includes
this noise contribution through \(\Vhat\approx2\Ihat\). As MI increases,
the cell log ratios move farther from their values at independence, and
the omitted terms in the local expansion become more important. Checks
on the sampled tables show that \(2\Ihat\) then increasingly understates
\(\Vhat\). The observed means also separate between the columns, with
\(Q\) higher at the larger MI values. The identical local predictions
cannot capture this margin dependence because they retain only the
common table size, sample size and MI.

In the middle row, the observed scaled sampling variance also starts
close to the near-independence prediction, but their separation grows
more strongly as MI increases. Checks on the same \(4\times4\),
different-skew MI sweep at \(n=1{,}000\) trace this underprediction
through the two steps of the local calculation. The calculation first
represents \(\Vhat\) by \(2\Ihat\), then predicts the sampling variance
of that quantity using a noncentral chi-squared distribution. At stronger association,
\(2\Ihat\) varies less between samples than \(\Vhat\), while the local
distribution also understates the observed variation in \(2\Ihat\)
itself. Both errors therefore lower the predicted sampling variance
relative to the observed \(\Var\{\Vhat\}\).
The gap is larger than in the mean row in both columns, and the observed
sampling variance is greater in \(Q\) than in \(P\) at the larger MI
values.
Thus the different margins affect sampling variation beyond the terms
retained by the shared local prediction.

In the bottom row, the mean estimated degrees of freedom remain several
units at the smallest MI values, around half the sampling-moment target,
while the population first-order value approaches zero. This separation
arises because the sample-based calculation retains apparent association
from sampling noise. Substituting \(\Vhat\approx2\Ihat\) and
\(\widehat\tau^2\approx4\Vhat\) into its formula gives
\(\widehat\nu_P\approx n_P\Ihat(P)\), whose average stays positive at
fixed sample size even when \(I(P)\) is almost zero. The population
first-order value instead approaches \(n_PI(P)\), which becomes very
small. The moment target has a shallow initial dip because its observed
sampling variance initially grows faster in proportion than its squared
sampling mean. Their relative growth later reverses and the target rises,
with the mean estimated degrees of freedom approaching it. At stronger
association, the dashed prediction rises above the mean estimated degrees
of freedom, while the population first-order curve remains below the
moment target. Here both local substitutions enter a ratio, with
\(\Vhat\) squared in the numerator and \(\widehat\tau^2\) in the denominator, so their combined error gives an upward error in the
predicted mean, despite the downward moment errors in the upper rows. Both
populations show this progression, but \(Q\) has lower absolute
component degrees of freedom over the displayed range.

The population and sample-based predictions for the sampling variance of
\(\Vhat\) also separate near independence. At the smallest MI target,
0.0001, the population first-order predictions are only about
5\% of the observed sampling variances, while the sample-based predictions
average roughly twice those variances. The local relation
\(\widehat\tau^2\approx4\Vhat\) carries sampling noise into the estimated
variance prediction, explaining its upward error in this range
(Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}). At MI 0.02, the population
first-order variance predictions are much more accurate, although the
sample-based ratios remain above one. The population calculation improves
as the linear sampling term becomes more informative, while the local
near-independence calculation loses accuracy as association grows. At MI
0.05, the mean and both variance predictions are close to their observed
sampling moments, and the mean estimated
degrees of freedom are closer to the moment target than the population
first-order values. The latter still use \(V\) in place of the enlarged
sampling mean. Across these MI values, \(Q\) has lower absolute component
degrees of freedom: its sampling
variance is greater relative to its squared sampling mean, giving a lower
moment target.

\section{RQ2: False-positive rates under equal MI}

Under \(I(P)=I(Q)\), every rejection is a false positive. Both tests use
\(T=\Deltahat/\SEhat\), but Expanded Welch replaces Wald's standard normal
cutoff with a larger cutoff from the Student \(t\) distribution, reducing
rejection at the common nominal p-value threshold. We
therefore assess whether the reduction moves the rate towards the nominal
5\% level, and use the shared estimates to explain when it helps or
increases conservatism.

\subsection{Overall false-positive patterns}
\label{sec:null-rejection-patterns}

120 settings at common MI 0.02 nats. A setting is classified as improved
when Expanded Welch's false-positive rate is closer to 5\% than Wald's
by more than \RedesignGridTolerance{} percentage points, and worsened
when it is farther away by more than \RedesignGridTolerance{} percentage
points. Differences within this tolerance are grouped separately. The
rows distinguish settings where Wald starts above or below the target,

In the \(2\times2\) panel of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-null-uniform}, both
rates fall into a deep trough and then recover towards 5\%, with Expanded
Welch more conservative over most of the range. These uniform-margin
settings use identical population tables, \(P=Q\), and equal sample
sizes, so the expected MI
estimation biases cancel in the difference. The trough therefore requires
an explanation through the standard error and the distribution of \(T\).
Because sampled tables with larger absolute MI differences also tend to
give larger standard errors, division by those denominators reduces the
extreme values of \(T\). This reduction in extreme statistics contributes to the low rejection
rate, which Expanded Welch's larger cutoff lowers further. The dependence weakens at larger sample sizes, allowing both rates to
recover, as shown by the diagnostics in
Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy}.

In the \(4\times4\) panel, Wald initially rejects too often while Expanded
Welch is close to the target, before both rates fall below 5\% and then
recover. This reversal accompanies the average \(\SEhat^2\) moving from below
the observed variance of \(\Deltahat\) at small sample sizes to above
it over the conservative range. For a given difference estimate, a smaller
standard error increases \(|T|\), while a larger standard error decreases
it. The same increase in rejection cutoff therefore helps in the first
range and deepens conservatism in the second. With larger samples, the
squared standard error becomes more accurate and the methods approach 5\%.

In the \(8\times8\) panel, an early shoulder of excessive rejection is
followed by a later conservative trough and recovery. The shared squared
standard error initially underestimates the difference's sampling variance,
then overestimates it across the trough. The larger alphabet spreads
probability over more combinations, so the sampled tables require more
observations before these variance errors diminish. Expanded Welch reduces
the early excess but also lowers the conservative part of the curve. As in
\(4\times4\), its effect on calibration changes along the horizontal axis.

The \(16\times16\) panel has an additional rise between the early decline
and the later conservative trough. The average variance error also changes
direction more than once as the sampled tables fill: underestimation,
closer agreement, renewed underestimation and then overestimation accompany
the corresponding changes in rejection. Because the populations are identical, expected difference bias still
cancels, so the nonmonotonic curve reflects errors in the standard error
and the tails of \(T\), rather than a changing population difference. Expanded Welch lowers rejection throughout, but the methods become close
well before both rates settle near 5\%, so their agreement alone does not
establish accurate false-positive rates.

Under different-skew margins, \(I(P)=I(Q)\) in
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-null-different}, but the population tables differ
and their expected MI estimation biases can therefore differ too.
The resulting bias in \(\Deltahat\) can shift the shared statistic away
from zero as well as changing its spread.

\caption{False-positive rates under different-skew margins.}

In the different-skew \(2\times2\) panel, Wald remains below 5\%
throughout the grid, so Expanded Welch's reduction mainly increases
conservatism. Both methods again have a trough and recovery, retaining
the small-table pattern seen under uniform margins despite the changed
margins. As the shared estimates improve, both rates recover towards 5\%, while
the increasing degrees of freedom bring Expanded Welch's cutoff closer
to Wald's.

In \(4\times4\), Wald starts above the target and then falls below it,
while Expanded Welch lowers both regions. The average squared standard error
moves from underestimating to overestimating the observed difference
variance, helping explain the fall into the trough. As that overestimation
diminishes, both rates rise towards 5\%. At \(n=10\), the first Expanded Welch rate is close to 5\% even though
only about three quarters of calculations are valid. Its
apparent closeness at that point therefore includes repetitions recorded
as non-rejections because the calculation failed.

The \(8\times8\) panel begins with much greater over-rejection than its
uniform counterpart. Expanded Welch initially rises as its validity and
cutoff change, then both methods fall below 5\% before recovering.
The diagnostics show that positive bias moves the centre of
\(\Deltahat\) away from zero, while overestimation of \(\SEhat^2\)
in the intermediate range tends to reduce \(|T|\). At \(n=100\), both effects are present even though Expanded
Welch brings the rate closer to 5\%. As the bias diminishes, the remaining
variance overestimation accompanies the conservative trough. The larger
cutoff deepens that trough before the shared estimates recover. A rate
close to the target at an intermediate point can therefore reflect the
combined effects of errors rather than accurate estimation of each input.

In the \(16\times16\) panel, both methods severely over-reject across
several intermediate sample sizes. Here the bias in \(\Deltahat\) grows relative to its sampling SD,
shifting the statistic away from zero (see
Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy} for the comparison with the
unbiased same-skew case). The squared standard error is also too large
on average over part of this range, but that compensating effect does
not remove the large displacement. Expanded Welch changes the cutoff
without changing the displaced difference estimate, so its reduction in
rejection leaves substantial over-rejection. With larger samples, bias diminishes and both rates fall towards 5\%.

The validity strips separate early calculation failures from these later
patterns. Under uniform margins, appreciable losses are concentrated in the smallest
\(2\times2\) samples, whereas under different skew several table sizes
lose outputs at the smallest sample sizes. Across the full
120-setting grid, the minimum valid fractions are 0.958 for Normal Wald
and 0.631 for Expanded Welch. The conservative troughs in \(4\times4\)
and larger tables, and the different-skew \(16\times16\) plateau,
persist after validity is complete, so those patterns require explanations
through the estimates and statistic.

At the largest sample size in both figures, both tests' rates are close
to 5\% across the table sizes. More accurate cell frequencies reduce the
bias and variance errors entering the shared statistic, while larger
estimated degrees of freedom bring Expanded Welch's cutoff towards Wald's.
The curves consequently approach one another and the nominal target in
these settings.

\subsection{Bias and standard-error accuracy}
\label{sec:null-estimator-accuracy}

RQ1 examined the moments of \(\Vhat(P)\) and \(\Vhat(Q)\) needed to
choose degrees of freedom. For the shared statistic, we now separately check whether \(\Deltahat\)
is centred on the population difference and whether \(\SEhat^2\)
describes its sampling variance.
At small sample sizes, \(V(P)/n_P\) can itself understate MI sampling
variance because it includes only linear sampling error. Consequently,
\(\Vhat(P)/n_P\) can exceed that population approximation while still
falling below the actual sampling variance of the MI estimate.

Table~\ref{tab:redesign-shared-estimates} calculates both diagnostics
across the evaluation repetitions. ``Bias / SD'' divides
\(\E(\Deltahat)-\Delta\) by the observed sampling SD of \(\Deltahat\),
so it measures displacement relative to the spread of the estimates.
The variance ratio divides the average \(\SEhat^2\) by the observed
\(\Var(\Deltahat)\): values below one indicate underestimation of
sampling variance, and values above one indicate overestimation. The
observed variance is measured about the sampling mean, so this ratio
can be close to one even when the difference is biased.
The table compares the selected null settings A, uniform \(4\times4\) with \(n=20\);
B, different-skew \(8\times8\) with \(n=100\); and C,

The sparse different-skew case shows why changing the cutoff cannot
resolve a displaced difference estimate. At \(16\times16\) and
\(n=1{,}000\), the difference bias is about 0.030 nats, or 2.06 observed
SDs. Under the equal-MI null, its sampling centre
\(\E(\Deltahat)=\Delta+\Bias(\Deltahat)\) becomes
\begin{equation}
 \E(\Deltahat)\approx 0+0.02985=0.02985\text{ nats}.
 \label{eq:redesign-null-bias-shift}
\end{equation}
With an observed SD of approximately 0.01450 nats, this positive shift
places the sampling distribution largely above zero and moves most
rejections into the positive tail despite a zero population difference.
Expanded Welch leaves this centre unchanged, so both tests reject far too often.

The same-skew \(16\times16\) case has almost the same variance ratio
but rejection far below 5\%. Under equal MI, the expected estimation
biases cancel in the difference because the population tables and sample
sizes are identical. Its average
\(\SEhat^2\) exceeds the observed \(\Var(\Deltahat)\), shrinking
\(|T|\) without a large bias shifting the estimates away from zero.
Under different skew, that variance overestimation coexists with the
large positive bias, and rejection instead remains excessive. Thus the
similar variance ratios in Table~\ref{tab:redesign-shared-estimates}
can accompany rates on opposite sides of 5\% in
Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-rates}. At \(n=10{,}000\), both skew
regimes have rejection rates closer to 5\%.

For the identical uniform-margin populations, expected difference bias
cancels, while changes in the average \(\SEhat^2\) below or above the
observed \(\Var(\Deltahat)\) tend to enlarge or reduce \(|T|\), respectively. These changes accompany the repeated
crossings in the \(16\times16\) panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-null-uniform}. The estimates recover at
different rates as sparse tables fill, so increasing sample size need
not improve rejection monotonically.

The \(2\times2\) trough also involves how the numerator and denominator
move together within repetitions. Larger absolute MI differences tend
to occur with larger standard errors, so division suppresses the
extreme values that would otherwise cause rejection. Consequently,
rejection can be far below 5\% even when the average \(\SEhat^2\)
is fairly close to the observed difference variance. Beyond the trough,
this dependence weakens and the tails of \(T\) move closer to the
normal approximation. The Spearman rank correlation between
\(|\Deltahat-\operatorname{mean}(\Deltahat)|\) and \(\SEhat\),
calculated over repetitions with both quantities finite, falls from
0.656 at \(n=50\) to 0.167 at \(n=1{,}000\).
Together these checks explain the broad patterns
without isolating every finite-sample contribution to rejection.

\section{RQ3: Power at a fixed MI difference}
\label{sec:fixed-gap-power-results}

The power study retains the four table sizes, three margin regimes and
ten sample sizes of the broad null comparison, but fixes \(I(P)=0.02\)
and \(I(Q)=0.03\) nats. The true difference is therefore
\(\Delta=-0.01\) nats throughout. Each plotted rate divides valid
rejections by all 100,000 repetitions at that setting, with invalid
calculations counted as non-rejections. Shading gives pointwise 95\%
intervals, and the lower strips show the fraction of valid results.

Both tests use their usual p-value threshold of 0.05. Expanded Welch's
larger cutoff makes its rejections a subset of Wald's, so the comparison
quantifies how much detection probability is lost. These rates are read
alongside the false-positive results in Section~\ref{sec:null-rejection-patterns}:
high rejection under an alternative can coexist with excessive rejection
under equal MI. Because the tests are two-sided, power also includes
rejections whose estimated MI difference has the opposite sign to the
population difference.

\subsection{Uniform margins}

Figure~\ref{fig:fixed-gap-power-uniform} uses uniform row and column
margins in both populations. Each panel holds the population tables and
MI difference fixed while increasing the common sample size along the
logarithmic horizontal axis. The two lines compare Normal Wald and
Expanded Welch at the same sampled table pairs.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fixed_gap_power_uniform.pdf}
\caption{Power at a fixed MI difference under uniform margins.}
\label{fig:fixed-gap-power-uniform}
\end{figure}

All four panels show low detection over part of the small-sample range,
followed by a sustained rise with more observations. Larger tables
require more observations before power becomes high. For example, at
\(n=10{,}000\), Expanded Welch detects the difference in 89.44\% of
\(2\times2\) repetitions and 71.12\% of \(16\times16\) repetitions.
This ordering reflects both the sparse-table estimation errors discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy} and the population variances:
in this copying family, increasing table size also increases \(V(P)\)
and \(V(Q)\). Their contributions \(V(P)/n+V(Q)/n\) therefore give a
larger first-order sampling variance for the same MI gap and sample size.
As the variance decreases with more observations, the estimated
difference becomes more consistently separated from zero and power rises.

Expanded Welch remains below Wald, with the largest separation at the
smallest sample size in the \(16\times16\) panel. Its valid fraction
there is above 99\%, so most of that separation comes from the larger
cutoff rather than failed calculations. The method gap becomes small
in the larger-sample range as increasing degrees of freedom bring the
Student \(t\) distribution close to the standard normal distribution.
The early shoulders and troughs also occur in the null rejection curves,
where standard-error errors and the distribution of \(T\) can produce
excessive or conservative rejection. Consequently, the early high points
are not evidence that fewer observations reliably identify the MI difference.

\subsection{Same-skew margins}

Figure~\ref{fig:fixed-gap-power-same-skew} assigns both populations the
row and column profile \(m_k(0.8)\), retaining the same MI targets and
sample sizes. The margins agree between populations, but their different
MI values require different copying probabilities and hence different
population tables.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fixed_gap_power_same_skew.pdf}
\caption{Power at a fixed MI difference under same-skew margins.}
\label{fig:fixed-gap-power-same-skew}
\end{figure}

The curves again have an early low-power range followed by recovery,
with the trough occurring later for larger tables. Relative to uniform
margins, the uneven frequencies leave more combinations rarely observed,
and the population pointwise-MI variances are also larger. Both features
make the fixed gap harder to detect. In the \(16\times16\) case at
\(n=1{,}000\), the average squared standard error is 1.81 times the
observed variance of the difference, while difference bias is small
relative to the true gap. The oversized denominator suppresses \(|T|\),
contributing to Expanded Welch's power of only 1.64\% at that setting.

Both methods gain power once larger samples improve the cell estimates
and reduce the spread of the MI difference. Expanded Welch reaches
60.99\% in \(16\times16\) at \(n=10{,}000\), compared with 71.12\%
under uniform margins, and exceeds 99.9\% in every same-skew panel at
\(n=50{,}000\). The differences between methods become much smaller
than these differences between table sizes and margin regimes. At the
smallest sample sizes, however, the validity strips show that failed
Expanded Welch calculations also contribute to its lower all-repetition
power; the larger cutoff is not the only source of that loss.

\subsection{Different-skew margins}

Figure~\ref{fig:fixed-gap-power-different-skew} uses \(m_k(0.7)\) for
\(P\) and \(m_k(0.8)\) for \(Q\). The true difference remains
\(-0.01\) nats, but the unequal margins can give the MI estimates
different residual biases, shifting the centre of their difference.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fixed_gap_power_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Power at a fixed MI difference under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:fixed-gap-power-different-skew}
\end{figure}

The \(2\times2\) and \(4\times4\) panels retain the low-power range
and subsequent rise, while the two larger tables have more pronounced
early rejection before falling and recovering. The \(16\times16\)
curve shows why this early rejection needs careful interpretation.
At \(n=1{,}000\), positive difference bias is approximately 0.0307
nats, moving the sampling mean from the true \(-0.01\) to approximately
\(+0.0207\) nats. Expanded Welch rejects in 9.66\% of repetitions,
but more than 99.9\% of those rejections have a positive estimated
difference, opposite to the true direction. The shared biased numerator
therefore produces rejection without reliably identifying which population
has greater MI, as in the displaced null comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy}.

As sample size increases, that positive bias decreases and the sampling
centre passes back towards zero. At \(n=2{,}500\), the remaining positive
bias nearly cancels the negative population difference, leaving the
sampling mean close to zero, and Expanded Welch's power falls to 2.48\%.
With still more observations, the bias diminishes further and the
sampling centre approaches the negative population difference, while
sampling variance decreases. Negative-tail rejection then rises and
power becomes high. Changing the cutoff lowers both parts of this
nonmonotonic curve without removing the biased movement of its centre.

Across all three margin regimes, both methods exceed 99.9\% power at
\(n=50{,}000\). At \(n=10{,}000\), their power differences are below
0.32 percentage points at every table size and margin regime. The fixed-gap
study therefore shows a detection cost from Expanded Welch's larger cutoff,
especially with small samples, alongside shared estimation errors that
can leave both methods with low power or rejection in the wrong direction.
The false-positive findings determine whether that lower rejection
improves calibration in the corresponding null setting.

\section{Application to online shopping data}
\label{sec:shoppers-application}

The Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention dataset records website sessions
and whether each session ends in a purchase \citep{sakar2018}. We compare
month--purchase MI between new and returning visitors, then make the
same comparison for weekend--purchase MI. For each predictor,
\(X\) is month or weekend status and \(Y\) is the purchase outcome,
giving joint distributions \(P\) and \(Q\) of \((X,Y)\) among new
and returning visitors, respectively. Each comparison therefore tests
\(H_0:I(P)=I(Q)\) for that predictor and purchase outcome.

The dataset contains 12,330 sessions, each belonging to a different user
according to the source documentation \citep{sakar2018}. We retain all
1,694 sessions labelled \texttt{New\_Visitor} and 10,551 labelled
\texttt{Returning\_Visitor}, excluding the 85 \texttt{Other} sessions.
The outcome is the original Boolean \texttt{Revenue} variable, which is
true when a purchase occurs and false otherwise. We treat the two visitor
groups as independent samples and the sessions within each group as
independent observations.

\begingroup
\setlength{\intextsep}{4pt}
\setlength{\abovecaptionskip}{4pt}
January and April have no recorded sessions, so each month table has
\(10\times2\) cells, with rows for the ten recorded months and columns
for no purchase or purchase. All ten months occur in both visitor groups,
although the new-visitor February row contains only one session and no
purchase. Both groups use the same categories, retaining zero cells
without smoothing. Replacing month with the Boolean \texttt{Weekend}

These two shopping comparisons were selected from six comparisons across

Both methods reject equal month--purchase MI because their p-values

sampling-moment target or measure false-positive rates.

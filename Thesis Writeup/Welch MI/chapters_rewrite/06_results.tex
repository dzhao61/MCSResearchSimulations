points to its right measure detection when the population MIs differ.
The horizontal coordinate is fixed by the population construction, not
estimated from the random samples. Blue solid circles show Normal Wald and
magenta dashed squares show Expanded Welch. The dotted line marks the

Where a zoom is shown, the upper panels use a 0--1 rejection axis and the

The comparisons proceed from null calibration to detection, sparse-sample

\section{Calibration across table size and margins}

The first comparison keeps both populations uniform in a \(2\times2\)
table. Figure~\ref{fig:main-2x2-uniform} shows three equal sample sizes
and asks whether the lower Expanded Welch rejection rate is closer to
the target 0.05 at each size.

\mainlandscapefigure{2x2}{\(2\times2\)}{uniform}{both \(P\) and \(Q\) have uniform row and column margins \((1/2,1/2)\)}{uniform}{At zero difference, both methods reject below 0.05 at \(n=100\) and 1,000; Expanded Welch is lower still. At \(n=10\), Wald is slightly above 0.05 and Expanded Welch below it. Validity is high in all three panels. Rejection grows most clearly with MI difference at \(n=1{,}000\), where the two curves are close. At the smaller sizes, low rejection should not be mistaken for better detection.}
\FloatBarrier

Figure~\ref{fig:main-8x8-uniform} asks whether the same comparison
changes in a larger, still-uniform \(8\times8\) table.

\mainlandscapefigure{8x8}{\(8\times8\)}{uniform}{both \(P\) and \(Q\) have uniform row and column margins \((1/8,\ldots,1/8)\)}{uniform}{At \(n=10\) and 100, the zero-difference rates exceed 0.05 for both methods; Expanded Welch reduces the excess but does not always reach the target. These plotted calculations are valid, so the difference is not caused by missing outputs. At \(n=100\), rejection changes little over the displayed alternatives; at \(n=1{,}000\), it grows strongly. In the \(8\times8\) row, Expanded Welch moves Wald's above-target
rate closer to 0.05. In the \(2\times2\) row, it moves an already-low
rate farther below the target. Invalid outputs cannot explain either
difference. The reason for this one-sided trade-off is built into the methods:
on the same valid table pair, Expanded Welch uses the same statistic
as Wald but evaluates it against a Student \(t\) distribution, which gives
a no-smaller two-sided p-value.
It can lower rejection, not raise it. This makes detection under
unequal MI the next question.

\section{Detection as the true MI difference grows}

The main grid shows only differences up to 0.02 nats. Figure~
\ref{fig:broad-focus} extends a uniform \(2\times2\) comparison
to 0.2 nats to check whether the low rejection over small effects
persists as the true MI difference grows.

Both populations have row and column margins \((0.5,0.5)\), use the first
\(2\times2\) change block, and have sample sizes \(n_P=n_Q=100\).
The population MI values are \(I(P)=0.02\) and
\(I(Q)=0.02+|\Delta|\). The difference grid is
\(|\Delta|=0,0.001,0.002,0.005,0.01,0.02,0.05,0.1,0.2\) nats.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.70\textwidth]{broad_2x2_uniform.pdf}
\caption[Wider MI differences, uniform \(2\times2\)]{Rejection rates over
wider MI differences for uniform \(2\times2\) tables.}
\label{fig:broad-focus}
\end{figure}

At zero difference, both methods are below the 0.05
line; Expanded Welch is lower, and validity is high. Rejection then rises markedly as the true MI
difference reaches the larger targets: by 0.2 nats both methods reject more
than 90\% of samples. Expanded Welch remains below Wald at intermediate
differences, but the gap narrows when detection is already high. This wider
axis shows that the low rejection over the main grid's small differences is
not a permanent ceiling on detection.

For \(I(P)=0.0001,0.001,0.02\), the zero-difference Wald rates are

\FloatBarrier

The local calculation in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} explains why
its degrees of freedom can become small near independence: the
cutoff from the Student \(t\) distribution becomes more demanding where the
first-order MI approximation is also less reliable. Alternative rejection is
measured at the common nominal 5\%
threshold. Because the methods have different null rejection rates,
the curves should not be read as a comparison at matched achieved
false-positive rates. They show the practical trade-off under the
procedures as implemented: in these examples, the extra caution
reduces detection as well as false positives.

\section{Validity under sparse sampling}

An observed table can omit many cells even when every population
cell has positive probability. Figure~\ref{fig:extreme-focus}
pushes the margins toward one dominant category while holding
\(n_P=n_Q=100\). It asks whether near-zero rejection in very sparse
tables means good calibration or simply a failure to calculate a test.

From left to right, the dominant row and column probabilities for
\((P,Q)\) are \((0.9,0.95)\), \((0.99,0.995)\), and
\((0.999,0.9995)\). Each remaining margin entry divides the leftover
probability equally between the two other categories. Both populations use
the first \(2\times2\) change block, with \(I(P)=0.00001\) and
\(I(Q)=I(P)+|\Delta|\). The difference grid is
\(|\Delta|=0,0.00001,0.00005,0.0001,0.0002\) nats.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{extreme_3x3.pdf}
\caption[Extreme-skew \(3\times3\) tables]{Rejection rates for
\(3\times3\) tables with increasingly dominant categories. The lower
panels enlarge the 0--0.15 range.}
\label{fig:extreme-focus}
\end{figure}

For dominant margins 0.9 and 0.95, the null rates are
0.0701 for Wald and 0.0088 for Expanded Welch: the adjustment moves an
excessive rate past 0.05. At the most extreme margins, 0.999 and 0.9995,
both rates are zero, but only 0.0118 of Wald and 0.00005 of Expanded Welch
null calculations are valid. Rejection does not meaningfully grow over the
displayed alternatives there. The hollow markers show why near-zero
unconditional rejection cannot be read as good calibration in sparse data.

\FloatBarrier

Low validity explains much of the zero rejection in the most extreme
panel. There is a second, distinct small-sample effect: Expanded Welch
rejects in none of the \(\MainFiveNullCount\) main-grid null
regimes with \(n=5\), including uniform \(3\times3\), \(5\times5\)
and \(8\times8\) regimes where most calculations are valid. Thus its
zero rejection cannot always be explained by missing outputs. In
one \(8\times8\) different-skew alternative at \(n=5\), Wald
rejects \(\EightSparseFiveAlternativeWaldRate\) and Expanded Welch
rejects \(\EightSparseFiveAlternativeExpandedRate\), while their
valid-result rates are \(\EightSparseFiveAlternativeWaldValid\)
and \(\EightSparseFiveAlternativeExpandedValid\). Rejection and
validity must be reported together.

Wald needs a positive usable total estimated variance. The expanded
implementation additionally requires positive finite component
sensitivities and degrees of freedom. Some count tables therefore
produce a Wald p-value but no Expanded Welch p-value. The primary
rate counts such failures as non-rejections for the procedure; they
are not evidence that the population MIs are equal. The exact
numerical validity criteria are given in Appendix~\ref{sec:validity-rules}.

\section{Checks on population and sampling choices}

The preceding figures isolate the central trade-off. Additional
matched checks ask whether it depends on where the association sits,
how the populations are constructed, the sample allocation or the
table shape. Their plots and exact specifications are collected in
Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}. Moving association between
early, rare and spread cells changes the underlying table even when
margins and MI are fixed (Figure~\ref{fig:patterns-focus}). An
ordinal log-linear construction also changes rates under matched
margins and MI (Figure~\ref{fig:construction-focus}).
Reversing which population has the larger sample or higher MI changes
the alternative curves (Figures~\ref{fig:imbalance-q} and
\ref{fig:imbalance-p}). Rectangular tables show that the conservative
pattern is not exclusive to square shapes
(Figure~\ref{fig:rectangular-focus}). These checks limit how far any
single plotted example can be generalised; none removes the need to
judge null calibration and validity for the particular population
pair.

The exact-independence check in
Figure~\ref{fig:independence-focus} is kept with those supporting
plots because it addresses a different mathematical boundary:
the first-order MI variance becomes zero at independence.
Appendix~\ref{app:independence-boundary} explains why a one-table
independence test has a different reference distribution.

\section{Limits of changing the reference distribution}
\label{sec:mechanism-results}

Both methods use the same estimated MI difference and the same
plug-in standard error. Changing the reference distribution leaves
errors in either quantity untouched. Even when the population MIs
are equal, the two estimates can retain different small-sample biases
if \(P\) and \(Q\) have different margins. Their leading equal-sample
corrections cancel, but the remaining biases need not; the estimated
difference can therefore be shifted away from zero. The plots do not
show how much this contributes to excess rejection.

A supplementary check illustrates the shared-standard-error problem.
For a \(3\times3\) different-skew null with \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\) and
\(n=100\), Wald rejects 0.0166 of independently simulated table pairs.
Keeping the same estimated MI differences but dividing each by one
fixed standard deviation estimated in a separate pilot simulation
raises rejection to 0.0551. The cutoff from the normal distribution has not changed.
In this setting, the average squared plug-in standard error is about
17\% larger than the measured variance of the MI difference. A larger
standard error tends to make the absolute test statistic smaller and rejection
less likely.

The fixed-denominator check cannot isolate that size effect: it also
removes the way the estimated difference and standard error move together
between samples. In the original Wald calculation, both come from the same
observed tables.
Appendix~\ref{sec:mi-variance-covariance} examines their first-order
relationship. These calculations are diagnostics, not replacement tests.
They show why changing only to a Student \(t\) distribution cannot correct
every error in the statistic shared by Wald and Expanded Welch.

\section{Larger samples and computational cost}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-focus} keeps each population pair fixed
under equal MI and increases the sample size. This tests whether the
poor null rates are temporary small-sample effects for the chosen
populations, and whether the answer changes with their common MI.

The rows compare \(2\times2\) and \(8\times8\) tables, and the columns use
common population MI values of 0.0001 and 0.02 nats. For each shape,
\(P\)'s row and column margins are \(m_k(0.7)\) and \(Q\)'s are
\(m_k(0.8)\), with \(k=2\) or 8. Both populations use the first
\(2\times2\) change block. The equal sample sizes are
\(n_P=n_Q=1{,}000,2{,}500,10{,}000,50{,}000\), plotted on a labelled
logarithmic scale. Every point has equal population MI.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{convergence.pdf}
\caption[Large-sample null behaviour]{False-positive rates as sample size
increases for \(2\times2\) and \(8\times8\) tables. The vertical axis
ranges from 0 to 0.10.}
\label{fig:convergence-focus}
\end{figure}

All displayed calculations are valid. In the
displayed \(2\times2\) and \(8\times8\) settings, rates move toward 0.05
more clearly when common MI is 0.02 than when it is 0.0001. Across all 12
first-block shape--margin combinations at \(n=50{,}000\), the 0.02-MI
null rates range from 0.0470 to 0.0527 for Wald and 0.0466 to 0.0526 for
Expanded Welch. At MI 0.0001, the ranges remain 0.0089--0.0280 and
0.0076--0.0138. Convergence can therefore be slow near independence.

\FloatBarrier

These fixed populations do not establish a universal sample-size
threshold for other tables. A separate consideration is computation.
A full Expanded Welch method call
has the same \(O(rc)\) asymptotic complexity as Wald, but its extra
sensitivity and degree-of-freedom calculations take longer in the
measured implementation. Table~\ref{tab:runtime-by-shape} shows
representative timings from the 60 timing regimes.

These examples use different-skew null populations with
\(n_P=n_Q=100\) and \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\). Entries give the median and
interquartile range, in microseconds, over five passes on 200 pre-generated table pairs
per regime. The inputs are already available when timing begins.

Across the 60 timing regimes, the median call time is about
85 microseconds for Wald and 150 for Expanded Welch, a ratio of
about 1.75. These timings measure calls after tables are available;
they exclude population construction and sampling. The extra cost
is modest beside the statistical issue. Expanded Welch is useful
where Wald is demonstrably too liberal and Expanded Welch remains
valid, but the figures do not support replacing Wald across all
regimes. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} states the contribution and
limits of that conclusion.

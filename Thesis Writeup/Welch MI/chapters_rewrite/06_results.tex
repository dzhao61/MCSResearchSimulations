\section{Accuracy of the variance approximations}

\subsection{Mean and sampling variance}

The population first-order approximations can predict the sampling variance of
\(\Vhat\) reasonably accurately while underestimating its sampling mean.
At \(n=1{,}000\), the sampling-variance prediction divided by the
observed variance ranges from 0.710 to 0.996 across all table sizes and
margin regimes. Over the same grid, the observed mean divided by \(V\)
ranges from 1.023 to 4.571. The mean discrepancy is particularly large
for the larger tables.

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments} shows the different-skew populations
at common MI 0.02 nats. The top row compares the mean of \(\Vhat\)
with \(V\). In the middle and bottom rows, solid curves use the
population first-order predictions. Dashed curves use the mean plug-in
sampling-variance prediction in the middle row and the mean estimated
component degrees of freedom in the bottom row. The latter are shown
only when all component estimates are positive and finite. A ratio of
one indicates agreement; shading gives approximate 95\% Monte Carlo
intervals around the solid curves.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=.78\textheight,keepaspectratio]{focused_redesign_moments_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Component approximation ratios under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:redesign-moments}
\end{figure}

Increasing sample size improves both approximations. At \(n=50{,}000\),
the sampling-variance ratios across the same broad grid lie between
0.974 and 1.009, while mean ratios range from 1.000 to 1.091. The
experimental supplement provides the companion uniform and same-skew plots.

The remaining mean error lowers the population first-order degrees of
freedom relative to the sampling-moment target. For different-skew
\(4\times4\) populations at \(n=1{,}000\) and MI 0.02, the
sampling-variance ratios are 0.970 for \(P\) and 0.967 for \(Q\).
The observed means are \(1.190V(P)\) and \(1.174V(Q)\). The first-order
calculation substitutes \(V(P)\) for the larger sampling mean
\(\E\Vhat(P)\), and likewise for \(Q\). Because the moment-matching
formula squares the mean, this substitution lowers the degrees of freedom
even though the sampling-variance prediction is close. The resulting
first-order values, 15.30 and 13.61, fall below the moment targets,
21.03 and 18.14. Table~\ref{tab:variance-stability} gives the component
values; Appendix~\ref{sec:moment-reference-details} gives the algebra.

Estimating the component degrees of freedom from each sample can produce
a further shortfall from the moment target. At setting A (uniform
\(4\times4\), \(n_P=n_Q=20\)), the mean for the \(P\) component is
about 6.52 against a moment target of 14.81, or 44\% of the target.
In the \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) same- and different-skew cases,
the corresponding ratios range from 55\% to 60\%. All 100,000 component
estimates are included in these comparisons. At C (different-skew
\(4\times4\), \(n_P=n_Q=10{,}000\)), the ratios are about 99\%.
The bottom-row dashed curves show this additional discrepancy when
population probabilities in the first-order formula are replaced by
sample proportions. The moment target itself does not guarantee an
exact Student \(t\) distribution for the test statistic.

\subsection{Behaviour near independence}

Near independence, quadratic sampling terms contribute substantially to
\(\Vhat\). For \(4\times4\) different-skew populations at common MI
0.0001 and \(n=1{,}000\), the local calculation in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} predicts \(n\E\Vhat=9.2\)
for each population; the observed value for \(P\) is 9.158. The mean
estimated component degrees of freedom for \(P\) are 4.86, close to the local
prediction 4.6, while the sampling-moment target is about nine. Thus the
local calculation describes the mean estimated degrees of freedom
accurately even though that mean remains below the moment target.

The population and sample-based first-order sampling-variance predictions
have errors in opposite directions near independence. At the smallest MI target in
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4}, the population sampling-variance prediction
is only about 5\% of the observed value. The mean plug-in prediction is
1.97 times the observed value for \(P\) and 2.14 times for \(Q\).
The quadratic sampling contribution remains substantial, while the
population first-order degrees of freedom are below 0.1. The shaded band
shows the fifth--95th percentile range of estimated component degrees of
freedom, with their mean and median shown separately.

At MI 0.02, the population sampling-variance approximation is much more
accurate, while the mean plug-in variance predictions remain about
7--8\% above the observed values. The remaining mean error still lowers
the first-order degrees of freedom relative to the moment target.
Appendix~\ref{sec:component-diagnostics} reports the mean error across
MI and margins.

\section{False positives when MI is equal}

Expanded Welch improves false-positive rates in selected settings where
Normal Wald rejects too often. In others, it further lowers already
conservative rates. Across the 120 settings at common MI 0.02 nats,
Expanded Welch moves rejection closer to 5\% in \RedesignToward{}
settings and farther away in \RedesignAway{}, using changes in distance
greater than \RedesignGridTolerance{} percentage points. All
\RedesignToward{} improvements occur with Wald above 5\%;
\RedesignAwayBelow{} of the \RedesignAway{} deteriorations occur with
Wald below 5\%. The remaining \RedesignWithin{} settings fall within
this tolerance.

Figures~\ref{fig:redesign-null-uniform} and
\ref{fig:redesign-null-different} show uniform and different-skew margins,
with the fixed moment reference as a diagnostic. The lower panels give
the fraction of valid outputs for Normal Wald and Expanded Welch. The
fixed moment reference shares Wald's validity in these settings. Validity
is lowest in the smallest samples: across the 120 settings, the minimum
valid fractions are 0.958 for Normal Wald and the fixed reference, and
0.631 for Expanded Welch. The supplement reports conditional rates and
included counts separately.

\subsection{Small-sample improvements and sparse-table failures}

The selected small-sample improvements occur with different estimation
errors. At the uniform \(4\times4\) setting A, Expanded Welch gives
4.90\% rejection compared with 8.35\% for Wald. At the \(8\times8\)
different-skew setting B, the rates are 5.52\% and 7.47\%, respectively.
Expanded Welch is closer to 5\% at B but still rejects too often.
Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-rates} compares these settings with the
larger-sample and sparse-table cases. Both tests and the fixed moment
reference are valid on every sampled pair in all seven settings.

Both tests over-reject at stronger common MI: in A at
\RedesignUpperAMI{} nats, Wald rejects \RedesignUpperAWaldRate{} and
Expanded Welch rejects \RedesignUpperAExpandedRate{} of pairs.
In B at \RedesignUpperBMI{} nats, the rates are
\RedesignUpperBWaldRate{} and \RedesignUpperBExpandedRate{}, respectively.

At A, difference bias is negligible, but the mean squared standard error
is only 0.827 of the observed difference variance, so the standard error
understates sampling variation. At B, difference bias is positive and
the variance is overestimated. Expanded Welch lowers false positives in
both cases, although the shared estimates have different errors. At
the larger-sample setting C, bias is negligible and the variance ratio is
1.010. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-diagnostics} gives the numerical
diagnostics and degrees-of-freedom comparisons in the appendix.

Changing the degrees of freedom does not resolve the sparse-table
failure. At \(16\times16\), different skew and \(n=1{,}000\), the
difference bias is about 0.030 nats, or 2.06 observed SDs. Rejection ranges
from 24.8\% to 26.0\% across both tests and the fixed moment reference;
the reference rate is 25.3\% despite its independently fitted moments.
Under the equal-MI null, the estimated difference is centred at
\begin{equation}
 \E(\Deltahat)\approx 0+0.02985=0.02985\text{ nats}.
 \label{eq:redesign-null-bias-shift}
\end{equation}
This shift is large relative to the observed SD of approximately 0.01450
nats. It moves most rejections into the positive tail even though the
population difference is zero.

Under equal MI, the same-skew populations have identical tables. With
equal sample sizes, their expected MI estimation biases therefore cancel
in the difference. Difference bias is negligible, but the variance ratio is
1.93; the three rejection rates lie between 0.53\% and 0.60\%.
The matched populations distinguish rejection driven by a large
difference bias from conservative behaviour when that bias cancels.
At \(n=10{,}000\), both skew regimes have much smaller estimation errors
and rates closer to 5\%. The fixed moment reference's different rates
across these populations show that matching denominator moments alone
does not ensure a correctly calibrated Student \(t\) reference for the
shared statistic.

\section{Power at the selected settings}

\subsection{Nominal power}

Expanded Welch has lower nominal power than Normal Wald at the selected
small-sample settings A and B (Table~\ref{tab:redesign-focus-settings}).
At A, two null SDs correspond to about 0.310 nats: Expanded Welch rejects
35.9\% of pairs, compared with 41.0\% for Wald.
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-power} shows all three focus settings with MI
differences measured in fitted null standard deviations. The nominal
comparison includes their different null rejection rates at zero.
Appendix~\ref{sec:calibration-sensitivity-results} gives numerical rates
and paired contrasts.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_power.pdf}
\caption{Nominal power across differences measured in fitted null standard deviations.}
\label{fig:redesign-power}
\end{figure}

As the population MI values move farther apart at B, rejection initially
falls before rising. Expanded Welch's rate falls from 5.52\% under the null to
1.99\% at one null SD, before rising to 33.1\% at four SDs. As
\(I(Q)\) increases, the true signed difference becomes negative,
initially offsetting the positive estimation bias observed under the
null. The estimated difference moves towards zero, reducing \(|T|\)
and rejection before the curve rises. This distortion of the shared
statistic remains when the rejection cutoff is increased.
Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} reports the estimated directions
among rejections in the sparse-table comparisons.

At C, nominal power differences are small. At two null SDs, or about
0.00635 nats, Wald gives 45.2\% and Expanded Welch gives 44.9\%.
Expanded Welch's paired loss relative to Wald is 0.30 percentage points,
with a 95\% interval of 0.27--0.34. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-power-contrasts}
reports paired comparisons at two null SDs for all three focus settings.
The intervals use the same sampled pairs to estimate the difference
directly. The calibrated comparison then assesses power after matching
null rejection.

\subsection{Power after empirical null calibration}

Expanded Welch's power gain depends on which equal-MI population supplies
the cutoff. With both population MI values set to 0.02 nats, the lower-null
calibration gives rejection rates of 4.93\% to 4.99\% on independent null
samples across A--C for both tests and the fixed reference. Wald and the
fixed moment reference have identical calibrated decisions.
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-calibrated-power} shows the full curves
under this lower-null calibration.

The upper null sets both MI values to the fixed alternative's larger MI.
Table~\ref{tab:redesign-calibration-sensitivity} compares lower- and
upper-null calibration while holding each alternative fixed. At A and two
null SDs, the gain over Wald changes from 3.58 percentage points to a loss
of 0.76 points. At B and four SDs, a 3.92-point gain becomes a 0.39-point
loss. Under upper-null calibration, A at four SDs retains a small positive
estimate whose interval includes zero, while B at two SDs retains a much
smaller gain. At C, both calibrations give differences well within the
one-percentage-point tolerance.

The second column gives the common MI of the upper null. Brackets give
paired 95\% bootstrap intervals including uncertainty from fitting the
cutoffs.

\section{Association pattern and sample allocation}

Concentrating dependence in four cells reproduces the main sparse-table
failure. At \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) and different skew, the
null difference bias is 0.03155 nats, compared with 0.02985 under copying.
Both tests and the fixed moment reference reject far too often. With
same-skew populations, expected difference bias cancels and rejection
remains conservative.

In the different-skew \(4\times4\) comparison, allocating fewer
observations to \(Q\) lowers Expanded Welch's power. Assigning only 400
of the 2,000 observations to \(Q\) increases the observed SD from 0.01173
to 0.01611 nats. At the 0.01-nat alternative, power falls from 10.26\%
under equal allocation to 3.42\%. Expanded Welch's median degrees of freedom also
fall, increasing the rejection cutoff. Reversing which population has
fewer observations changes the result because the two populations have
different variances and residual biases. The experimental supplement
reports the complete construction and allocation comparisons.

\section{Application to online shopping data}
\label{sec:shoppers-application}

The Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention dataset provides a practical
comparison of month--purchase MI between new and returning visitors
\citep{sakar2018}.
A larger MI means that the month provides more information about whether
a session ends in a purchase. A second comparison uses the weekend
indicator instead of month to examine whether its MI with purchasing
differs between the same visitor groups. This application was selected
from an initial exploration of six comparisons across three public datasets.

The dataset contains 12,330 sessions, with each session belonging to a
different user according to the source documentation \citep{sakar2018}.
The analysis retains all 1,694 sessions labelled \texttt{New\_Visitor}
and 10,551 labelled \texttt{Returning\_Visitor}, excluding the 85
\texttt{Other} sessions. The outcome is the original Boolean
\texttt{Revenue} variable: true indicates a purchase and false indicates
no purchase. The tests treat the two disjoint visitor groups as independent
samples and the sessions within each group as independent observations.

Each group forms a \(10\times2\) month--purchase count table. The rows
are the ten months recorded in the dataset: February, March, May, June,
July, August, September, October, November and December. The columns
are no purchase and purchase, and each session adds one to its corresponding
cell. Both groups use these same categories, with no smoothing or removal
of zero cells. All ten months occur in both groups, although the new-visitor
February row contains only one session and no purchase. Replacing month
with the Boolean \texttt{Weekend} variable gives a \(2\times2\) table
for each group, with weekday and weekend rows; all four cells are observed.

Both methods use the bias-corrected MI estimates in
Table~\ref{tab:shoppers-application}. For the month tables, the leading
bias correction subtracts \((10-1)(2-1)/(2n)=9/(2n)\) from each plug-in
MI estimate \citep{miller1955,moddemeijer1989};
for the weekend tables, it subtracts \(1/(2n)\). Here \(n\) is the
number of sessions in the relevant visitor group. The estimated
difference is new-visitor MI minus returning-visitor MI for the same
predictor--purchase pair. Both tests use this difference and the shared standard error,
with two-sided rejection at the 5\% level.

Month has higher estimated MI with purchasing among returning visitors.
The corrected difference is \(-0.013563\) nats, with a standard error of
\(0.003333\) nats, giving
\begin{equation}
  T\approx\frac{-0.013563}{0.003333}\approx-4.069.
  \label{eq:shoppers-month-statistic}
\end{equation}
Both tests reject equal month--purchase MI for the visitor groups,
although the Student \(t\) distribution with 26.79 degrees of freedom gives
a larger p-value than the standard normal distribution. Holm's adjustment
for all six comparisons \citep{holm1979} gives month p-values of 0.000189
for Normal Wald and 0.00149 for Expanded~Welch, so rejection persists.
Table~\ref{tab:application-holm} gives all six comparisons and their raw
and adjusted p-values.

For weekend, both corrected MI estimates are approximately 0.0007 nats.
Their difference, \(-0.000063\) nats, is small relative to the standard
error of \(0.001124\) nats, giving \(T\approx-0.056\). Both p-values
remain close to one despite Expanded Welch's 2.18 estimated degrees of
freedom. Neither test provides evidence of different weekend--purchase MI
between the visitor groups; this does not establish equality. Small
estimated degrees of freedom with weak associations and large samples
are consistent with the near-independence simulations. The unknown
population tables prevent a direct check of the sampling-moment target.

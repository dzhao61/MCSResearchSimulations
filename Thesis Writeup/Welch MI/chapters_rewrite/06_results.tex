\chapter{Experimental Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results}

The results first show how sample size, table size and category frequencies
affect false-positive rates relative to the intended 5\% level. They then
examine how the MI value, equal in both populations, affects Expanded
Welch's estimated degrees of freedom, followed by power when population MI
differs. Each simulation rate uses 20,000 sampled table pairs. Both tests
return valid p-values in every repetition of the population-MI sweep,
power study, construction check and sample-allocation check. The null study
includes small-sample settings where some calculations fail.

Blue solid curves show Normal Wald and magenta
dashed curves show Expanded Welch. Error bars or bands give pointwise
Wilson 95\% intervals, and the dotted line marks 0.05. Hollow markers
indicate valid-result frequency below 90\%. The parameter settings are
those in Chapter~\ref{chap:design}; additional comparisons and the full
120-setting null table are in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.
The chapter ends with a self-contained application to online shopping data.

\section{False positives across sample size and population structure}

\subsection{Increasing table size}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-null-sizes} holds both population MI values at
0.02 nats and compares the four table sizes under different skew.
The dominant marginal probabilities are 0.7 for \(P\) and 0.8 for \(Q\),
with the remaining mass shared equally across their other categories.
Each panel varies only sample size, from 10 to 50,000 per population.
The lower strip shows how often each method returns a valid p-value.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_null_sizes.pdf}
\caption{False positives and validity under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:focused-null-sizes}
\end{figure}

The false-positive pattern changes substantially with table size. In
\(2\times2\) tables both methods reject too rarely over much of the
sample-size range. In \(4\times4\) tables Wald is above 0.05 at
\(n=10\) and 20, but both methods fall below the target at larger
intermediate sample sizes. In the \(8\times8\) comparison, the transition
occurs at larger sample sizes. Expanded Welch lowers rejection throughout, helping
where Wald is excessive and moving an already-low rate further below 0.05.

At the smallest sample size, part of that reduction comes from failed
calculations. For \(4\times4\) tables at \(n=10\), Wald rejects at 0.1332
and Expanded Welch at 0.0465, but their valid-result frequencies are
0.9910 and 0.7588. Among the 15,175 table pairs for which both tests return
valid p-values, the rejection rates are 0.1029 and 0.0612. Expanded
Welch's larger cutoff still lowers rejection on this subset, but its rate
across all 20,000 pairs is near 0.05 partly because failed calculations
count as non-rejections.

The \(16\times16\) table shows that a test can return valid p-values
and still reject equal MI too often. At \(n=1{,}000\), every calculation
is valid, yet Wald
rejects at 0.2522 and Expanded Welch at 0.2402. The paired reduction
is 0.0120, with approximate 95\% interval [0.0105, 0.0135],
so Expanded Welch's larger cutoff lowers rejection but leaves a large
excess of false positives. At \(n=10{,}000\) the rates are 0.0463 and 0.0453;
both remain near 5\% at 50,000. More observations substantially
improve false-positive control in this setting.

\subsection{Changing the margin regime}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-null-margins} compares all three margin regimes
at \(4\times4\) and \(16\times16\), retaining MI of 0.02 nats in
both populations and the same sample-size sweep. Uniform and same-skew populations have identical
joint distributions within each pair, whereas different-skew populations have
different tables but equal MI. Comparing the columns shows how uneven
category frequencies and different population margins change rejection rates.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_null_margins.pdf}
\caption{False positives and validity across three margin regimes.}
\label{fig:focused-null-margins}
\end{figure}

At \(4\times4\), all three regimes give low rejection at intermediate
sample sizes and rates much nearer 0.05 by \(n=10{,}000\).
At \(n=1{,}000\), for example, Wald rates range from 0.0383 to
0.0394, while Expanded Welch ranges from 0.0319 to 0.0331.
Changing the margins has a relatively small effect on these particular
rates. The larger table behaves
differently: at \(n=1{,}000\), Wald rejects at 0.0339 under uniform
margins, 0.0065 under same skew, and 0.2522 under different skew.
Expanded Welch gives 0.0331, 0.0059 and 0.2402, respectively.
The different-skew excess is therefore not a general consequence of
uneven margins alone.

Excessive false positives also occur when the population tables are
identical. With uniform margins, Wald rejects at 0.0737 for
\(8\times8\) tables at \(n=100\), and at 0.0859 for \(16\times16\)
tables at \(n=250\). All calculations are valid. Because the two samples
then have the same distribution and size, their expected MI estimation
biases cancel in the difference. In these settings, the mean squared standard error
is approximately 0.84 and 0.77 times the observed variance, respectively.
The tests therefore assign too little uncertainty on average,
which can increase false positives even without a biased MI difference.

Different population margins can introduce a further problem: bias in the
estimated MI difference itself.
Table~\ref{tab:focused-diagnostics} reports four
different-skew null settings. Observed SD is the sample standard deviation
of the corrected MI difference. Estimated SD is the square root of the
mean squared standard error. The
variance ratio divides the mean squared standard error by the observed
sampling variance. All four settings have full validity and finite diagnostics.

The \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) difference has bias 0.02960 nats,
more than twice its observed SD of approximately 0.01449. To see where
this shift comes from, recall that bias is the mean estimate across
samples minus the population value. The bias of a difference is exactly
bias of \(P\)'s MI estimate minus the bias of \(Q\)'s MI estimate. Here the
individual corrected MI biases are approximately \(-0.00794\) for \(P\)
and \(-0.03754\) for \(Q\). Since the true difference is zero, the
estimated difference is centred at
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb{E}(\Deltahat)
 \simeq 0+(-0.00794)-(-0.03754)=0.02960
 \quad\text{nats}.
 \label{eq:focused-null-bias-shift}
\end{equation}
Both corrected estimates are too low on average, but \(Q\)'s is lower
by more, so subtracting it creates a positive difference even
though the population MI values match. The fixed-alphabet bias correction
cancels because the table and sample sizes match, so the raw plug-in
difference has exactly the same bias. The sparse-sample bias in the two MI
estimates is not captured by their common leading correction
\citep{paninski2003}. In this
copying setting, an average of 147 of \(P\)'s 256 cells and 183 of
\(Q\)'s cells are empty.

For the copying \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) pair, estimated uncertainty
is also inaccurate: mean squared standard error is about 1.94 times the
observed variance. Excessive false positives nevertheless remain:
overestimating the variance does not remove the positive bias in the
estimated MI difference. At \(n=10{,}000\)
the bias is much smaller and the variance ratio is 1.07, accompanying
the improved null rate. The population first-order variance is about
0.60 times the observed variance at \(n=1{,}000\), compared with 0.84
at 10,000, showing that the approximation itself also needs attention.

Expanded Welch's estimated degrees of freedom also change with sample size
and table size. Under same skew at \(4\times4\), the median
rises from 33.45 at \(n=1{,}000\) to 279.00 at 10,000, bringing the
Student \(t\) distribution closer to the standard normal distribution.
At \(16\times16\) and \(n=1{,}000\), its same-skew median is
78.89, but Expanded Welch still rejects at only 0.0059. The mean squared
standard error used by both tests is then 1.93 times the observed variance
of the MI difference. Thus the estimated uncertainty can remain inaccurate
even when Expanded Welch's Student \(t\) distribution is close to the
standard normal distribution. Table~\ref{tab:df-structure} compares both
skew regimes.

\section{Population MI and degrees of freedom}
\label{sec:mechanism-results}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-association} uses \(4\times4\) tables under
same skew and different skew. Within each regime, the MI value varies
from 0.0001 to 0.05 nats while remaining equal in both populations, and
the category frequencies stay fixed.
The two columns use \(n=100\) and 1,000. Upper panels show false
positives; lower panels show the median estimated combined degrees of
freedom, with the 5th--95th percentile range shaded. Circles indicate same
skew and triangles different skew.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_association_skew_comparison.pdf}
\caption{Population MI, false positives and degrees of freedom under both skew regimes.}
\label{fig:focused-association}
\end{figure}

Both tests reject too rarely near independence under either regime, and
Expanded Welch rejects still less. Under different skew at MI
0.0001 and \(n=1{,}000\), Wald's rate
is 0.0101 and Expanded Welch's is 0.0044. At MI 0.05, Wald rejects
at 0.0472 and Expanded Welch at 0.0438. Larger population MI brings the
rates closer to
0.05 in this comparison, especially at the larger sample size. The
\(n=100\) rates remain low even at the largest MI target, so a larger
MI value does not substitute for enough observations.

The matched same-skew results show that these low rejection rates also occur
when category frequencies match. At MI 0.0001 and
\(n=1{,}000\), its Wald and Expanded Welch rates are 0.0130 and
0.0042; at MI 0.05, they are 0.0463 and 0.0429. Since the two population
tables and sample sizes match under the null, their expected MI biases
cancel, but both tests still reject too rarely near independence.

Expanded Welch's estimated degrees of freedom increase with population MI
in both regimes. Under different skew at
\(n=1{,}000\), their median rises from 8.62 at MI 0.0001 to 78.24
at MI 0.05; at \(n=100\), it rises from 4.23 to 13.80. Under same
skew at \(n=1{,}000\), the median likewise rises from 8.23 to 74.42.
The relationship between population MI and estimated degrees of freedom
therefore persists when category frequencies match.
Tables~\ref{tab:association-diagnostics} and
\ref{tab:same-association-diagnostics} give the corresponding diagnostics.

The median degrees of freedom illustrate the change in cutoff. For a
two-sided 5\% test, the cutoffs from the Student \(t\) distribution
are approximately 2.277 at 8.62 degrees of freedom and 1.991 at 78.24,
compared with Normal Wald's 1.960. Thus \(|T|=2.1\) would reject under
Wald but not under Expanded Welch with 8.62 degrees of freedom; at
78.24 both would reject. These examples use the reported medians,
whereas each sampled table pair uses its own estimated degrees of freedom.

At the smallest MI target, the median estimated degrees of freedom remain
several units. The population approximation in
Section~\ref{sec:near-independence-mechanism} instead approaches zero as
the population table approaches independence. The estimates use sampled
tables, where random count variation creates apparent MI even when
population MI is almost zero. Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}
gives the corresponding quadratic calculation.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Accuracy of the standard error.}

The standard error used by both tests overestimates the variation of the
estimated MI difference near independence. For different-skew
\(4\times4\) populations at MI 0.0001 and \(n=1{,}000\), the mean
squared standard error is 1.74 times the observed variance of the estimated
MI difference. In contrast, the first-order variance calculated from the
true probabilities, \(V(P)/n_P+V(Q)/n_Q\), is only 0.04 times that
observed variance. Both comparisons concern the sampling variance of the
MI difference: the first uses sample estimates of the cell probabilities,
while the second uses the known population probabilities.

Near independence, the true first-order variance is small because the
population cell probabilities are close to the products of their
margins. A finite sample can still show apparent association: observed
cell proportions generally differ from the products of their observed
margins. The plug-in variance estimate treats those departures as features
of an estimated population table and can therefore assign a substantially
larger first-order variance. Meanwhile, the linear approximation at the
true table omits the quadratic sampling terms that matter near
independence. \citet{brillinger2004} distinguishes these first-order and
independence calculations; Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}
shows why the quadratic terms remain when the first-order term vanishes.

At MI 0.05 in the same different-skew \(4\times4\), \(n=1{,}000\)
comparison, mean squared standard error is approximately 1.00 times
the observed variance, and the population first-order variance is 0.95
times the observed variance. Detailed values are in
Table~\ref{tab:association-diagnostics}.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Variability of the MI variance estimate.}

Expanded Welch also needs to estimate how much \(\Vhat(P)\) itself
changes between samples, because this variability determines its component
degrees of freedom. Table~\ref{tab:variance-stability} compares the
observed variance of \(\Vhat(P)\) with the population prediction
\(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) and the average plug-in prediction
\(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\), and repeats the comparison for \(Q\).
Here the quantity varying between repetitions is the estimated MI variance,
rather than the estimated MI difference examined above.

For different-skew \(4\times4\) populations with MI 0.02 and
\(n=10{,}000\), both predictions are within 2\% of the observed
variability of the MI variance estimate. At MI 0.0001 and
\(n=1{,}000\), the population prediction accounts for only about 5\%
of that variability, while the average plug-in prediction is 1.95 times
the observed value for \(P\) and 2.09 times for \(Q\). Thus the
sampling-variability approximation used to calculate degrees of freedom
is accurate in the first of these settings but has substantial errors
near independence.

\section{Power to detect MI differences}

\subsection{Power across table sizes}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-detection-sizes} fixes \(I(P)=0.02\) nats,
sets \(I(Q)=0.02+|\Delta|\), and uses different-skew margins. Each
panel compares \(n=1{,}000\) and 10,000 at one table size. The figure
enlarges differences up to 0.02 nats; the full grid through 0.2 is in
Figure~\ref{fig:focused-detection-full}. Since
\(\Delta=I(P)-I(Q)\), the true difference is negative when \(Q\)
has the larger MI, while the figures plot its nonnegative magnitude
\(|\Delta|\).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_detection_sizes.pdf}
\caption{Rejection rates over small MI differences under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:focused-detection-sizes}
\end{figure}

For the smaller tables, increasing the magnitude of the true MI difference
generally increases power, and larger samples make a clear improvement.
In \(4\times4\) tables, Wald and Expanded Welch have estimated power
of 0.1132 and 0.1013 for a 0.01-nat difference at \(n=1{,}000\),
compared with 0.7962 and 0.7940 at \(n=10{,}000\). Expanded Welch's larger
cutoff costs some power at the smaller sample size and very little at
the larger one, because higher estimated degrees of freedom
bring Expanded Welch's cutoff closer to Wald's.
Both tests use a nominal 5\% significance level, but their actual
false-positive rates differ, as the zero-difference points show.

The \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) curve instead falls initially as
the magnitude of the true difference grows. Wald rejects at 0.2522 under
equal MI, 0.1005 at a difference of 0.01, and 0.0363 at 0.02. The positive bias
identified earlier opposes the negative true difference. The mean
estimated difference is approximately 0.02960 at the null.

\begin{samepage}
Under the next two alternatives, the true difference and estimated difference
bias combine as follows:
\begin{align}
 \mathbb{E}(\Deltahat)&\simeq-0.01+0.03061=0.02061,\\*
 \mathbb{E}(\Deltahat)&\simeq-0.02+0.03164=0.01164.
 \label{eq:focused-detection-bias-shift}
\end{align}
\end{samepage}
The bias in the estimated MI difference changes slightly across these
alternatives, but remains
positive and larger than the magnitude of the true difference. As the
true difference becomes more negative, it offsets more of that bias,
so the centre of the estimated difference moves towards zero
rather than away from it. Dividing by the estimated standard error
can then give smaller absolute statistics, despite the larger population
difference. Unequal estimation bias therefore makes rejection fall as
the magnitude of the true difference grows over this range. The full-range
comparison shows rejection rising again at larger differences.

The bias can also reverse the estimated direction of the difference.
Although \(Q\) has the larger population MI at \(|\Delta|=0.01\),
2,009 of the 2,010 Wald rejections and 1,905 of the 1,906 Expanded
Welch rejections have a positive estimated difference, indicating larger
MI for \(P\). The rejection curves count all two-sided rejections;
they do not show whether the estimated ordering is correct.
Table~\ref{tab:rejection-direction} separates these outcomes.

At \(n=10{,}000\), the \(16\times16\) bias is much smaller, and
power increases across the small differences. For a 0.01-nat difference,
Wald's estimated power is 0.6992 and Expanded Welch's is 0.6968; by 0.02
the rates are 0.9932 and 0.9931. Every rejection in these two larger-sample
comparisons has the correct negative estimated direction. The larger
sample improves both the null behaviour and power.

The matched same-skew curves give a different sparse-table pattern
(Figures~\ref{fig:same-detection-sizes} and
\ref{fig:same-detection-full}). In \(16\times16\) tables at
\(n=1{,}000\), Wald rejects at 0.0065 under equal MI, 0.0185 at
a 0.01-nat difference and 0.0630 at 0.02. The sharp initial fall under
different skew is absent. At 0.01 nats, the same-skew difference bias is
0.00092 nats, much smaller than the different-skew bias of 0.03061;
356 of its 370 Wald rejections have the correct estimated direction.
Matching margins therefore changes the sparse power curve substantially,
although low power and some wrong-direction rejections remain.

Both tests still assign too much uncertainty to the same-skew difference
in this sparse setting. At the 0.01-nat alternative and \(n=1{,}000\),
the mean squared standard error is 1.82 times the observed variance.
Expanded Welch's further reduction in power, from Wald's 0.0185 to
0.0170, comes from using the Student \(t\) distribution; its median
estimated degrees of freedom are 84.00.

At \(n=10{,}000\) for the same alternative, the variance ratio is
1.01 and power rises to 0.6146 for Wald and 0.6113 for Expanded Welch.
The larger sample improves the variance estimate used by both methods.
Expanded Welch's median degrees of freedom also rise to 307.66, bringing
its Student \(t\) distribution closer to the standard normal distribution
and reducing the difference between the methods' rejection rates.

\subsection{Power across margin regimes}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-detection-margins} fixes the table size at
\(4\times4\), sets \(I(P)=0.02\) and \(I(Q)=0.02+|\Delta|\), and
compares the three margin regimes at \(n=1{,}000\) and 10,000. This
isolates the effect of the margin regime on power at a fixed table size.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_detection_margins.pdf}
\caption{Rejection rates over small MI differences in \(4\times4\) tables.}
\label{fig:focused-detection-margins}
\end{figure}

Uniform margins give somewhat higher power in these settings.
At \(n=10{,}000\) and a 0.01-nat difference, Wald's estimated power is 0.8635
under uniform margins, 0.7790 under same skew, and 0.7962 under
different skew. Expanded Welch gives 0.8619, 0.7768 and 0.7940.
The margin-related change is larger than the difference between tests
at this sample size.

The observed precision helps explain the ordering of power. For this
0.01-nat difference at \(n=10{,}000\), the sample SD of the estimated
difference is approximately 0.00327 nats under uniform margins, 0.00364
under same skew and 0.00361 under different skew. The biases are small
in all three settings, and the square roots of the mean squared standard
errors are close to these observed SDs. The same population difference
is therefore easier to distinguish from sampling variation when the
copying populations have uniform margins. Equal MI targets do not imply
equal precision:
the cell probabilities also determine the variance in
Equation~\eqref{eq:background-two-sample-variance}.

At \(n=1{,}000\), Expanded Welch's larger cutoff produces a
more visible reduction in power, while all three null points are
already below 0.05. By a difference of 0.2 nats, both methods reject
every simulated pair in all three regimes at both sample sizes.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Supporting checks.}

Changing the population construction preserves the main comparison between
the tests at the matched \(4\times4\) settings. Under both skew
regimes, Expanded Welch lowers an already-low false-positive rate at
\(n=1{,}000\), while the power difference between the methods is small
at \(n=10{,}000\). Tables~\ref{tab:construction-check} and
\ref{tab:same-construction-check} compare copying and the four-cell
transfer under different skew and same skew, respectively.

The four-cell construction also reproduces the sparse \(16\times16\)
different-skew bias. At \(n=1{,}000\) under equal MI, its difference
bias is 0.03145 nats and the false-positive rates are 0.3095 for Wald and
0.2977 for Expanded Welch. At \(n=10{,}000\), its bias is much smaller
and the rates are 0.0499 and 0.0491. In contrast, the matched same-skew
populations give low false-positive rates under both constructions at
\(n=1{,}000\). These comparisons distinguish the different-skew bias
failure from the low rejection observed when the category frequencies match.

At the \(16\times16\) different-skew 0.01-nat alternative and
\(n=1{,}000\), the four-cell construction gives 2,493 of 2,494 Wald
rejections and 2,350 of 2,351 Expanded Welch rejections with the wrong
estimated ordering. Thus the direction error also persists when dependence
is concentrated in four cells. At \(n=10{,}000\), every rejection
under either construction has the correct estimated direction.
Table~\ref{tab:large-construction-check} gives the matched comparisons.

The unequal-sample check keeps the \(4\times4\) different-skew
populations fixed and changes the allocation of 2,000 observations.
For the 0.01-nat difference, giving \(Q\) only 400 observations reduces power to
0.0540 for Wald and 0.0340 for Expanded Welch, compared with their
balanced rates of 0.1132 and 0.1013.
The lower degrees of freedom under imbalance
increase Expanded Welch's rejection cutoff, moving already-conservative
false-positive rates further below 5\%.
Table~\ref{tab:allocation-check} reports both allocation directions.

With same-skew margins, the null populations are identical, but unequal
allocation still leaves residual difference bias. At \((400,1600)\)
and \((1600,400)\), the biases are approximately 0.00128 and
\(-0.00148\) nats. Expanded Welch's median degrees of freedom fall
from 33.45 under balance to approximately 11.3 under either imbalance,
and its larger cutoff further lowers the false-positive rates.

At the same-skew 0.01-nat alternative, allocating more observations to
\(Q\) gives higher power than allocating fewer to \(Q\). Wald's
rates are 0.1300 when \(Q\) receives 1,600 observations and 0.0506
when it receives only 400; Expanded Welch gives 0.0946 and 0.0340.
Table~\ref{tab:same-allocation-check} reports these allocations and
their estimation diagnostics. Matching category frequencies therefore
does not remove the effects of unequal sample sizes on bias and power.

The jackknife check asks whether changing the MI bias correction can
improve the estimated ordering without changing the rejection cutoff.
Replacing Wald's analytic MI bias correction with a jackknife correction
reduces wrong-direction rejections from 2,009 of 2,010 to 5 of 549 for the
\(16\times16\) different-skew 0.01-nat alternative at \(n=1{,}000\).
Correct-direction rejections increase from 1 to 544. The check retains the
existing standard error and standard normal distribution cutoff, and its
null rejection rate of 0.0085 remains below the 0.05 target.
Appendix~\ref{sec:jackknife-difference-check} reports this four-setting check.

\section{Application to online shopping data}
\label{sec:shoppers-application}

The Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention dataset provides a practical
comparison of month--purchase MI between new and returning visitors
\citep{sakar2018}.
A larger MI means that the month provides more information about whether
a session ends in a purchase. A second comparison uses the weekend
indicator instead of month to examine whether its MI with purchasing
differs between the same visitor groups.

The dataset contains 12,330 sessions, with each session belonging to a
different user according to the source documentation \citep{sakar2018}.
The analysis retains all 1,694 sessions labelled \texttt{New\_Visitor}
and 10,551 labelled \texttt{Returning\_Visitor}, excluding the 85
\texttt{Other} sessions. The outcome is the original Boolean
\texttt{Revenue} variable: true indicates a purchase and false indicates
no purchase. The tests treat the two disjoint visitor groups as independent
samples and the sessions within each group as independent observations.

Each group forms a \(10\times2\) month--purchase count table. The rows
are the ten months recorded in the dataset: February, March, May, June,
July, August, September, October, November and December. The columns
are no purchase and purchase, and each session adds one to its corresponding
cell. Both groups use these same categories, with no smoothing or removal
of zero cells. All ten months occur in both groups, although the new-visitor
February row contains only one session and no purchase. Replacing month
with the Boolean \texttt{Weekend} variable gives a \(2\times2\) table
for each group, with weekday and weekend rows; all four cells are observed.

Both methods use the bias-corrected MI estimates in
Table~\ref{tab:shoppers-application}. For the month tables, the leading
bias correction subtracts \((10-1)(2-1)/(2n)=9/(2n)\) from each plug-in
MI estimate \citep{miller1955,moddemeijer1989};
for the weekend tables, it subtracts \(1/(2n)\). Here \(n\) is the
number of sessions in the relevant visitor group. The estimated
difference is new-visitor MI minus returning-visitor MI for the same
predictor--purchase pair. Normal Wald
compares this difference divided by its standard error with the standard
normal distribution; Expanded Welch uses the same statistic with a
Student \(t\) distribution and estimated degrees of freedom. Both tests
are two-sided at the 5\% level.

Month has higher estimated MI with purchasing among returning visitors.
The corrected difference is \(-0.013563\) nats, with a standard error of
\(0.003333\) nats, giving
\begin{equation}
  T\approx\frac{-0.013563}{0.003333}\approx-4.069.
  \label{eq:shoppers-month-statistic}
\end{equation}
With 26.79 estimated degrees of freedom, the Student \(t\) distribution gives
a larger p-value than the standard normal distribution, but both tests
reject equal month--purchase MI for new and returning visitors. This
application was selected from an initial exploration
of six comparisons across three public datasets. Accounting for all six
comparisons with Holm's multiple-testing adjustment \citep{holm1979}
gives month p-values of 0.000189 for Normal Wald and 0.00149 for Expanded
Welch, so the conclusion remains unchanged.
Table~\ref{tab:application-holm} lists all six comparisons and their raw
and adjusted p-values.

The weekend comparison gives a different result: the corrected
weekend--purchase MI estimates for new and returning visitors are both
approximately 0.0007 nats. Their difference,
\(-0.000063\) nats, is small relative to the standard error of
\(0.001124\) nats, giving \(T\approx-0.056\). Although Expanded Welch
estimates only 2.18 degrees of freedom, both p-values remain close to one.
Neither test provides evidence that weekend--purchase MI differs between
the visitor groups, but this does not establish that their population MI
values are equal. The small statistic, rather than the degrees of freedom
alone, explains the agreement between the methods here.

Both methods reach the same decisions in this application, although
Expanded Welch returns larger p-values. Its degrees of freedom depend on
how much the estimated MI variances fluctuate relative to their mean
values, so large session counts alone need not make its p-values close
to Normal Wald's.

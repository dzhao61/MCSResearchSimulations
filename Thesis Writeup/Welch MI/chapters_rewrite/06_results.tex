\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_redesign_reporting_values}

This chapter checks the approximations used to choose Expanded Welch's
degrees of freedom, then compares its false-positive rates and power with
Normal Wald. Supporting simulations examine additional settings, and the
online-shopping application illustrates interpretation of the test outputs.

One simulation repetition consists of independently drawing one count table
from \(P\) and one from \(Q\). Normal Wald and Expanded Welch are both
applied to the same two sampled tables in that repetition. Each evaluation
setting uses 100,000 repetitions.

\section{RQ1: Accuracy of variance approximations}

For a fixed population table \(P\) and sample size \(n_P\), each
repetition produces a new \(\Vhat(P)\). We examine its average value,
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\), and its variation between repetitions,
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\). These are the two moments needed for the component
degrees of freedom. Table~\ref{tab:results-variance-quantities} in
Chapter~\ref{chap:baselines} distinguishes these quantities from the
sampling variance of the MI difference.

\subsection{Mean and sampling-variance predictions}

Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-moment-approximations} predicts
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx V(P)\) and
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx\tau^2(P)/n_P\), where \(\tau^2(P)\)
measures the variability of the observation-level sensitivities derived
in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}. We compare each prediction with the
corresponding moment observed across repetitions, and repeat the checks
for \(Q\).

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments} shows the different-skew populations
at common MI 0.02 nats. The top row divides the simulated mean of
\(\Vhat(P)\) by the population benchmark \(V(P)\), and likewise for \(Q\).
The middle row divides \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) by the observed
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\). The bottom row uses the average
\(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\) as its numerator and the same observed
variance as its denominator. Both variance rows use the same vertical
scale, so their differences show the effect of substituting sample
proportions. A ratio of one indicates agreement. Shading in the first
two rows shows approximate 95\% Monte Carlo
intervals from 100,000 repetitions; their half-widths are below 1.3\%
of the plotted values, making them barely visible on this scale.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_moment_predictions_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Mean and sampling-variance ratios under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:redesign-moments}
\end{figure}

In the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}, the larger-table
mean ratios first rise and then fall. Initially, many
rare combinations are unobserved and contribute nothing to \(\Vhat\).
As more appear, cells observed only once can have large estimated
pointwise MI. For example, if a cell and its row and column each contain
one observation, its empirical pointwise MI is \(\log n\).
Such values can be far from the table's plug-in MI and contribute
strongly to \(\Vhat\). A decomposition of the \(16\times16\) sampled
tables confirms that once-observed cells dominate its mean around the
peak (Appendix~\ref{sec:component-diagnostics}). Their contribution then
falls as repeated counts give better estimates of the cell probabilities.
Under these margin rules, larger tables spread probability over more
rare combinations, extending the sample-size range over which this
distortion matters.

In both top panels, the \(2\times2\) mean ratio falls towards one
without a pronounced peak
over the plotted range; the \(4\times4\) peak is smaller and earlier
than those for the two larger tables. The larger tables also take more
observations to recover. The normalised mean overshoot is greater in
\(P\) than in \(Q\), although \(Q\) has more uneven margins.
Changing the margins changes both the population benchmark \(V\) and
which rare cells appear in a sample, so equal MI does not give equal
relative errors.

The middle-row dips show a related limitation of the linear approximation.
The prediction \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) includes the first-order change in
\(\Vhat(P)\), while the empirical log ratios can change substantially
when rare cells appear or their counts increase. Checks of the linear
change and the remaining error find substantial variation beyond the
linear term around the larger-table peaks. The observed sampling variance
then exceeds the prediction, putting the ratio below one.

In the bottom row, substituting sample proportions changes the sensitivities used to
estimate \(\tau^2(P)\). Around the peak, once-observed cells contribute
strongly to this estimate as well as to \(\Vhat(P)\). The sample-based
prediction can consequently overshoot the observed sampling variance,
giving bottom-row ratios above one even while the population prediction
remains below it.

The contrast between the middle and bottom rows is strongest for the larger
tables in both populations: their population predictions dip farther
below the observed variance, while their sample-based predictions
first underestimate it and then overshoot. The \(2\times2\) curves
have much smaller departures and settle near one sooner. Thus sample
substitution can reverse the direction of the prediction error, rather
than simply reduce the error in the population formula. For
\(16\times16\), the deepest dip and the transition to sample-based
overprediction occur later in \(Q\) than in \(P\), showing that
margins also affect the timing of these errors.

Across all three rows, larger samples make empirical probabilities more accurate and the
variance ratios approach one. The mean ratios can remain above one after
the variance ratios are already close, so accurate prediction of variation
between samples does not require equally accurate prediction of the mean.
The experimental supplement provides
the companion uniform and same-skew plots.

\subsection{How moment errors affect degrees of freedom}

Equation~\eqref{eq:design-moment-target} defines
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\) from \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\) and
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\). We compare this target with \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\),
calculated from the population first-order formula, and the average of
\(\widehat\nu_P\), estimated from each sampled table.
Table~\ref{tab:component-df-roles} identifies their inputs. The first
comparison checks the moment approximations; the second also includes
the effect of substituting sample proportions.

For the same different-skew populations,
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df} uses the moment target,
estimated from the simulations, as the common denominator. The top row shows
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}/\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\); the bottom row shows
\(\E(\widehat\nu_P)/\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\), and likewise for \(Q\).
Both rows use the same vertical scale. Ratios below one indicate fewer
degrees of freedom than the moment target; ratios above one indicate more.
A bottom-row value is shown only when that component's estimate is positive
and finite in all 100,000 repetitions. Shading in the top row gives
approximate 95\% Monte Carlo intervals.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_component_df_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Component degrees-of-freedom approximations relative to their moment targets.}
\label{fig:redesign-component-df}
\end{figure}

The top row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df} combines two moment
errors that act in opposite directions. In the moment-matching
formula, underestimating \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\) lowers the squared
numerator, whereas underestimating \(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\) lowers the
denominator and raises the resulting degrees of freedom. The top row
therefore reflects both errors in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}.
Over most of the sample-size range, the mean error dominates and the
first-order degrees of freedom fall below the moment target. As the moment ratios
approach one, the first-order value approaches that target.

In both top panels, \(2\times2\) reaches the target first, followed
by \(4\times4\); the two larger tables remain below it over a wider
sample-size range. This follows the size ordering of the mean errors
in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}, amplified by squaring the mean.
The first-order ratios are generally closer to one for \(Q\), whose
normalised mean overshoot is smaller.

In the bottom row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df},
sample substitution changes both parts of the degrees-of-freedom formula. Using
\(\Vhat(P)\) in the numerator captures its enlarged sampling mean,
but the sample-based prediction of its sampling variance can be too large.

These bottom panels also recover in table-size order, but their early
curves differ from the broadly rising first-order ratios. The first
shown \(16\times16\) estimates exceed the target, then fall below
it as the sample-based variance prediction changes from too small to
too large. The \(4\times4\) and \(8\times8\) estimates have smaller
excursions before recovering. The \(2\times2\) summaries begin later
and are already close to the target when shown. Their missing earlier
points reflect the requirement that every component estimate be
positive and finite; they do not represent zero degrees of freedom.

For a given numerator, a larger variance prediction gives fewer degrees
of freedom. Averaging the sampled ratio also affects the size of the
error; it is not equivalent to inserting average inputs into the formula.

At larger sample sizes in these bottom panels, the sample-based variance
prediction improves and the mean estimated
degrees of freedom recover towards the moment target. The population
first-order value can remain farther below the target because it still
uses \(V(P)\) in place of the enlarged sampling mean.

Table~\ref{tab:variance-stability} gives numerical comparisons for
different-skew \(4\times4\) populations at \(n=1{,}000\).
``Mean ratio'' divides the simulated mean of \(\Vhat(P)\) by
\(V(P)\). Both ``Var. ratio'' columns divide their prediction by the
observed \(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\): ``pop.'' uses the population
prediction; ``sample'' averages the sample-based predictions.

For component degrees of freedom (df), ``Mean df'' averages
\(\widehat\nu_P\), ``Moment df'' gives \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\), and
\(\nu_{\mathrm{FO}}\) gives \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\). The corresponding
definitions apply to \(Q\).
All 100,000 component estimates are positive, finite and included in each row.

\subsection{Behaviour near independence}

Near independence, the pointwise-MI weights in the linear sampling error
become small, so quadratic terms in the cell-probability errors can
contribute substantially to the variability of the estimates. Even when
population MI is almost zero, sampling can produce apparent association
and positive \(\Vhat(P)\) and \(\Vhat(Q)\) values. The
near-independence calculation in Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}
retains these quadratic terms to predict the moments of \(\Vhat\) and
the mean estimated component degrees of freedom.

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4} shows \(4\times4\) different-skew
populations at \(n=1{,}000\). The first two rows compare the observed
mean and sampling variance of \(\Vhat\), respectively, with their
near-independence predictions. The bottom row compares the mean
estimated degrees of freedom, its
near-independence prediction, the sampling-moment target and the
population first-order value.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=.78\textheight,keepaspectratio]{focused_redesign_near_independence_different_skew_1000.pdf}
\caption{Component moments and degrees of freedom near independence.}
\label{fig:redesign-a4}
\end{figure}

At the smallest MI values in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4}, the top and
middle rows show that the near-independence predictions capture
the positive mean and sampling variance of \(\Vhat\) sustained by
sampling noise. In the bottom row, the quadratic sampling contribution
also explains why the mean estimated component degrees of freedom
remain several units, around half the moment target. The population
first-order degrees of freedom approach zero because they omit that
quadratic contribution.

In the bottom row, the moment target has a shallow initial dip before rising. Across
those early MI settings, the observed sampling variance grows faster
than the square of the observed mean, lowering their moment-matching
ratio. At larger MI the squared mean grows faster and the target rises.
The mean estimated degrees of freedom also rise towards the target,
so their initial factor-of-two separation does not persist across the
whole MI range.

Across the figure, stronger population association raises the observed
moments and degrees of freedom as MI increases. The dashed predictions become less
accurate as the terms omitted from the local expansion around
independence become more important. In particular, the approximation
\(\Vhat(P)\approx2\Ihat(P)\) increasingly understates the observed
mean and sampling variance of \(\Vhat(P)\), with a similar pattern for
\(Q\). The middle-row separation therefore reflects a loss of accuracy
in the local sampling-variance prediction.
Appendix~\ref{sec:component-diagnostics} gives the supporting
checks. The experimental supplement retains the full diagnostic plots.

Comparing the two columns, the local moment predictions are the same for \(P\) and \(Q\), since they
use the same table size, sample size and MI. The observed moments
separate as MI increases, with a larger sampling variance in \(Q\).
The different margins therefore matter beyond the quantities retained
in this local approximation. The discrepancy is greater in the
sampling-variance row than in the mean row in both columns.

In the bottom row, the near-independence prediction rises above the
mean estimated degrees of freedom as MI increases, while the
population first-order curve remains below the moment target. The
local substitutions \(\Vhat\approx2\Ihat\) and
\(\widehat\tau^2\approx4\Vhat\) increasingly understate the sampling
means of these inputs. Their combined error in the nonlinear degrees-of-freedom
formula gives an upward error in the predicted mean here, even though
the local moment predictions in the upper rows are too small.

Table~\ref{tab:variance-stability} compares two other predictions of
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\): the population first-order value
\(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) and the average sample-based value
\(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\). The same comparisons apply to \(Q\).
At the smallest MI target in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4}, the
population first-order predictions are only about 5\% of the observed
sampling variances. The sample-based predictions average roughly twice
the observed sampling variances. This upward error follows from the
local sample-based relation
\(\widehat\tau^2(P)\approx4\Vhat(P)\): sampling noise contributes
to both estimates, producing an inflated prediction of variation
(Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}).

At MI 0.02, the population first-order predictions of
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\) and \(\Var\{\Vhat(Q)\}\) are much more
accurate. The sample-based predictions still overestimate those
sampling variances, with ratios above one for both populations.
The population first-order variance calculation improves as association
strengthens, while the local near-independence calculation loses
accuracy. Underestimating the mean of \(\Vhat(P)\) still lowers
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\) relative to \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\),
and likewise for \(Q\).
Appendix~\ref{sec:component-diagnostics} reports the mean error across
MI and margins.

Table~\ref{tab:variance-stability} shows the continuation to MI 0.05:
all three moment ratios are close to one, and the mean estimated
degrees of freedom are closer to the moment target than the population
first-order values. Across the MI levels, \(Q\) has slightly smaller
mean ratios but also lower absolute component degrees of freedom.
Its sampling variance is greater relative to its squared sampling
mean, giving a lower moment target. Relative accuracy and the absolute
size of the degrees of freedom are therefore different comparisons.

\section{RQ2.1: False-positive rates under equal MI}

Under \(I(P)=I(Q)\), every rejection is a false positive.
Both tests use \(T=\Deltahat/\SEhat\), but Expanded Welch uses a
cutoff from the Student \(t\) distribution in place of Wald's standard
normal cutoff. At the common nominal p-value threshold, the larger cutoff
reduces rejection. We assess whether that reduction brings the false-positive
rate closer to the nominal 5\% level, and examine the shared estimates
to interpret the observed patterns.

\subsection{Overall false-positive patterns}
\label{sec:null-rejection-patterns}

Expanded Welch improves false-positive rates in selected settings where
Normal Wald rejects too often. In others, it further lowers already
conservative rates. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-null-grid} summarises the
120 settings at common MI 0.02 nats.

We classify Expanded Welch as improving nominal calibration at a setting
when its false-positive rate is closer to 5\% than Wald's by more than
\RedesignGridTolerance{} percentage points. It worsens nominal calibration
when its rate is farther from 5\% by more than \RedesignGridTolerance{}
percentage points. Differences within this tolerance are grouped separately. All
\RedesignToward{} improvements occur with Wald above 5\%;
\RedesignAwayBelow{} of the \RedesignAway{} deteriorations occur with
Wald below 5\%.

Moving farther from 5\% is more common than moving closer across this
grid. The larger cutoff therefore often intensifies conservatism,
even though it reduces excessive rejection at other settings.

Reported rejection rates divide valid rejections by all simulation
repetitions, counting invalid outputs as non-rejections. Brackets around
rates and shading in rejection plots give pointwise 95\% intervals for
uncertainty from the finite simulation batch.

Figures~\ref{fig:redesign-null-uniform} and
\ref{fig:redesign-null-different} show uniform and different-skew margins
at common MI 0.02 nats. Each table-size panel plots false-positive rates
against sample size, with a black line marking the 5\% target. Vertical
scales differ between panels, so comparisons between table sizes should
use the labelled rates. The lower strips show the fraction of valid
outputs. Because invalid results count as non-rejections, these strips
help identify rates that also reflect failed calculations.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_null_uniform.pdf}
\caption{False-positive rates under uniform margins.}
\label{fig:redesign-null-uniform}
\end{figure}

In Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-null-uniform}, uniform margins give
\(P=Q\), so the expected MI estimation biases
cancel in the difference at every plotted sample size. The four panels
therefore show how testing can fail even without difference bias.
Errors in the shared standard error and the tails of \(T\) are examined
in Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy}.

In the \(2\times2\) panel, both rates fall into a deep trough before
recovering towards 5\%. Expanded Welch is more conservative throughout
most of this range. The \(4\times4\) panel begins with excessive Wald
rejection, which Expanded Welch brings close to the target. Both then
become conservative, and the same larger cutoff moves Expanded Welch
farther below 5\%. The benefit of lowering rejection thus changes
within one panel as sample size increases.

The \(8\times8\) panel has an initial shoulder of excessive rejection,
followed by a later conservative trough and recovery. Expanded Welch
reduces the initial excess but deepens the trough. The \(16\times16\)
panel has a more irregular progression: early excess falls to around
5\%, rises again, and then gives way to conservatism before recovering.
These crossings accompany changes in whether the estimated standard
error understates or overstates sampling variation. Increasing sample
size does not make those errors shrink monotonically. Across the
larger-table panels, the methods draw close well before both rates
settle near 5\%, so agreement between tests does not establish good
calibration.

In Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-null-different}, the two smaller-table panels
also have a trough followed by
recovery. In \(2\times2\), even Wald remains below 5\% throughout the
grid, so Expanded Welch mainly increases conservatism. In
\(4\times4\), Wald starts above the target and then falls below it;
Expanded Welch lowers both regions. These shapes resemble their
uniform counterparts despite the different population tables.

The \(8\times8\) panel starts with much greater excessive rejection
than under uniform margins. Both methods eventually fall below 5\%
and then recover, but Expanded Welch remains visibly more conservative
through the intermediate range. The bias diagnostic in the next
subsection shows that its early improvement at B accompanies both a
shifted difference estimate and an overestimated squared standard error.
Lowering rejection alone does not identify which error is being offset.

In contrast, the \(16\times16\) panel shows severe
over-rejection across several intermediate sample sizes. The two
populations now have different cell probabilities, so their MI estimation
biases need not cancel. The bias in the difference grows relative to its
sampling SD over much of this range, shifting \(T\) away from zero.
Expanded Welch's larger cutoff reduces rejection but leaves this shift
unchanged. The bias later diminishes and rejection falls towards 5\%.
Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy} gives the supporting diagnostics.

At the smallest sample sizes in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-null-different},
closeness to 5\% can also accompany a loss
of usable results. For different-skew \(4\times4\) tables at \(n=10\),
Expanded Welch's rate is close to 5\%, but it produces valid outputs
in only about three quarters of repetitions. Across the full 120-setting
grid, the minimum valid fractions are 0.958 for Normal Wald and 0.631 for Expanded Welch.
The supplement reports rejection rates conditional on validity and the
included counts separately.

The validity strips in Figures~\ref{fig:redesign-null-uniform}
and~\ref{fig:redesign-null-different} distinguish these early failures from the later
patterns. Under uniform margins, appreciable losses are concentrated
in the smallest \(2\times2\) samples; the other strips stay near one.
Under different skew, several table sizes lose outputs at the smallest
sample sizes. The conservative troughs in \(4\times4\) and larger
tables, and the different-skew \(16\times16\) plateau, persist after
validity is complete. Failed calculations therefore cannot account for
those parts of the curves.

At the largest sample size in both figures, both tests' rates are close
to 5\% across all table-size panels. Better-estimated cell frequencies reduce the distortions
of the shared statistic, while larger estimated degrees of freedom bring
Expanded Welch's cutoff closer to Wald's. The curves therefore draw
together as well as approaching the target in these settings.

\subsection{Bias and standard-error accuracy}
\label{sec:null-estimator-accuracy}

RQ1 checked the moments of \(\Vhat(P)\) and \(\Vhat(Q)\) used to
choose degrees of freedom. We now check the shared estimates entering
\(T\): the centre of \(\Deltahat\) and whether \(\SEhat^2\) describes
its observed sampling variance. Because \(V(P)/n_P\) is itself a
first-order approximation, an upward error in \(\Vhat(P)\) relative to
\(V(P)\) can coexist with an underestimated standard error for the MI
difference.

Difference bias measures how far the average \(\Deltahat\) lies from
\(\Delta\). The variance ratio for \(\Deltahat\) divides the average
\(\SEhat^2\) by the observed \(\Var(\Deltahat)\). A ratio below one
means that the squared standard error underestimates sampling variance;
a ratio above one means that it overestimates sampling variance.
These diagnostics measure difference bias and average error in the
squared standard error, both of which can affect rejection.
Table~\ref{tab:redesign-shared-estimates}
compares the focus settings and the sparse \(16\times16\) cases at
\(n=1{,}000\). The full diagnostics, including the larger-sample
\(16\times16\) cases, remain in Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-diagnostics}.

In Table~\ref{tab:redesign-shared-estimates}, A's difference bias is
negligible, but the average \(\SEhat^2\) is
below the observed \(\Var(\Deltahat)\). At B, \(\Deltahat\) is
biased upwards and the average \(\SEhat^2\) exceeds the observed
\(\Var(\Deltahat)\). Expanded Welch lowers false positives in both
cases despite these different estimation errors. At C, bias is negligible
and the variance ratio is close to one. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-case-diagnostics}
gives the full diagnostics and degrees-of-freedom comparisons.

The sparse different-skew rows of Tables~\ref{tab:redesign-shared-estimates}
and~\ref{tab:redesign-case-rates} show that changing the degrees of freedom
does not resolve excessive rejection. At \(16\times16\), different skew and \(n=1{,}000\), the
difference bias is about 0.030 nats, or 2.06 observed SDs. Both tests
reject far too often; changing the rejection cutoff leaves the bias
unchanged.
The sampling centre is
\(\E(\Deltahat)=\Delta+\Bias(\Deltahat)\). Under the equal-MI null,
this gives
\begin{equation}
 \E(\Deltahat)\approx 0+0.02985=0.02985\text{ nats}.
 \label{eq:redesign-null-bias-shift}
\end{equation}
This shift is large relative to the observed SD of approximately 0.01450
nats. It moves most rejections into the positive tail even though the
population difference is zero.

The sparse same-skew and different-skew rows have almost the same
variance ratio in Table~\ref{tab:redesign-shared-estimates}, yet their
rejection rates lie on opposite sides of 5\%. The distinction is the
centre of the estimated difference. Under equal MI, the same-skew
populations have identical tables. With
equal sample sizes, their expected MI estimation biases therefore cancel
in the difference. The average \(\SEhat^2\) exceeds the observed
\(\Var(\Deltahat)\), and both rejection rates are far below 5\%.
Large difference bias accompanies excessive rejection in the different-skew
case; in the same-skew case, that bias cancels and both tests are conservative.
At \(n=10{,}000\), both skew regimes have much smaller estimation errors
and rates closer to 5\%.


The sparse-table failure also occurs under the four-cell construction.
At \(16\times16\), common MI 0.02 nats and
\(n_P=n_Q=1{,}000\), replacing copying by a probability transfer
within four cells preserves each population's margins and MI.
Under different-skew margins, the estimated difference remains
substantially biased and both false-positive rates are near 30\%.
The experimental supplement reports the full construction comparison.

The uniform-table curves in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-null-uniform}
help separate errors in \(\SEhat^2\) from difference bias. Across their
sample-size grid, the difference remains
centred near zero, but the average \(\SEhat^2\) can move from below
to above the observed \(\Var(\Deltahat)\). For a given estimated
difference, a smaller standard error increases the magnitude of \(T\).
Underestimating the sampling variance therefore helps explain excessive
rejection, while overestimating it can produce conservatism.
In the \(16\times16\) panel, the repeated
crossings accompany changes in the direction of this variance error.
The estimates entering the statistic recover at different rates as the
sparse sampled tables fill, so its rejection rate need not improve
monotonically.

In that figure's \(2\times2\) trough, average variance accuracy does
not fully determine the tails of \(T\). Sampled datasets with larger absolute MI
differences tend also to have larger standard errors. Dividing by those
larger standard errors reduces the extreme values of \(T\), helping
explain why rejection can be far below 5\% even when the average

without isolating every finite-sample contribution to rejection.

\section{RQ2.2: Nominal and calibrated power}

The power comparison uses A (uniform \(4\times4\), \(n_P=n_Q=20\)),
B (different-skew \(8\times8\), \(n_P=n_Q=100\)) and
C (different-skew \(4\times4\), \(n_P=n_Q=10{,}000\)). For each
setting, \(\sigma_0\) is the independently fitted SD of \(\Deltahat\)
under \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\) nats. We hold \(I(P)=0.02\) and increase
\(I(Q)\), so \(\Delta=I(P)-I(Q)\) is negative under the alternatives.
The horizontal axis is \(-\Delta/\sigma_0\): moving right increases
the MI advantage of \(Q\) in fitted null SD units.
Nominal power uses each test's usual 5\% cutoff; calibrated power uses
cutoffs fitted to target 5\% rejection at a specified equal-MI setting.

\subsection{Nominal power}

Expanded Welch has lower nominal power than Normal Wald at the selected
small-sample settings A and B (Table~\ref{tab:redesign-focus-settings}).
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-power} shows all three focus settings with MI
differences measured in fitted null standard deviations. The nominal
comparison includes their different null rejection rates at zero.
Appendix~\ref{sec:calibration-sensitivity-results} gives numerical rates
and differences between methods evaluated on the same simulated data.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_power.pdf}
\caption{Nominal power across differences measured in fitted null standard deviations.}
\label{fig:redesign-power}
\end{figure}

In panels A and C of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-power}, increasing \(I(Q)\) moves the sampling centre of
\(\Deltahat\) farther below zero. Its magnitude grows relative to
the standard error, so more repetitions cross the negative rejection
boundary and power rises. Expanded Welch's larger cutoff leaves some
of these repetitions unrejected, placing its curve below Wald's.

At A, the separation between methods is largest over the middle of the
curve. More repetitions then lie between the two rejection boundaries.
At stronger alternatives, more cross both boundaries, while the sampled
degrees of freedom increase and the cutoff from the Student \(t\)
distribution falls. The power gap consequently narrows again.

As the population MI values move farther apart at B, rejection initially
falls before rising. Positive estimation bias persists at the first alternatives. As
\(I(Q)\) increases, the negative true difference partly offsets this
bias, moving the sampling mean of \(\Deltahat\) towards zero. This
movement reduces positive-tail rejections before the negative difference
becomes large enough to produce many negative-tail rejections. This
change in rejection direction helps explain the dip before power rises.
Increasing the cutoff leaves this distortion of the shared statistic unchanged.
Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} reports the estimated directions
among rejections in the sparse-table comparisons.

B also remains the least powerful setting farther along the curve.
The positive estimation bias continues to offset part of the negative
population difference, while the observed SD of \(\Deltahat\) grows
relative to its null value. Both reduce the separation of the sampled
differences from zero relative to their sampling variation. At C,
bias remains negligible and the sampling SD changes less along the
alternative path, so power eventually exceeds A's as well as B's.
The shared horizontal unit \(\sigma_0\) standardises the null
variation; it does not make bias or alternative sampling variation
the same across the three panels.

At C, the estimated degrees of freedom are large enough that the
cutoff from the Student \(t\) distribution is close to the standard
normal cutoff. The methods therefore make nearly the same decisions,
and their power curves almost overlap. Table~\ref{tab:redesign-power-contrasts}
quantifies the small loss and reports comparisons at two null SDs for
all three focus settings.
Each interval uses both methods' decisions from the same simulation
repetitions to estimate their power difference. These are paired
comparisons: the pairing is between methods applied to the same data.
The calibrated comparison then assesses power after matching null rejection.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Power after matching false-positive rates}

At settings A and B, Expanded Welch's lower nominal power accompanies a
lower false-positive rate than Normal Wald's. We now ask whether its
power remains lower after adjusting both tests to have approximately the
same false-positive rate.

Empirical calibration fits a separate p-value threshold for each test
using independent equal-MI simulations to target 5\% rejection. These
replace the usual threshold of 0.05. We hold them fixed, check their
false-positive rates on
independent equal-MI samples, and apply them to the same alternative
samples used in the nominal comparison. The MI difference \(\Deltahat\),
standard error \(\SEhat\) and calculated p-values remain unchanged.

The earlier nesting of rejections applied at a common p-value threshold.
With separately fitted thresholds, differences in how the tests rank
sampled datasets can give Expanded Welch greater calibrated power.

We first fit the thresholds at \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\) nats. On independent
null evaluation samples, both tests reject between 4.93\% and 4.99\%
across A--C, so their false-positive rates are closely matched there.
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-calibrated-power} shows rejection under these
thresholds: at zero on the horizontal axis it measures false positives;
to the right, it measures power against increasing MI differences.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_power_calibrated.pdf}
\caption{Power after calibration at common MI 0.02; bands show bootstrap uncertainty.}
\label{fig:redesign-calibrated-power}
\end{figure}

In panel A of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-calibrated-power}, calibration
mainly lowers Wald's curve: its nominal false-positive
rate was above 5\%, whereas Expanded Welch's was already close to the
target. Expanded Welch now has higher power over the alternatives
shown, reversing the nominal ordering. Its advantage grows over the
middle of the curve and narrows slightly at the largest difference.

At B, both curves move down because both nominal false-positive rates
were above 5\%. Expanded Welch now has greater power over the positive
horizontal-axis values, with a larger advantage at the stronger
alternatives. Nevertheless, both curves retain the initial dip.
Calibration leaves the biased \(\Deltahat\) unchanged, so reversing
the method ordering does not remove the distortion in the shared
statistic.

At C, calibration makes little change because both nominal
false-positive rates were already close to 5\%. The curves continue to
overlap and rise steeply towards high power. The cross-panel ordering
at stronger alternatives also remains: C is most powerful and B least
powerful, consistent with their different bias and sampling-variation
patterns.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Does the calibration setting change the comparison?}

The first comparison matches false-positive rates at common MI 0.02.
We next ask whether Expanded Welch's power advantage persists when the
thresholds are fitted at a different common MI.

For an alternative with \(I(P)=0.02\) and \(I(Q)>0.02\), we compare:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Lower-null calibration:} fit both tests at
  \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\), the alternative's smaller MI.
  \item \textbf{Upper-null calibration:} fit both tests with their
  population MI values equal to the alternative's larger \(I(Q)\).
\end{itemize}
Both calibration settings retain the alternative's margins and sample
sizes. Applying both sets of thresholds to the same alternative samples
ensures that only the thresholds change the power comparison.

percentage points: positive values favour Expanded Welch and negative

for the shared evaluation data and uncertainty from fitting thresholds.

\section{Application to online shopping data}
\label{sec:shoppers-application}

The Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention dataset records website sessions
and whether each session ends in a purchase \citep{sakar2018}. We use it
to address two application questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.},leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Month:} Does month provide the same amount of information
  about purchasing among new and returning visitors?
  \item \textbf{Weekend:} Does weekend status provide the same amount of
  information about purchasing among new and returning visitors?
\end{enumerate}

The dataset contains 12,330 sessions, with each session belonging to a
different user according to the source documentation \citep{sakar2018}.
The analysis retains all 1,694 sessions labelled \texttt{New\_Visitor}
and 10,551 labelled \texttt{Returning\_Visitor}, excluding the 85
\texttt{Other} sessions. The outcome is the original Boolean
\texttt{Revenue} variable: true indicates a purchase and false indicates
no purchase. The tests treat the two disjoint visitor groups as independent
samples and the sessions within each group as independent observations.

For each question, \(X\) is month or weekend status and \(Y\) is the
purchase outcome. Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be the joint distributions of
\((X,Y)\) among new and returning visitors, respectively. Each question
tests \(H_0:I(P)=I(Q)\).

\begingroup
\setlength{\intextsep}{4pt}
\setlength{\abovecaptionskip}{4pt}
For month, each group forms a \(10\times2\) count table. The rows are
the ten recorded months: February, March, May, June, July, August,
September, October, November and December. The columns are no purchase
and purchase. Both groups use these categories, with no smoothing or
removal of zero cells. All ten months occur in both groups, although the
new-visitor February row contains only one session and no purchase.
Replacing month with the Boolean \texttt{Weekend} variable gives a
\(2\times2\) table for each group, with weekday and weekend rows;
all four cells are observed.

The leading MI bias correction subtracts
\((10-1)(2-1)/(2n)=9/(2n)\) from each month plug-in estimate
\citep{miller1955,moddemeijer1989}, and \(1/(2n)\) from each weekend
estimate, where \(n\) is the relevant group size. The corrected
difference \(\Deltahat\) is new-visitor MI minus returning-visitor MI.
Table~\ref{tab:shoppers-application} gives these differences alongside
their shared standard errors, \(\SEhat\), and the resulting
\(T=\Deltahat/\SEhat\). MI estimates, differences and standard errors
are in nats.

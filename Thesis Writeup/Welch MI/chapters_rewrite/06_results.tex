\chapter{Experimental Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results}

The results follow the three experimental questions: when the tests give
reliable false-positive rates, how association strength affects the
adjustment, and how often a true MI difference is detected. Each rate uses
20,000 sampled table pairs. Blue solid curves show Normal Wald and magenta
dashed curves show Expanded Welch. Error bars or bands give pointwise
Wilson 95\% intervals, and the dotted line marks 0.05. Hollow markers
indicate valid-result frequency below 90\%. The parameter settings are
those in Chapter~\ref{chap:design}; additional comparisons and the full
120-setting null table are in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

\section{False positives across sample size and population structure}

\subsection{Increasing table size}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-null-sizes} holds both population MI values at
0.02 nats and compares the four table sizes under different skew.
The dominant marginal probabilities are 0.7 for \(P\) and 0.8 for \(Q\),
with the remaining mass shared equally across their other categories.
Each panel varies only sample size, from 10 to 50,000 per population.
The lower strip shows how often each method returns a valid p-value.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_null_sizes.pdf}
\caption{False positives and validity under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:focused-null-sizes}
\end{figure}

The false-positive pattern changes substantially with table size. In
\(2\times2\) tables both methods reject too rarely over much of the
sample-size range. In \(4\times4\) tables Wald is above 0.05 at
\(n=10\) and 20, but both methods fall below the target at larger
intermediate sample sizes. The \(8\times8\) comparison makes this
transition later. Expanded Welch lowers rejection throughout, helping
where Wald is excessive and moving an already-low rate further below 0.05.

At the smallest sample size, part of that reduction involves lost
validity. For \(4\times4\) tables at \(n=10\), Wald rejects at 0.1332
and Expanded Welch at 0.0493, but their valid-result frequencies are
0.9910 and 0.7776. Restricting both tests to the 15,552 common-valid
pairs gives rejection rates 0.1053 and 0.0633. The Student \(t\)
distribution still reduces rejection on those same pairs, but the
unconditional rate near 0.05 also reflects failed calculations.
Validity and calibration therefore answer different questions.

The \(16\times16\) table shows that valid calculations need not be
well calibrated. At \(n=1{,}000\), every calculation is valid, yet Wald
rejects at 0.2522 and Expanded Welch at 0.2402. Their Wilson intervals
are approximately [0.2462, 0.2583] and [0.2343, 0.2462]. The paired
reduction is 0.0120, with approximate 95\% interval [0.0105, 0.0135],
so the adjustment lowers rejection but leaves a large excess of false
positives. At \(n=10{,}000\) the rates are 0.0463 and 0.0453;
at 50,000 they are 0.0477 and 0.0474. More observations substantially
improve this setting, although the path is not a simple steady decline.
The next comparison asks how much of this pattern changes with the margins.

\subsection{Changing the margin regime}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-null-margins} compares all three margin regimes
at \(4\times4\) and \(16\times16\), retaining the common MI target
and sample-size sweep. Uniform and same-skew populations have identical
joint distributions within each pair. Different-skew populations have
different tables but equal MI. Comparing the columns shows how uneven
category frequencies and different population margins change the test.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_null_margins.pdf}
\caption{False positives and validity across three margin regimes.}
\label{fig:focused-null-margins}
\end{figure}

At \(4\times4\), all three regimes give low rejection at intermediate
sample sizes and rates much nearer 0.05 by \(n=10{,}000\).
At \(n=1{,}000\), for example, Wald rates range from 0.0383 to
0.0394, while Expanded Welch ranges from 0.0319 to 0.0331.
Changing the margins has a relatively small effect on these particular
rates, and both methods are fully valid. The larger table behaves
differently: at \(n=1{,}000\), Wald rejects at 0.0339 under uniform
margins, 0.0065 under same skew, and 0.2522 under different skew.
Expanded Welch gives 0.0331, 0.0059 and 0.2402, respectively.
The different-skew excess is therefore not a general consequence of
uneven margins alone.

The shared estimates help explain this contrast.
Table~\ref{tab:focused-diagnostics} reports the four preselected
different-skew null settings. Observed SD is the sample standard deviation
of the corrected MI difference. Estimated SD is the square root of the
mean squared standard error, rather than the mean standard error. The
variance ratio divides the latter variance estimate by the observed
variance. All four settings have full validity and finite diagnostics.

The \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) difference has bias 0.02960 nats,
more than twice its observed SD of approximately 0.01448. To see where
this shift comes from, recall that bias is the mean estimate across
samples minus the population value. The bias of a difference is exactly
the first estimate's bias minus the second estimate's bias. Here the
individual corrected MI biases are approximately \(-0.00794\) for \(P\)
and \(-0.03754\) for \(Q\). Since the true difference is zero, the
estimated difference is centred at
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb{E}(\Deltahat)
 \simeq 0+(-0.00794)-(-0.03754)=0.02960
 \quad\text{nats}.
 \label{eq:focused-null-bias-shift}
\end{equation}
Both corrected estimates are too low on average, but \(Q\)'s is lower
by more. Subtracting it therefore creates a positive difference even
though the population MI values match. The common leading correction
cancels because the table and sample sizes match; it cannot remove the
remaining difference in biases. This is the finite-sample problem
highlighted by \citet{paninski2003}: a leading bias correction need not
remove the error in sparsely sampled information estimates.

On average, 147 of \(P\)'s 256 cells and 183 of \(Q\)'s cells
are empty. The shifted difference and sparse sampling occur together,
while the observed individual biases directly identify the source of
the shift.

Estimated uncertainty is also inaccurate in this setting: mean squared
standard error is about 1.94 times the observed variance. Excessive false
positives nevertheless remain, because a large standard error does not
restore a difference estimate centred away from zero. At \(n=10{,}000\)
the bias is much smaller and the variance ratio is 1.07, accompanying
the improved null rate. The population first-order variance is about
0.60 times the observed variance at \(n=1{,}000\), compared with 0.84
at 10,000, showing that the approximation itself also needs attention.
These diagnostics describe distinct errors; they do not divide the
rejection rate into additive contributions. Expanded Welch changes none
of these shared estimates. Its separate role is to change the rejection
cutoff, which the association-strength study examines next.

\section{Association strength and degrees of freedom}
\label{sec:mechanism-results}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-association} keeps \(4\times4\) different-skew
tables and varies the common population MI from 0.0001 to 0.05 nats.
The two columns use \(n=100\) and 1,000. Upper panels show false
positives; lower panels show the median estimated combined degrees of
freedom, with the 5th--95th percentile range shaded. Every calculation
in this sweep is valid, so the low rejection rates do not reflect
missing test outputs.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_association.pdf}
\caption{Association strength, false positives and estimated degrees of freedom.}
\label{fig:focused-association}
\end{figure}

Both tests reject too rarely near independence, and Expanded Welch
rejects still less. At common MI 0.0001 and \(n=1{,}000\), Wald's rate
is 0.0101 and Expanded Welch's is 0.0044. At common MI 0.05, the rates
are 0.0472 and 0.0438. Stronger association brings the rates closer to
0.05 in this comparison, especially at the larger sample size. The
\(n=100\) rates remain low even at the largest MI target, so a larger
MI value does not substitute for enough observations.

Estimated degrees of freedom increase with association strength. At
\(n=1{,}000\), their median rises from 8.62 at MI 0.0001 to 78.24
at MI 0.05; at \(n=100\), it rises from 4.23 to 13.80. Smaller
degrees of freedom give a larger cutoff from the Student \(t\)
distribution, requiring a larger absolute statistic for rejection.
The near-independence calculation in Section~\ref{sec:near-independence-mechanism}
explains why small positive MI can make this adjustment restrictive.
Here it acts on a Wald test that is already conservative.

The median degrees of freedom illustrate the change in cutoff. For a
two-sided 5\% test, the cutoffs from the Student \(t\) distribution
are approximately 2.277 at 8.62 degrees of freedom and 1.991 at 78.24,
compared with Normal Wald's 1.960. Thus \(|T|=2.1\) would reject under
Wald but not under Expanded Welch with 8.62 degrees of freedom; at
78.24 both would reject. These examples use the reported medians.
Each sampled table pair uses its own estimated degrees of freedom.

The estimation diagnostics show that the reference distribution is not
the only issue. At MI 0.0001 and \(n=1{,}000\), mean squared standard
error is 1.74 times the observed variance. The population first-order
variance is only about 0.04 times that variance, indicating that the
first-order approximation is poor this close to independence at this
sample size. These ratios compare two different calculations. The
population calculation uses the true probabilities in \(V(P)/n_P+V(Q)/n_Q\).
The estimated calculation replaces them by each sample's observed
proportions before averaging the resulting squared standard errors.
They need not have the same error.

Near independence, the true first-order variance is small because the
population cell probabilities are close to the products of their
margins. A finite sample can still show apparent association: observed
cell proportions generally differ from the products of their observed
margins. The plug-in calculation treats those departures as features
of an estimated population table and can therefore assign a substantially
larger first-order variance. Meanwhile, the linear approximation at the
true table omits the quadratic sampling terms that matter near
independence. \citet{brillinger2004} distinguishes these first-order and
independence calculations; Section~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}
shows why the quadratic terms remain when the first-order term vanishes.
Thus a population first-order variance that is too small can coexist
with a plug-in variance estimate that is too large. The observed ratios
show both errors in this setting.

At MI 0.05 the estimated/observed ratio is approximately
1.00 and the population/observed ratio is 0.95. Detailed values are in
Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}. Improving the cutoff alone cannot
correct errors in the statistic being compared with it. We now examine
what that means when the populations have different MI values.

\section{Detection when population MI differs}

\subsection{Detection across table sizes}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-detection-sizes} fixes \(I(P)=0.02\) nats,
sets \(I(Q)=0.02+|\Delta|\), and uses different-skew margins. Each
panel compares \(n=1{,}000\) and 10,000 at one table size. The figure
enlarges differences up to 0.02 nats; the full grid through 0.2 is in
Figure~\ref{fig:focused-detection-full}. Every displayed detection
setting is fully valid.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_detection_sizes.pdf}
\caption{Detection over small MI differences under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:focused-detection-sizes}
\end{figure}

For the smaller tables, increasing the true difference generally
increases detection, and larger samples make a clear improvement.
In \(4\times4\) tables, a 0.01-nat difference is detected at rates
0.1132 and 0.1013 by Wald and Expanded Welch at \(n=1{,}000\),
compared with 0.7962 and 0.7940 at \(n=10{,}000\). The adjustment
costs some detection at the smaller sample size and very little at the
larger one. This follows its larger degrees of freedom in larger samples.
The null points remain essential: these curves compare tests at the
same nominal threshold, not at equal achieved false-positive rates.

The \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) curve instead falls initially as
the true difference grows. Wald rejects at 0.2522 under equal MI,
0.1005 at a difference of 0.01, and 0.0363 at 0.02. This is not
improved calibration under those alternatives. The positive bias
identified earlier opposes the negative true difference. The mean
estimated difference is approximately 0.02960 at the null. Under the
next two alternatives, the true difference and estimated difference
bias combine as follows:
\begin{align}
 \mathbb{E}(\Deltahat)&\simeq-0.01+0.03061=0.02061,\\
 \mathbb{E}(\Deltahat)&\simeq-0.02+0.03164=0.01164.
 \label{eq:focused-detection-bias-shift}
\end{align}
The bias changes slightly between these populations, but remains
positive and larger than the magnitude of the true difference. As the
true difference becomes more negative, it offsets more of that bias.
The centre of the estimated difference therefore moves towards zero
rather than away from it. Dividing by the estimated standard error
can then give smaller absolute statistics, despite the larger population
difference. The declining rejection rate is a consequence of comparing
biased estimates, not evidence that the two population MI values have
become more similar. The full-range comparison
shows rejection rising again at larger differences.

At \(n=10{,}000\), the \(16\times16\) bias is much smaller, and
detection increases across the small differences. Wald detects a
0.01-nat difference at 0.6992 and Expanded Welch at 0.6968; by 0.02
the rates are 0.9932 and 0.9931. The larger sample therefore improves
both the null behaviour and the detection pattern. High rejection in
the smaller sample was not evidence of a more sensitive test.

\subsection{Detection across margin regimes}

Figure~\ref{fig:focused-detection-margins} fixes the table size at
\(4\times4\), retains the same MI targets, and compares the three
margin regimes at the two sample sizes. This separates their effect
on detection from the table-size comparison.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{focused_detection_margins.pdf}
\caption{Detection over small MI differences in \(4\times4\) tables.}
\label{fig:focused-detection-margins}
\end{figure}

Uniform margins give somewhat higher detection in these settings.
At \(n=10{,}000\) and a 0.01-nat difference, Wald detects at 0.8635
under uniform margins, 0.7790 under same skew, and 0.7962 under
different skew. Expanded Welch gives 0.8619, 0.7768 and 0.7940.
The margin-related change is larger than the difference between tests
at this sample size.

The observed precision helps explain the detection ordering. For this
0.01-nat difference at \(n=10{,}000\), the sample SD of the estimated
difference is approximately 0.00327 nats under uniform margins, 0.00364
under same skew and 0.00361 under different skew. The biases are small
in all three settings, and the square roots of the mean squared standard
errors are close to these observed SDs. The same population difference
is therefore easier to distinguish from sampling variation under the
uniform construction. Equal MI targets do not imply equal precision:
the cell probabilities also determine the variance in
Equation~\eqref{eq:background-two-sample-variance}. This connects the
margin comparison to the first-order MI variance, rather than to the
choice of rejection cutoff alone.

At \(n=1{,}000\), the adjustment produces a
more visible detection reduction, while all three null points are
already below 0.05. By a difference of 0.2 nats, both methods reject
every simulated pair in all three regimes at both sample sizes.

The supporting four-cell check gives the same qualitative comparison
at its matched \(4\times4\) settings: the adjustment lowers an
already-low null rate at \(n=1{,}000\), and changes detection little
at 10,000. Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} gives the rates and
diagnostics. This agreement supports the representative comparison
across those two constructions, while the main curves retain their
specific copying populations.

\section{Computation and practical implications}

Table~\ref{tab:focused-runtime} reports scalar-call timings on an arm64
machine running macOS 26.6.2, Python 3.13.5, NumPy 2.4.4 and SciPy 1.17.1.
Each setting uses 200 pre-generated different-skew null table pairs at
\(n=1{,}000\), 20 warm-up calls per method and five timed passes.
Calls are timed individually, the method order alternates between passes,
and the table shows the median and interquartile range of 1,000 calls.

Expanded Welch costs approximately 1.7--1.8 times as much in these timings.
Both methods require \(O(k^2)\) work for a \(k\times k\) table, but
fixed call overhead can be important for these small arrays. The statistical
trade-off is more consequential than this modest absolute cost: the
adjustment helps only when its lower rejection improves an excessive
false-positive rate without too much loss of detection or validity.
Larger samples reduce several shared estimation errors, whereas changing
the reference distribution alone leaves those errors in place.

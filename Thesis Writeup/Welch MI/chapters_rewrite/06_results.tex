\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}
\input{figures_rewrite/focused_redesign_reporting_values}

This chapter checks the approximations used to choose Expanded Welch's
degrees of freedom, then compares its false-positive rates and power with
Wald. The online-shopping application illustrates how to interpret
the test outputs.

Each evaluation setting uses 100,000 independent repetitions, each drawing
one count table from \(P\) and one independently from \(Q\). Wald
and Expanded Welch are both applied to the same two sampled tables in
each repetition.

\section{RQ1: Accuracy of variance approximations}

For a fixed population table \(P\) and sample size \(n_P\), each
repetition produces an estimate \(\Vhat(P)\) of the pointwise-MI variance
\(V(P)\). We first check predictions for its sampling mean,
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\), and its variation between repetitions,
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\), because both moments enter the component
degrees-of-freedom calculation. We then examine how errors in these
moments affect the degrees of freedom, before checking the approximations
as the populations approach independence.
Table~\ref{tab:results-variance-quantities} in
Chapter~\ref{chap:baselines} distinguishes these quantities from the
sampling variance of the MI difference.
The figures focus on populations with equal MI but different margins,
allowing approximation accuracy to be compared between distinct population
tables.

\subsection{Mean and sampling-variance predictions}

Equation~\eqref{eq:redesign-moment-approximations} predicts
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx V(P)\) and
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx\tau^2(P)/n_P\), where \(\tau^2(P)\)
measures the variability of the observation-level sensitivities derived
in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}. We compare each prediction with the
corresponding moment observed across repetitions, and repeat the checks
for \(Q\).

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments} shows the different-skew populations
at shared MI 0.02 nats, with row and column margins \(m_k(0.7)\) for
\(P\) and \(m_k(0.8)\) for \(Q\).
The top row divides the simulated mean of
\(\Vhat(P)\) by the population benchmark \(V(P)\), and likewise for \(Q\).
The middle row divides \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) by the observed
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\). The bottom row uses the average
\(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\) as its numerator and the same observed
variance as its denominator. Both variance rows use the same vertical
scale, so their differences show the effect of substituting sample
proportions. A ratio of one indicates agreement. Shading in the first
two rows shows approximate 95\% Monte Carlo
intervals from 100,000 repetitions. Their half-widths are below 1.3\%
of the plotted values, making the bands barely visible on this scale.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_moment_predictions_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Mean and sampling-variance ratios under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:redesign-moments}
\end{figure}

In the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}, larger tables have
higher peaks in the mean ratio and take more observations to approach
one. The \(2\times2\) curve falls towards one without a
pronounced peak, whereas the \(4\times4\), \(8\times8\) and
\(16\times16\) curves first rise and then fall. The \(4\times4\)
peak is smaller and earlier than those for the two larger tables.
The rise in these curves occurs as rare combinations that were initially
unobserved begin to appear in sampled tables. Cells observed only once
can have estimated pointwise MI far from the table's plug-in MI,
producing large squared deviations in \(\Vhat\). For example, if a
cell and its row and column each contain one observation, its empirical
pointwise MI is \(\log n\). A cell-contribution decomposition using
10,000 repetitions for the \(16\times16\) populations shows that, at
\(n=50\), once-observed cells account for about 88\% of the mean of
\(\Vhat\) in \(P\) and 92\% in \(Q\)
(Appendix~\ref{sec:estimation-diagnostics}).
Their contribution falls as repeated counts give better estimates of
the cell probabilities. Larger tables spread probability over more rare
combinations under these margin rules, so this distortion persists to
larger sample sizes. Both populations show these patterns, but the
larger-table peaks are higher in \(P\) than in \(Q\), and the
\(16\times16\) peak occurs later in \(Q\). For each of these larger
table sizes, the peak mean of \(\Vhat\) is greater in \(P\) than in
\(Q\), while \(V(P)<V(Q)\). Both the larger numerator and the smaller
denominator therefore raise \(P\)'s peak ratio. Equal MI fixes the population mean of pointwise MI, whereas its variance
\(V\) also depends on the cell-specific pointwise-MI values and their
probability weights, which change with the margins.

In the middle row, the \(2\times2\) curves have shallow troughs,
while the larger tables have progressively deeper troughs and take
longer to approach one. Ratios below one mean that \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\)
understates the observed \(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\), with the same
interpretation for \(Q\). The dip develops because, for a fixed
population, the predicted variance decreases exactly as \(1/n\), while
the observed sampling variance does not initially follow that decrease.
At small sample sizes, many rare combinations remain unobserved. As
\(n\) increases, some sampled tables contain a given rare cell while
others still miss it. An unobserved cell contributes zero to \(\Vhat\),
whereas a single observation can give a large squared deviation of its
pointwise MI from the table's plug-in MI. The \(1/n\) change in a cell
proportion can be large relative to its true probability, so its effect
on \(\Vhat\) can be poorly represented by linearisation around the
population table. The observed variance can therefore
initially rise, or fall more slowly than \(1/n\), while the predicted variance decreases, so the ratio then falls. In the same \(16\times16\)
repetitions, subtracting the linear change from \(\Vhat-V\) leaves
a remainder whose variance exceeds that of the linear change around the
trough. The difference between the variances of the full error and the
linear change equals the remainder's variance plus twice its covariance
with the linear change (Appendix~\ref{sec:estimation-diagnostics},
Equation~\eqref{eq:linear-remainder-variance-decomposition}). Around the
trough, this combined contribution remains large and positive, so the
linear change alone misses much of the observed variation.
With larger samples, repeated
counts make the estimated cell probabilities and their log ratios more
stable. The variation missed by the linear approximation becomes smaller
relative to \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\), and the observed variance approaches
this prediction, so the ratio rises back towards one. Under these margin
rules, larger tables contain more rare combinations and require more
observations before this approximation becomes accurate. The observed
variance measures spread about the sampling mean, so it can be predicted
accurately while that mean remains above the population benchmark, as
in the top row. The same ordering by table size appears in \(P\) and
\(Q\), but the \(16\times16\) trough occurs later in \(Q\), where the
rarer row and column categories have lower probabilities and accumulate
observations more slowly.

In the bottom row, the \(4\times4\), \(8\times8\) and
\(16\times16\) sample-based predictions first underestimate the
observed sampling variance, then overestimate it before approaching
agreement. Their ratios begin below one, rise through one to a peak,
and then fall towards one, although the \(16\times16\) curves initially
dip further before rising. The \(4\times4\) overshoot is smaller and
earlier than those for the two larger tables, while \(16\times16\)
has the largest overshoot and takes longest to settle near one.
The \(2\times2\) curves begin above one and settle near it sooner.
Substituting sample proportions changes the sensitivities used to
estimate \(\tau^2\). Around the overshoot, cells observed only once
contribute strongly to \(\widehat\tau^2\) as well as to \(\Vhat\),
so the averaged sample-based prediction can exceed the observed
sampling variance even while the middle-row population prediction
remains below it. Sample substitution can therefore reverse the
direction of the prediction error. Both populations show this
progression, but for the three larger table sizes the initial
underprediction is stronger in \(Q\), and the crossing above one
occurs later than in \(P\). The subsequent peaks are higher in \(P\)
than in \(Q\).

\subsection{How moment errors affect degrees of freedom}

We now examine how the moment errors in Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}
affect the component degrees of freedom for \(P\) and \(Q\), using the
three quantities and inputs distinguished in Table~\ref{tab:component-df-roles}. At each setting, the observed
mean and sampling variance of \(\Vhat(P)\) across 100,000 repetitions
give the moment target \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\) in
Equation~\eqref{eq:design-moment-target}. The population first-order value
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\) instead uses \(V(P)\) and \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\),
while the sample-based comparison calculates \(\widehat\nu_P\) from
each sampled table before averaging these estimates.


Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df} uses the different-skew populations
at shared MI 0.02 nats, with margins \(m_k(0.7)\) for \(P\) and
\(m_k(0.8)\) for \(Q\). Sample size increases along the horizontal axis,
and the lines represent table sizes. The top row divides
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\) by \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\); the bottom row divides
\(\E(\widehat\nu_P)\) by the same target, and likewise for \(Q\).
On the shared vertical scale, one indicates agreement, while values below
or above one indicate fewer or more degrees of freedom than the target. A bottom-row value is shown only when all 100,000 component
estimates are positive and finite. Shading in the top row gives approximate
95\% Monte Carlo intervals.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{focused_redesign_component_df_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Component degrees-of-freedom approximations relative to their moment targets.}
\label{fig:redesign-component-df}
\end{figure}

In the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-component-df}, the first-order
values remain below the moment targets over most of the sample-size range.
The \(2\times2\) curves approach one first, followed by \(4\times4\);
the \(8\times8\) and \(16\times16\) curves remain lower for longer.
This pattern arises because the two moment errors enter different parts
of the degrees-of-freedom calculation. Replacing the enlarged sampling
mean of \(\Vhat(P)\) by the smaller \(V(P)\) lowers the squared numerator.
A variance prediction below the observed sampling variance lowers the
denominator and raises the resulting degrees of freedom; a prediction
above the observed variance lowers them. Over most of the range, the
reduction from the squared mean outweighs the upward effect of an
underestimated denominator. The larger tables have greater mean errors in
Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}, so their first-order degrees of freedom
remain farther below the target. As the moment approximations improve,
both effects diminish and the ratios approach one. Although the same table-size ordering appears in \(P\) and \(Q\),
the larger-table ratios are generally closer to one in \(Q\), whose
smaller normalised mean
overshoot produces a smaller downward error in the squared numerator.

A numerical example shows why a fairly accurate variance prediction can
still give degrees of freedom below the target. For different-skew
\(4\times4\) population \(P\) at \(n=1{,}000\) and MI 0.02, the
sampling mean of \(\Vhat(P)\) is about 19\% above \(V(P)\), whereas
\(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) is only about 3\% below the observed sampling variance.
The first-order calculation uses \(V(P)\) in place of the larger sampling
mean and squares their ratio, giving approximately
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}}{\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}}
  \approx\frac{(1/1.19)^2}{0.97}
  \approx0.73.
\end{equation*}
The squared mean ratio therefore dominates the modest variance error,
consistent with first-order degrees of freedom of 15.30 compared with a
moment target of 21.03.

In the bottom row, the \(16\times16\) curves first appear above the target,
fall below it, and then recover towards one. The \(4\times4\) and
\(8\times8\) curves have smaller excursions, while the \(2\times2\)
curves are already close to one when they become visible. This change
from the top row occurs because each sampled table supplies both the
squared numerator \(\Vhat(P)^2\) and the variance prediction
\(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\). At the first shown \(16\times16\) points, the variance prediction is
too small on average, tending to raise the estimated degrees of freedom.
As the prediction subsequently becomes too large, it tends to lower
the estimated degrees of freedom before the variance prediction returns
towards the observed sampling variance. The two inputs come from the same table, so their joint variation also
affects the mean degrees of freedom when we average the sampled ratios
rather than insert average inputs into a single ratio.
With larger samples, both inputs become more stable and the mean estimated
degrees of freedom approach the target. They can recover sooner than the
population first-order values because their numerators use the sampled
\(\Vhat\), rather than the smaller fixed \(V\). For the three larger
tables, the curves appear later in \(Q\) because positive, finite component
degrees of freedom in every repetition require more observations than in \(P\).
Missing earlier points indicate failed component calculations in some
repetitions, rather than zero degrees of freedom.

\subsection{Behaviour near independence}

When \(X\) and \(Y\) are nearly independent within each population, the
pointwise-MI weights in the linear sampling error become small, so products
of cell-probability errors can contribute substantially to sampling variation. These quadratic terms allow sampled
tables to show apparent association even when population MI is almost
zero. The near-independence calculation in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} retains them to predict the mean
and sampling variance of \(\Vhat\) and the mean estimated component
degrees of freedom.

Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4} holds the sample size at \(n=1{,}000\) and
the table size at \(4\times4\), and increases the shared population MI
along the horizontal axis. The margins are \(m_k(0.7)\) for \(P\) and
\(m_k(0.8)\) for \(Q\). The top row multiplies the observed sampling
mean of \(\Vhat\) by \(n\); the middle row multiplies its observed
sampling variance by \(n^2\). These scalings cancel the sample-size
denominators in the near-independence moment formulas in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}. The dashed near-independence
predictions
use the same scalings and depend on the shared table size, sample size
and MI, so they are identical in the two columns. The bottom row compares
the mean estimated degrees of freedom, its near-independence prediction,
the sampling-moment target and the population first-order value. Across the simulation repetitions, we calculate the observed means and
variances and average the degrees of freedom estimated separately from
each sampled table.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=.78\textheight,keepaspectratio]{focused_redesign_near_independence_different_skew_1000.pdf}
\caption{Component moments and degrees of freedom near independence.}
\label{fig:redesign-a4}
\end{figure}

In the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4}, the observed scaled mean
and its near-independence prediction nearly coincide at the smallest MI
values, then both rise as association strengthens, with the observed mean
eventually exceeding the prediction. The positive starting level occurs because
sampling noise produces positive plug-in MI and \(\Vhat\) even when
population association is almost absent. The local calculation includes
this noise contribution through \(\Vhat\approx2\Ihat\). As MI increases,
the cell log ratios move farther from their values at independence, and
the omitted terms in the local expansion become more important. Checks
on the sampled tables show that \(2\Ihat\) then increasingly understates
\(\Vhat\). The observed means also separate between the columns, with
\(Q\) higher at the larger MI values. The identical local predictions
cannot capture this margin dependence because they retain only the
shared table size, sample size and MI.

In the middle row, the observed scaled sampling variance also starts
close to the near-independence prediction, but their separation grows
more strongly as MI increases. For the same \(4\times4\), different-skew
comparisons at \(n=1{,}000\), checks across increasing shared MI values
trace this underprediction through the two steps of the local calculation.
The calculation first
represents \(\Vhat\) by \(2\Ihat\), then predicts the sampling variance
of that quantity using a noncentral chi-squared distribution. At stronger association,
\(2\Ihat\) varies less between samples than \(\Vhat\), while the local
distribution also understates the observed variation in \(2\Ihat\)
itself. Both errors therefore lower the predicted sampling variance
relative to the observed \(\Var\{\Vhat\}\).
The gap is larger than in the mean row in both columns, and the observed
sampling variance is greater in \(Q\) than in \(P\) at the larger MI
values.
Thus the different margins affect sampling variation beyond the terms
retained by the shared local prediction.

In the bottom row, the mean estimated degrees of freedom remain several
units at the smallest MI values, around half the sampling-moment target,
while the population first-order value approaches zero. This separation
arises because the sample-based calculation retains apparent association
from sampling noise. Substituting \(\Vhat\approx2\Ihat\) and
\(\widehat\tau^2\approx4\Vhat\) into the component degrees-of-freedom
formula gives
\(\widehat\nu_P\approx n_P\Ihat(P)\), whose average stays positive at
fixed sample size even when \(I(P)\) is almost zero. The population
first-order value instead approaches \(n_PI(P)\), which becomes very
small. The moment target has a shallow initial dip because its observed
sampling variance initially grows faster in proportion than its squared
sampling mean. Their relative growth later reverses and the target rises,
with the mean estimated degrees of freedom approaching it. At stronger
association, the dashed near-independence prediction rises above the
observed mean estimated degrees of freedom, while the population
first-order curve remains below the moment target. The near-independence
calculation approximates a ratio with \(\Vhat\) squared in the numerator
and \(\widehat\tau^2\) in the denominator. In this range, replacing
\(\widehat\tau^2\) by \(4\Vhat\) generally reduces the denominator
and raises the mean estimated degrees of freedom. The subsequent
substitution \(\Vhat\approx2\Ihat\) lowers that mean because
\(2\Ihat\) understates \(\Vhat\), partly offsetting the first
increase. The denominator effect is larger, so the local calculation
overpredicts the mean estimated degrees of freedom even though it
underpredicts the moments in the upper rows. Both populations show this progression,
but the observed mean estimated degrees of freedom and the moment target
are lower in \(Q\).

Using the same repetitions, we also compare the population first-order
and average sample-based variance predictions defined for the middle and
bottom rows of Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-moments}. At the smallest MI target,
0.0001, the population first-order predictions are only about
5\% of the observed sampling variances, while the sample-based predictions
average roughly twice those variances. The local relation
\(\widehat\tau^2\approx4\Vhat\) carries sampling noise into the estimated
variance prediction, explaining the sample-based prediction's upward error
in this range
(Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}). At MI 0.02, the population
first-order variance predictions are much more accurate, although the
sample-based ratios remain above one. The population calculation improves
as the linear sampling term becomes more informative, while the local
near-independence calculation loses accuracy as association grows. At MI
0.05, the observed means of \(\Vhat\) remain roughly 6\% above \(V\)
in both populations, while the population first-order and average
sample-based variance predictions are close to the observed sampling
variances. The mean estimated degrees of
freedom are closer to the moment target than the population first-order
values, which still use \(V\) in place of the enlarged sampling mean.

\section{RQ2: False-positive rates under equal MI}
\label{sec:null-rejection-patterns}

The null study uses shared population MI values of 0.005, 0.02 and 0.30
nats, with all four table sizes, three margin regimes and ten sample
sizes at each level. Every population pair has \(\Delta=0\), so each
rejection is a false positive. Each plotted rate divides the number of
rejections by all 100,000 repetitions, with unsuccessful calculations
counted as non-rejections.
Shading gives pointwise 95\% intervals, and the lower strips show the
calculation success rates.

Expanded Welch uses the same statistic as Wald with a larger
rejection cutoff, so its rejection rate is always lower or equal at the
same p-value threshold of 0.05. The comparison asks whether that
reduction brings rejection closer to 5\% or deepens conservatism. The
three figures below follow the same margin order as the power study and
show the effects of sample size, table size and shared MI together.

\subsection{Uniform margins}

Figure~\ref{fig:balanced-null-uniform} uses uniform margins in both
populations, giving identical population tables at each shared MI.
Each panel increases sample size along the logarithmic horizontal axis,
with colour identifying shared MI. Solid lines with filled circles show Wald,
and dashed lines with open squares show Expanded Welch. Where the
method curves overlap, their observed false-positive rates are equal or
too close to distinguish on the plotted scale. The dotted line marks 5\%.
For a given table size, the vertical scale is the same
across the three margin figures, while different table sizes can use
different scales.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{balanced_null_uniform.pdf}
\caption{False-positive rates across shared MI values under uniform margins.}
\label{fig:balanced-null-uniform}
\end{figure}

At shared MI 0.005 and 0.02, both tests have ranges of conservative
rejection before recovering towards 5\%, with later recovery for larger
tables. At shared MI 0.30, the curves instead show substantial excessive
rejection over parts of the smaller-sample range before settling near the
target. Increasing shared MI therefore changes the rejection pattern,
even though the population difference remains zero. The biases in the two
MI estimates cancel because the population tables and sample sizes are
identical, so the departures from 5\% arise through the estimated standard error and
the sampling distribution of the shared statistic.

Expanded Welch lowers both the excessive and conservative parts of these
curves. In \(4\times4\) at \(n=20\), its rate of 4.90\% is closer
to 5\% than Wald's 8.35\% at shared MI 0.02. At shared MI 0.30,
however, Expanded Welch still rejects in 13.11\% of repetitions at the
same dimensions and sample size. The larger cutoff can correct some
excess rejection without making the method uniformly accurate as shared
MI changes. Conversely, where Wald is already below 5\%, the reduction
moves rejection farther from the target.

\subsection{Same-skew margins}

Figure~\ref{fig:balanced-null-same-skew} assigns both populations the
row and column profile \(m_k(0.8)\), again giving identical tables
under equal MI. This comparison retains the same MI levels and
sample-size grid while concentrating more probability in the first category.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{balanced_null_same_skew.pdf}
\caption{False-positive rates across shared MI values under same-skew margins.}
\label{fig:balanced-null-same-skew}
\end{figure}

The weaker shared MI values produce deep conservative ranges, with the
trough occurring later as table size increases. Uneven margins leave
more category combinations rarely observed, prolonging errors in the
estimated cell probabilities and standard error. With identical population
tables, the expected bias of the MI difference still cancels, so it cannot
explain these departures. The diagnostic comparison below shows how an
oversized standard error can shrink the statistic and suppress rejection.
At shared MI 0.30, both methods instead over-reject at several small
sample sizes before approaching 5\% with more observations.

At the smallest sample sizes, the lower strips show that Expanded Welch
calculations can succeed less often than Wald calculations, so failed
calculations also lower Expanded Welch's rejection rate when all repetitions
are counted.
However, the conservative troughs persist even when both tests' calculations
succeed in every repetition, so calculation failure is not their sole
explanation. At larger samples,
the methods' curves become close as the estimated degrees of freedom
increase, but both can remain below 5\% in the weak-association,
larger-table settings.

\subsection{Different-skew margins}

Figure~\ref{fig:balanced-null-different-skew} uses \(m_k(0.7)\) for
\(P\) and \(m_k(0.8)\) for \(Q\). The population MI values are equal,
but the tables differ, allowing their MI estimates to have different
residual biases that can shift the shared statistic away from zero
under the null.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{balanced_null_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{False-positive rates across shared MI values under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:balanced-null-different-skew}
\end{figure}

The two smaller tables retain the conservative ranges at weaker MI and
the excessive rejection at stronger MI seen in the other margin regimes.
The \(16\times16\) panel shows a more severe failure: at shared MI
0.005 and \(n=1{,}000\), Wald rejects in 36.38\% of repetitions and
Expanded Welch in 34.38\%. The larger cutoff reduces rejection but
leaves it far above 5\%. Both methods' calculations succeed in every
repetition, so these high rates reflect inaccurate inference rather than
calculation failures.

The contrast across shared MI depends on the bias relative to sampling
spread. At \(16\times16\) and \(n=1{,}000\), the bias in the estimated
MI difference is about 0.03 nats at all three MI levels, but the sampling
standard deviation is larger at stronger association. The shift therefore
has a greater effect relative to the spread at weaker MI, accompanying the
much higher rejection there.
With more observations, the bias diminishes and rejection falls. At shared
MI 0.30, the large-sample curves settle near 5\%, whereas the weakest-MI
\(16\times16\) case eventually falls below the target. Increasing
sample size can therefore pass through excessive and conservative ranges
as the shared estimates recover.

\subsection{Explaining the main departures from 5\%}
\label{sec:null-estimator-accuracy}

Table~\ref{tab:balanced-null-diagnostics} contrasts three settings with a
shared MI of 0.02 nats to illustrate different estimation errors. Because the
population MI difference \(\Delta\) is zero, ``Bias / SD'' equals the
sampling mean of \(\Deltahat\) divided by its observed sampling standard
deviation. The variance ratio divides the average \(\SEhat^2\) by the
observed variance of \(\Deltahat\) about
its sampling mean, so it measures uncertainty estimation separately from
bias. Ratios below one indicate underestimation and ratios above one
indicate overestimation. Both methods' calculations succeed in every
repetition in these three settings. Rates are percentages of all 100,000 repetitions.

In the uniform \(4\times4\) setting, bias in the estimated MI difference
is negligible, but the average squared standard error understates the observed sampling
variance. For a given estimated difference, a smaller denominator enlarges
\(|T|\) and tends to increase rejection. Expanded Welch's larger cutoff
reduces that excess and brings the rate close to 5\%, showing how the
cutoff can offset an error in the shared standard error at a
particular population pair.

The two \(16\times16\) settings have almost the same variance ratio
but opposite rejection behaviour. Under same-skew margins, the biases in
the two MI estimates cancel and the oversized standard error suppresses
rejection, which Expanded Welch lowers further. Under different-skew
margins, the positive bias places the sampling mean of the estimated
difference more than two sampling standard deviations above zero. Changing the cutoff
leaves that bias in the estimated MI difference unchanged, helping explain
why both methods reject far too often even though the estimated standard
error is oversized.

Across the grid, the methods become close with larger samples, but their
agreement does not itself establish accurate rejection. For example, at
shared MI 0.005 and \(n=50{,}000\), both tests remain below 4\% in
every \(16\times16\) margin regime, with the shared estimated variance
still exceeding the observed sampling variance of the estimated MI
difference. Expanded Welch improves calibration where its lower rejection
corrects an excess, while increasing
conservatism where Wald already rejects too rarely.

\subsection{Comparison with ordinary Welch degrees of freedom}

To check whether MI-specific degrees of freedom make a difference, we also
use a reference distribution with ordinary Welch degrees of freedom at three
illustrative null settings.
This comparison keeps the same estimated MI difference, standard error and
statistic, but substitutes component degrees of freedom \(n_P-1\) and
\(n_Q-1\) into Equation~\eqref{eq:ws-general}. These sample-size-based
values come from the comparison of sample means; for MI they serve as a
benchmark rather than a derivation of the variance estimate's sampling
variation. Expanded Welch instead uses the component degrees of freedom
derived in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}.

The comparison reuses the same 100,000 sampled pairs per setting as the
main null study, with shared MI 0.02 nats and equal sample sizes
\(n_P=n_Q=n\). All three methods' calculations succeed in every repetition.

Different skew \(16\times16\), \(n=1{,}000\) & 26.01 & 25.95 & 24.84\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

At \(n=20\), ordinary Welch reduces excessive rejection less than Expanded
Welch. At \(n=50\), Wald is already close to 5\%, and Expanded Welch
instead produces the most conservative rate. In the different-skew
\(16\times16\) case, neither correction removes the excessive rejection
associated with bias in the shared MI difference. The MI-specific
calculation therefore changes the results beyond merely replacing the
normal distribution with a Student \(t\) distribution, but the larger
reduction in rejection does not consistently improve calibration.

\section{RQ3: Power across MI differences}
\label{sec:mi-gap-power-results}

The power study repeats the four table sizes, three margin regimes and
ten sample sizes of the broad null comparison at three MI-gap magnitudes:
\(|\Delta|=0.005,0.01,0.02\) nats. Population \(P\) has MI 0.02 nats,
while \(Q\) has MI 0.025, 0.03 or 0.04 nats, respectively, so each
signed difference \(\Delta=I(P)-I(Q)\) is negative. Each plotted rate
divides the number of rejections by all 100,000 repetitions at that setting,
with unsuccessful calculations counted as non-rejections. Shading gives
pointwise 95\% intervals, and the lower strips show the calculation success rates.

At the usual p-value threshold of 0.05, Expanded Welch's larger cutoff
makes its rejections a subset of Wald's, so the comparison
quantifies how much detection probability is lost. These rates are read
alongside the false-positive results in Section~\ref{sec:null-rejection-patterns}:
high rejection under an alternative can coexist with excessive rejection
under equal MI. Because the tests are two-sided, power also includes
rejections whose estimated MI difference has the opposite sign to the
population difference.

\subsection{Uniform margins}

Figure~\ref{fig:mi-gap-power-uniform} uses uniform row and column
margins in both populations. Each panel increases the shared sample size
along the logarithmic horizontal axis, while colour identifies the MI-gap
magnitude. Solid lines with filled circles show Wald, and dashed
lines with open squares show Expanded Welch. The same colour and line
conventions apply to all three power figures, with overlapping method
curves indicating equal or visually indistinguishable power. Curves for
different MI gaps also meet near 100\% once almost every repetition
rejects the null hypothesis.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{mi_gap_power_uniform.pdf}
\caption{Power across three MI differences under uniform margins.}
\label{fig:mi-gap-power-uniform}
\end{figure}

For each MI gap, all four panels show low detection over part of the small-sample range,
followed by a sustained rise with more observations. The early shoulders
and troughs also occur in the null rejection curves, where errors in the
estimated standard error and a poor fit between the sampling distribution
of \(T\) and its reference distribution can produce excessive or
conservative rejection. Consequently, the early high points
are not evidence that fewer observations reliably identify the MI difference.

Larger tables require more observations before power becomes high. For the 0.01-nat
gap at \(n=10{,}000\), Expanded Welch detects the difference in 89.44\% of
\(2\times2\) repetitions and 71.12\% of \(16\times16\) repetitions.
This ordering reflects both the sparse-table estimation errors discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy} and the population variances:
in this copying family, increasing table size also increases \(V(P)\)
and \(V(Q)\). Their contributions \(V(P)/n+V(Q)/n\) therefore give a
larger first-order sampling variance for the same MI gap and sample size.
As the variance decreases with more observations, the estimated
difference becomes more consistently separated from zero and power rises.

Once samples are sufficiently large, increasing the MI gap shifts the
power curves upwards. In the \(4\times4\) panel at \(n=2{,}500\),
Expanded Welch's power rises from 11.14\% for the 0.005-nat gap to
78.74\% for the 0.02-nat gap. Increasing \(I(Q)\) also increases
\(V(Q)\), but in these populations the MI gap grows faster than the
first-order sampling standard deviation of the difference. The true MI gap
is therefore larger relative to the sampling spread of the estimated difference.

Expanded Welch's power is no higher than Wald's for any gap, with the largest
separation for the 0.01-nat gap occurring at the smallest sample size in the
\(16\times16\) panel. Expanded Welch's calculation success rate at that
setting is above 99\%, so most of that separation comes from the larger
cutoff rather than failed calculations. The difference in power between the
tests becomes small in the larger-sample range as increasing degrees of freedom bring the
Student \(t\) distribution close to the standard normal distribution.

\subsection{Same-skew margins}

Figure~\ref{fig:mi-gap-power-same-skew} assigns both populations the
row and column profile \(m_k(0.8)\), retaining the same three MI gaps and
sample sizes. The margins agree between populations, but their different
MI values require different copying probabilities and hence different
population tables.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{mi_gap_power_same_skew.pdf}
\caption{Power across three MI differences under same-skew margins.}
\label{fig:mi-gap-power-same-skew}
\end{figure}

The curves again have an early low-power range followed by recovery,
with the trough occurring later for larger tables. Relative to uniform
margins, the uneven frequencies leave more combinations rarely observed,
and the population pointwise-MI variances are also larger. Both features
make a given gap harder to detect. At the smallest sample sizes, the
lower strips show that failed Expanded Welch calculations also reduce
its power when all repetitions are counted, so the larger cutoff is not
the only reason for its lower power. For the 0.01-nat gap in the
\(16\times16\) case at \(n=1{,}000\), the average squared standard error is 1.81 times the
observed variance of the difference, while bias in the estimated MI
difference is small relative to the true gap. The oversized denominator suppresses \(|T|\),
contributing to Expanded Welch's power of only 1.64\% at that setting.

Both methods gain power once larger samples improve the cell estimates
and reduce the spread of the MI difference. For the 0.01-nat gap,
Expanded Welch reaches 60.99\% in \(16\times16\) at \(n=10{,}000\), compared with 71.12\%
under uniform margins. The smaller 0.005-nat gap remains harder to
detect even at \(n=50{,}000\), where Expanded Welch reaches 79.87\%
in \(16\times16\), compared with over 99.9\% for both larger gaps.
The differences between methods become much smaller than these differences
between table sizes and margin regimes.

\subsection{Different-skew margins}

Figure~\ref{fig:mi-gap-power-different-skew} uses \(m_k(0.7)\) for
\(P\) and \(m_k(0.8)\) for \(Q\). Each true difference is negative,
but the unequal margins can give the MI estimates
different residual biases, shifting the centre of their difference.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{mi_gap_power_different_skew.pdf}
\caption{Power across three MI differences under different-skew margins.}
\label{fig:mi-gap-power-different-skew}
\end{figure}

The \(2\times2\) and \(4\times4\) panels retain the low-power range
and subsequent rise, while the two larger tables have more pronounced
early rejection before falling and recovering. The \(16\times16\)
curve shows why this early rejection needs careful interpretation.
For the 0.01-nat gap at \(n=1{,}000\), the estimated MI difference has
positive bias of approximately 0.0307 nats, moving its sampling mean from
the true \(-0.01\) to approximately \(+0.0207\) nats. Expanded Welch
rejects in 9.66\% of repetitions,
but more than 99.9\% of those rejections have a positive estimated
difference, opposite to the true direction. The shared biased numerator
therefore produces rejection without reliably identifying which population
has greater MI, as in the displaced null comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy}.

This positive bias also explains why a larger MI gap can give lower
rejection at the same sample size. In \(16\times16\) at
\(n=1{,}000\), Expanded Welch's power falls from 16.11\% for
\(|\Delta|=0.005\) to 3.39\% for \(|\Delta|=0.02\). The positive
bias remains about 0.03 nats across these gaps, so making the true
difference more negative moves the sampling centre closer to zero.
The resulting loss of positive-tail rejections outweighs the increase in
negative-tail rejections over this range. The ordering reverses at larger
samples, once bias is small enough for the negative population difference
to determine the direction of most rejections.

Along the 0.01-nat curve, increasing sample size reduces the positive
bias and moves the sampling centre back towards zero. At \(n=2{,}500\), the remaining positive
bias nearly cancels the negative population difference, leaving the
sampling mean close to zero, and Expanded Welch's power falls to 2.48\%.
With still more observations, the bias diminishes further and the
sampling centre approaches the negative population difference, while
sampling variance decreases. Negative-tail rejection then rises and
power becomes high. Changing the cutoff lowers both parts of this
nonmonotonic curve without removing the biased movement of its centre.

Across all three margin regimes, both methods exceed 99.9\% power at
\(n=50{,}000\) for gaps of 0.01 and 0.02 nats, whereas power for the
0.005-nat gap ranges from 79.87\% to 96.47\%. At \(n=10{,}000\),
the method power differences are below 0.32 percentage points across all
three gaps, table sizes and margin regimes. Thus the gap magnitude strongly
affects detection once the estimates become accurate, while Expanded
Welch's larger cutoff gives a smaller loss relative to Wald in this
larger-sample range.

\section{Example application to online shopping data}
\label{sec:shoppers-application}

This example illustrates how Wald and Expanded Welch are applied
and interpreted in practice by comparing month--purchase and
weekend--purchase associations between new and returning visitors.
The Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention dataset records website sessions
and whether each session ends in a purchase \citep{sakar2018}. For each predictor,
\(X\) is month or weekend status and \(Y\) is the purchase outcome,
giving joint distributions \(P\) and \(Q\) of \((X,Y)\) among new
and returning visitors, respectively. Each comparison therefore tests
\(H_0:I(P)=I(Q)\) for that predictor and purchase outcome.

The dataset contains 12,330 sessions, each belonging to a different user
according to the source documentation \citep{sakar2018}. We retain all
1,694 sessions labelled \texttt{New\_Visitor} and 10,551 labelled
\texttt{Returning\_Visitor}, excluding the 85 \texttt{Other} sessions.
The outcome is the original Boolean \texttt{Revenue} variable, which is
true when a purchase occurs and false otherwise. We treat the two visitor
groups as independent samples and the sessions within each group as
independent observations.

\setlength{\intextsep}{4pt}
\setlength{\abovecaptionskip}{4pt}
January and April have no recorded sessions, so each month table has
\(10\times2\) cells, with rows for the ten recorded months and columns
for no purchase or purchase. All ten months occur in both visitor groups,
although the new-visitor February row contains only one session and no
purchase. Both groups use the same categories, retaining zero cells
without smoothing. Replacing month with the Boolean \texttt{Weekend}

These two shopping comparisons were selected from six comparisons across
three public datasets. The other four concern occupation and education

Both methods reject equal month--purchase MI because their p-values

we cannot directly check the estimated degrees of freedom against the
sampling-moment target or measure false-positive rates.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:results-discussion}

Expanded Welch improves on Wald where its larger cutoff corrects
excessive false-positive rates. For uniform \(4\times4\) tables at
shared MI 0.02 nats and \(n=20\), Wald gives 8.35\% rejection and
Expanded Welch gives 4.90\%
(Table~\ref{tab:balanced-null-diagnostics}). This reduction towards 5\%
supports the motivation of \citet{panwall2002}: accounting for variability
in an estimated variance can reduce excessive Wald rejection. However,
where Wald already rejects too rarely, Expanded Welch lowers rejection
further. The accompanying loss of power must therefore
be interpreted alongside whether the reduction in false positives improves
calibration.

The approximation checks distinguish the variability of an estimated
variance from its average size. Predicting the sampling variance of \(\Vhat\)
accurately does not ensure that its sampling mean is close to the
population variance \(V\). Separately,
Table~\ref{tab:balanced-null-diagnostics} shows that, under same-skew
\(16\times16\) margins, the average squared standard error is about
1.93 times the observed variance of the MI difference. With negligible
difference bias in that setting, a larger cutoff compounds the suppression
of rejection. This agrees with \citet{bell2002}, who find that
Satterthwaite degrees of freedom can make tests particularly conservative
when the variance estimator has positive bias.

Bias in the MI difference connects the null failures to the misleading
power patterns. With identical population tables and sample sizes, the
biases of the two MI estimates cancel; with different margins, they can
leave a shifted difference under equal MI or reverse its estimated sign
under an alternative. The residual information-estimation bias discussed
by \citet{paninski2003} thus affects both false-positive rates and the
estimated direction of a difference. These findings explain why high
rejection under an alternative need not identify the population with greater MI reliably,
and why agreement between Wald and Expanded Welch on observed data does
not establish accurate inference. Within the tested settings, the larger
cutoff corrects some excessive rejection, but reliable comparisons also
require an estimated MI difference with small bias relative to its
sampling spread and a standard error that describes that spread.

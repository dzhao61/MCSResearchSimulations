\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis studied how to compare the mutual information (MI) of two
independently sampled categorical populations. Equal MI permits the
population tables, their margins and their patterns of association to
differ. The work makes two contributions to this problem. First, it
derives how a change in one observation changes the plug-in estimate
of MI variance, including the effects on cell probabilities and row
and column totals. This gives an MI-specific Welch--Satterthwaite
degrees-of-freedom calculation. Second, it evaluates that calculation
against Normal Wald on fixed populations whose true MI differences
are known, reporting false positives, detection, validity and runtime
together.

The main finding is a qualified negative result. Expanded Welch
reduces rejection relative to Wald and can move an excessive
false-positive rate toward 5\%. The same change makes an already-low
rate lower and often reduces detection when MI differs. In sparse
samples, it can also fail to return a p-value more often than Wald.
The reference distribution alone cannot correct errors in the
estimated MI difference or the standard error shared by both tests.
This identifies both the value and the limit of a familiar
variance-reference adjustment when applied to MI. Normal Wald remains
the simpler general baseline for the tested task; Expanded Welch is
best treated as a sensitivity check in a regime where its calibration
and validity have been examined separately.

That recommendation is bounded by the experiment. Its population
pairs were chosen to expose particular table sizes, margins,
association patterns and sample sizes, not to represent all joint
distributions. The derivation assumes independent observations,
positive population cell probabilities and positive first-order MI
variance. Exact independence requires a different approximation,
and the study does not establish a rule for paired data, continuous
MI estimators or growing alphabets.

The most useful next step is to study the estimated MI difference
and its standard error together, especially near independence and
in sparse tables. A likelihood-ratio or resampling method could
provide a different comparison, but it would need validation for
the actual null \(I(P)=I(Q)\), including cases where \(P\ne Q\).
A real-data application and equal-MI pairs with association in
different cells would then test how far the present simulation
conclusions extend.

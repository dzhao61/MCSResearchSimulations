\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

\section{Contributions and main findings}

This thesis derives an Expanded Welch--Satterthwaite test for comparing
MI of the same pair of categorical variables across independent populations.
Its mathematical
contribution is the observation-level sensitivity of the estimated MI
variance, which determines the degrees of freedom. Its empirical
contribution is a controlled 240-setting comparison with Normal Wald
that identifies the estimation errors behind the observed false
positives and detection. Three connected studies are supported by direct
checks of variance-estimate variability, population construction and
sample allocation. An online-shopping application illustrates the
interpretation of both tests for observed categorical data.

The variance-sensitivity formula supplies estimated degrees of freedom for
the Student \(t\) distribution used by Expanded Welch. Direct checks
show close agreement between predicted and observed variability in the
well-sampled \(4\times4\) setting, but substantial approximation and
plug-in errors near independence. This identifies where the first-order
approximation describes the sampling variability of the MI variance
estimate and where it needs further development. The usefulness of the resulting test also
depends on the accuracy of the MI difference and standard error.

Expanded Welch lowers rejection, but its benefit depends on the errors
already present in Wald. Near independence it makes conservative rates
lower still. In larger different-skew tables, unequal MI bias can instead
produce excessive false positives and reverse the estimated direction
even among rejections. These failures occur under both tested population
constructions. Expanded Welch's larger cutoff leaves the estimated MI
difference and standard error unchanged.
Larger samples improve the estimation diagnostics and observed
performance, and the tests become similar. These findings do not support
Expanded Welch as a general replacement for Normal Wald. Normal Wald remains
the simpler baseline, although sparse estimation can make neither test
reliable. The main practical requirement is accurate estimation of the
MI difference and its uncertainty, which a larger cutoff cannot supply.

\section{Limitations}

The recommendation is limited to independent categorical samples with
fixed categories. The controlled populations and small allocation check
do not cover every association pattern or sample-size imbalance.
The first-order derivation requires positive cell probabilities and
positive first-order MI variance; exact independence needs a different
approximation.

\section{Future work}

Future work should first address bias in sparse MI differences and the
dependence between the difference and its estimated standard error.
The \(16\times16\) different-skew jackknife check provides a starting
point: at \(n=1{,}000\), it improves the estimated direction while the
test remains conservative.
Further comparisons should assess changes to the MI difference and its
standard error separately before combining them.
The falling detection curve gives a concrete target: in these sparse
settings, an improved comparison should avoid moving the estimated
difference towards zero when the population difference grows.
Alternative bias treatments
should therefore be assessed through both null calibration and the
shape of detection curves, rather than through MI estimation error
alone.

Near independence, a second-order approximation to MI's sampling variation
may be more appropriate than changing the rejection cutoff of the
first-order test. A likelihood-ratio
or resampling method would need validation for equal MI even when the
complete population tables differ. This would address the approximation
problem revealed by the population-MI diagnostics. Broader unequal-sample
comparisons, other association
patterns and applications to additional datasets would extend the practical reach
of these conclusions.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis derives an Expanded Welch--Satterthwaite test for comparing
MI between independent categorical populations. Its mathematical
contribution is the observation-level sensitivity of the estimated MI
variance, which determines the degrees of freedom. Its empirical
contribution is a controlled 232-setting comparison with Normal Wald.
Three connected studies, estimation diagnostics and a supporting
construction check explain the observed false positives and detection.

Together, these contributions connect the stability of an estimated MI
variance to an explicit, computable cutoff adjustment. They also show
why that adjustment must be evaluated alongside the accuracy of the
MI difference and standard error. This separates three questions that
can otherwise be confused: whether the effect estimate is centred
correctly, whether its uncertainty is estimated accurately, and whether
the reference distribution gives a useful rejection threshold.

Expanded Welch lowers rejection, but its benefit depends on the errors
already present in Wald. Near independence it makes conservative rates
lower still. In larger different-skew tables, unequal MI bias can instead
produce excessive false positives and an initial decline in detection
as the true difference grows. The adjustment leaves the shared estimates
unchanged. Larger samples improve the estimation diagnostics and observed
performance, and the tests become similar. Normal Wald remains the simpler
baseline; Expanded Welch is a supplementary check where a closely matched
calibration study supports its use.

The evaluation covers four square table sizes, three margin regimes and
equal sample sizes, primarily under a copying construction. The derivation
requires positive cell probabilities and positive first-order MI variance;
exact independence needs a different approximation. These conditions
bound the practical recommendation.

\begin{samepage}
Future work should first address bias in sparse MI differences and the
dependence between the difference and its estimated standard error.
The falling detection curve gives a concrete target: in these sparse
settings, an improved comparison should avoid moving the estimated
difference towards zero when the population difference grows.
Alternative bias treatments
should therefore be assessed through both null calibration and the
shape of detection curves, rather than through MI estimation error
alone.

Near independence, a second-order comparison may be more appropriate
than refining the first-order reference distribution. A likelihood-ratio
or resampling method would need validation for equal MI even when the
complete population tables differ. This would address the approximation
problem revealed by the association-strength diagnostics. Unequal sample
sizes, other association
patterns and a real-data application would then test the practical reach
of these conclusions.

\end{samepage}

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis compared two tests of whether independent groups have the same
population MI. Equal MI does not require independence within either group or
identical population tables. Normal Wald and Expanded Welch calculate the
same estimated difference and standard error; they use different reference
distributions to decide whether that difference is unusual.

Expanded Welch chooses its Student reference by calculating how the estimated
MI variance responds when a count table changes. The calculation includes
changes to cell probabilities and to the row and column totals used in MI.
It provides a reasoned way to choose degrees of freedom, but not an exact
small-sample distribution.

The experiment found both benefits and costs. Expanded Welch can bring an
excessive Wald false-positive rate closer to 5\%. If Wald already rejects too
rarely, Expanded Welch lowers rejection further. In sparse samples it also
returns fewer usable results, partly because of the implementation's validity
rule. A low rejection rate caused by failed calculations should not be
mistaken for good calibration. Both methods approached 5\% in some tested
large-sample populations, but populations close to independence remained
difficult. Expanded Welch took about 1.75 times as long per test in the
measured implementation.

The difficulty near independence helps explain why the correction is not a
general solution. It can choose a Student reference that requires stronger
evidence to reject, while leaving any error in the estimated MI difference or
standard error unchanged. The evidence therefore supports Normal Wald as the
simpler general baseline. Expanded Welch is an additional check where Wald
is known to reject equal MI too often, not a universal replacement.

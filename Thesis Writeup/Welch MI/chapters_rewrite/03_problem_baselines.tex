\chapter{Overview of the Two-Sample MI Test and Wald Baseline}
\label{chap:baselines}

This chapter defines the estimated MI difference and standard error shared
by Normal Wald and Expanded Welch, then uses their statistic to obtain
the Normal Wald p-value.

\section{Problem setup and bias-corrected estimator}

Let
\begin{equation}
  N^P=(N^P_{ij}),
  \qquad
  N^Q=(N^Q_{ij})
\end{equation}
be independent \(r\times c\) count tables with totals \(n_P\) and \(n_Q\).
The rows and columns represent the same categorical variables \(X\) and
\(Y\) in both populations, with the same category definitions.
The empirical probabilities are
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{ij}=\frac{N^P_{ij}}{n_P},
  \qquad
  \widehat q_{ij}=\frac{N^Q_{ij}}{n_Q}.
\end{equation}

The population parameter is the signed difference
\begin{equation}
  \Delta=I(P)-I(Q).
  \label{eq:delta-population}
\end{equation}
The principal test is two-sided:
\begin{equation}
  H_0:\Delta=0
  \quad\text{against}\quad
  H_1:\Delta\ne0.
  \label{eq:two-sample-null}
\end{equation}

The large-sample argument uses the following assumptions.

\begin{samepage}
\begin{assumption}[Sampling]
Observations are independent and identically distributed within each
population, and the two population samples are independent.
\end{assumption}
\end{samepage}

\begin{assumption}[Aligned fixed alphabets]
The row and column categories are fixed in advance and aligned between the two
tables.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Positive cell probabilities]
Every cell in the population table has a probability greater than zero. A
sample can still contain no observations in a cell, especially when that cell
is rare. Such samples remain in the study; we record how often cells have
zero observed counts to show how sparse the samples are.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Positive MI]
Both populations have positive MI: \(I(P)>0\) and \(I(Q)>0\). With the positive
cell probabilities in Assumption~3, this is equivalent to requiring positive
first-order variances \(V(P)>0\) and \(V(Q)>0\). It excludes exact
independence, where the first-order approximation breaks down.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Logarithm units]
All logarithms in this thesis are natural, so MI differences and standard
errors are reported in nats. Changing the logarithm base scales both by the
same positive constant and leaves the standardised statistic unchanged.
This is a choice of units, not a condition for the test to be valid.
\end{assumption}

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Bias-corrected estimator.}

For the first table, define empirical pointwise MI by
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\pmi_P(i,j)
  =\log\left(
  \frac{\widehat p_{ij}}
       {\widehat p_{i+}\widehat p_{+j}}
  \right)
  \qquad\text{when }\widehat p_{ij}>0.
  \label{eq:empirical-pmi}
\end{equation}
The plug-in MI estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \Ihat(P)
  =\sum_{i,j:\widehat p_{ij}>0}
  \widehat p_{ij}\widehat\pmi_P(i,j).
  \label{eq:plugin-mi}
\end{equation}
The same calculation gives \(\Ihat(Q)\).

Subtracting the leading fixed-support MI bias in
Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-leading-bias} \citep{miller1955,moddemeijer1989}
from each plug-in estimate gives the estimated effect:
\begin{equation}
  \Deltahat
  =\left\{\Ihat(P)-\frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2n_P}\right\}
  -\left\{\Ihat(Q)-\frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2n_Q}\right\}.
  \label{eq:bias-corrected-difference}
\end{equation}
The two terms in braces are the bias-corrected MI estimates for the
separate populations. Their corrections use the declared row and column
categories because every population cell has positive probability under
the model, even when some cells are unobserved in a sample. We retain
negative corrected estimates when calculating the difference.
When \(n_P=n_Q\), the two corrections cancel, so \(\Deltahat\) is exactly
the raw plug-in MI difference. With unequal sample sizes, their difference
removes the leading unequal-sample bias described in
Section~\ref{sec:background-mi-bias}. A correction that estimates support
from occupied cells would be a different estimator.

\section{Shared standard error and test statistic}

Section~\ref{sec:first-order-mi-background} showed that the first-order
sampling variance of \(\Ihat(P)\) is \(V(P)/n_P\), where \(V(P)\) is the
population variance of pointwise MI \citep{moddemeijer1989,brillinger2004}.
Subtracting the fixed leading bias term
in Equation~\eqref{eq:bias-corrected-difference} does not change this
variance.

The observed table estimates \(V(P)\) by
\begin{equation}
  \Vhat(P)
  =\sum_{i,j:\widehat p_{ij}>0}
  \widehat p_{ij}
  \{\widehat\pmi_P(i,j)-\Ihat(P)\}^2.
  \label{eq:estimated-mi-variance}
\end{equation}
Define \(\Vhat(Q)\) analogously.

Because the two samples are independent, their estimated variance
contributions add:
\begin{equation}
  \SEhat^2
  =\frac{\Vhat(P)}{n_P}
  +\frac{\Vhat(Q)}{n_Q}.
  \label{eq:shared-se}
\end{equation}
Taking the positive square root gives \(\SEhat\), which estimates the
standard deviation of \(\Deltahat\) across repeated samples.
Table~\ref{tab:results-variance-quantities} distinguishes the variation of
pointwise MI across observation outcomes from the sampling variation of
the estimates calculated from whole tables.

The common standardised statistic is
\begin{equation}
  T=\frac{\Deltahat}{\SEhat}.
  \label{eq:shared-t}
\end{equation}
The sign of \(T\) indicates whether \(P\) or \(Q\) has the larger
corrected MI estimate, while its magnitude measures the difference relative
to its estimated standard error. Both tests use
Equations~\eqref{eq:bias-corrected-difference}--\eqref{eq:shared-t}, but
compare \(T\) with different reference distributions centred at zero
under \(H_0\). The two rejection rules therefore differ through the
reference distribution while using the same effect estimate and plug-in
standard error.

\section[Normal Wald test and motivation for Expanded Welch]{Normal Wald test and motivation for\\Expanded Welch}

When both sample sizes grow while the table dimensions and positive
population probabilities stay fixed, the statistic approaches a standard
normal distribution under equal population MI
\citep{wald1943,vanderVaart1998}:
\begin{equation}
  T\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)
  \qquad\text{under }H_0.
\end{equation}
The two-sided Normal Wald p-value is
\begin{equation}
  p_{\mathrm{Wald}}
  =2\{1-\Phi(|T|)\},
  \label{eq:wald-pvalue}
\end{equation}
where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function. Under this
reference distribution for equal MI, the p-value is the probability of a
statistic at least as far from zero as the observed \(T\). The
two-sided calculation doubles the upper-tail probability
\(1-\Phi(|T|)\) because symmetry gives the same probability in the
lower tail.
At the nominal 5\% level, the test rejects when \(p_{\mathrm{Wald}}\leq0.05\).

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Why use a Student \(t\) distribution?}

Wald uses the standard normal distribution at every sample size, even
though its standard error is estimated from the observed tables. Expanded
Welch uses the sampling variability of \(\Vhat(P)\) and \(\Vhat(Q)\)
to choose the degrees of freedom of a Student \(t\) distribution.
Hutcheson's entropy test assigns each variance component \(n_i\)
degrees of freedom \citep{hutcheson1970}, while ordinary Welch's test
assigns \(n_i-1\) \citep{welch1947}. These assignments do not measure
how cell probabilities and margins make the estimated MI variance change
between samples.

To choose the degrees of freedom, we need the average value of each estimated
MI variance and how much that estimate varies across samples
\citep{satterthwaite1946}. For \(P\), to first order,
\begin{equation}
  \E\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx V(P).
\end{equation}
Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} derives
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\) by tracking how the calculated MI variance changes
when one observation changes the joint table.

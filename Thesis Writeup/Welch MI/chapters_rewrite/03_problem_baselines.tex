\chapter{The Two-Sample MI Test and Wald Baseline}
\label{chap:baselines}

Normal Wald and Expanded Welch share an estimated MI difference and standard
error. This chapter defines their statistic and the Normal Wald p-value.

\section{Problem setup and bias-corrected estimator}

Let
\begin{equation}
  N^P=(N^P_{ij}),
  \qquad
  N^Q=(N^Q_{ij})
\end{equation}
be independent \(r\times c\) count tables with totals \(n_P\) and \(n_Q\).
The row and column categories have the same definitions in both populations.
The empirical probabilities are
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{ij}=\frac{N^P_{ij}}{n_P},
  \qquad
  \widehat q_{ij}=\frac{N^Q_{ij}}{n_Q}.
\end{equation}

The population parameter is the signed difference
\begin{equation}
  \Delta=I(P)-I(Q).
  \label{eq:delta-population}
\end{equation}
The principal test is two-sided:
\begin{equation}
  H_0:\Delta=0
  \quad\text{against}\quad
  H_1:\Delta\ne0.
  \label{eq:two-sample-null}
\end{equation}

The large-sample argument uses the following assumptions.

\begin{samepage}
\begin{assumption}[Sampling]
Observations are independent and identically distributed within each
population, and the two population samples are independent.
\end{assumption}
\end{samepage}

\begin{assumption}[Aligned fixed alphabets]
The row and column categories are fixed in advance and aligned between the two
tables.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Positive cell probabilities]
Every cell in the population table has a probability greater than zero. A
sample can still contain no observations in a cell, especially when that cell
is rare. Such samples remain in the study; we record how often cells have
zero observed counts to show how sparse the samples are.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Positive MI]
Both populations have positive MI: \(I(P)>0\) and \(I(Q)>0\). With the positive
cell probabilities in Assumption~3, this is equivalent to requiring positive
first-order variances \(V(P)>0\) and \(V(Q)>0\). It excludes exact
independence, where the first-order approximation breaks down.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Logarithm units]
All logarithms in this thesis are natural, so MI differences and standard
errors are reported in nats. Changing the logarithm base scales both by the
same positive constant and leaves the standardised statistic unchanged.
This is a choice of units, not a condition for the test to be valid.
\end{assumption}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Bias-corrected estimator.}

For the first table, define empirical pointwise MI by
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\pmi_P(i,j)
  =\log\left(
  \frac{\widehat p_{ij}}
       {\widehat p_{i+}\widehat p_{+j}}
  \right)
  \qquad\text{when }\widehat p_{ij}>0.
  \label{eq:empirical-pmi}
\end{equation}
The plug-in MI estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \Ihat(P)
  =\sum_{i,j:\widehat p_{ij}>0}
  \widehat p_{ij}\widehat\pmi_P(i,j).
  \label{eq:plugin-mi}
\end{equation}
The same calculation gives \(\Ihat(Q)\).

The leading fixed-support MI bias appears in
Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-leading-bias} \citep{moddemeijer1989}. Subtracting it
from each plug-in estimate gives the estimated effect:
\begin{equation}
  \Deltahat
  =\left\{\Ihat(P)-\frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2n_P}\right\}
  -\left\{\Ihat(Q)-\frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2n_Q}\right\}.
  \label{eq:bias-corrected-difference}
\end{equation}
The two terms in braces are the bias-corrected MI estimates for the separate
populations.
The correction uses the fixed numbers of row and column categories, even
when some are unobserved in a sample. Corrected estimates can be negative;
those values are retained when calculating the difference.
When \(n_P=n_Q\), the two corrections cancel in the difference;
otherwise, their difference removes the leading unequal-sample bias described
in Section~\ref{sec:background-mi-bias}.

\section{Shared standard error and test statistic}

Section~\ref{sec:first-order-mi-background} showed that the first-order
sampling variance of \(\Ihat(P)\) is \(V(P)/n_P\), where \(V(P)\) is the
population variance of pointwise MI \citep{moddemeijer1989,brillinger2004}.
Subtracting the fixed leading bias term
in Equation~\eqref{eq:bias-corrected-difference} does not change this
variance.

The observed table estimates \(V(P)\) by
\begin{equation}
  \Vhat(P)
  =\sum_{i,j:\widehat p_{ij}>0}
  \widehat p_{ij}
  \{\widehat\pmi_P(i,j)-\Ihat(P)\}^2.
  \label{eq:estimated-mi-variance}
\end{equation}
Define \(\Vhat(Q)\) analogously.

Because the two samples are independent, their estimated variance
contributions add:
\begin{equation}
  \SEhat^2
  =\frac{\Vhat(P)}{n_P}
  +\frac{\Vhat(Q)}{n_Q}.
  \label{eq:shared-se}
\end{equation}
The common standardised statistic is
\begin{equation}
  T=\frac{\Deltahat}{\SEhat}.
  \label{eq:shared-t}
\end{equation}
The sign of \(T\) indicates which sample has the larger corrected MI
estimate. Its magnitude measures the difference relative to its estimated
standard error. Under \(H_0\), we compare \(T\) with a reference distribution
centred at zero.
Equations~\eqref{eq:bias-corrected-difference}--\eqref{eq:shared-t} are shared
by Normal Wald and Expanded Welch. Their difference lies in the reference
distribution, not the effect estimate or plug-in standard error.

\section{Normal Wald reference and motivation for expansion}

When both sample sizes grow while the table dimensions and positive
population probabilities stay fixed, the statistic approaches a standard
normal distribution under equal population MI
\citep{wald1943,vanderVaart1998}:
\begin{equation}
  T\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)
  \qquad\text{under }H_0.
\end{equation}
The two-sided Normal Wald p-value is
\begin{equation}
  p_{\mathrm{Wald}}
  =2\{1-\Phi(|T|)\},
  \label{eq:wald-pvalue}
\end{equation}
where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Why consider an expanded reference?}

Wald uses the standard normal distribution at every sample size, even
though its standard error is estimated from the observed tables. Expanded
Welch uses the variability of that variance estimate to choose a Student
\(t\) distribution instead.
Hutcheson's entropy test and ordinary Welch's test
set each variance component's degrees of freedom from its sample size:
\(n_i\) \citep{hutcheson1970} or \(n_i-1\) \citep{welch1947}. Neither assignment
measures how cell probabilities and margins make the estimated MI variance
change between samples.

To choose the degrees of freedom, we need the average value of each estimated
MI variance and how much that estimate varies across samples
\citep{satterthwaite1946}. For \(P\), to first order,
\begin{equation}
  \E\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx V(P).
\end{equation}
Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} derives
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\) by tracking how the calculated MI variance changes
when one observation changes the joint table.

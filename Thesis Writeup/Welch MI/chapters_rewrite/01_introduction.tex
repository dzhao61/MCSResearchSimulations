\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Suppose two independent groups are observed on the same categorical variables
\(X\) and \(Y\). Mutual information (MI) describes the strength of association
between those variables in each group. If the estimated MI differs, the
difference alone does not tell us whether the population MI values differ:
both estimates vary when new observations are drawn. The question in this thesis is whether
the two \emph{population} MI values can plausibly be equal.

This is not the usual test of whether \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent within
one group. The null hypothesis here permits dependence in both groups and
permits their complete joint distributions to differ. It specifies only
\begin{equation}
  H_0:I(P)=I(Q),
  \qquad H_1:I(P)\ne I(Q),
  \label{eq:introduction-null}
\end{equation}
where \(P\) and \(Q\) are the two joint distributions. The samples from
\(P\) and \(Q\) are independent of each other; that sampling assumption does
not imply independence of \(X\) and \(Y\) within either population.

\section{Why the comparison is difficult}

MI uses the probabilities in every cell of a table and the totals of its rows
and columns. In a small sample, some cells can be empty even when their
population probabilities are positive. The observed tables determine both
the estimated MI difference and its standard error, so sparse data can affect
both. The Normal Wald test compares their ratio with a normal curve, but in
small samples it may reject equal MI more or less often than the intended 5\%.

Expanded Welch offers a possible adjustment. It uses the same estimated
difference and standard error as Wald, but compares their ratio with a
Student \(t\) distribution. Its degrees of freedom depend on how much
the estimated MI variance changes from sample to sample. To calculate that
change, we must account for the fact that changing one cell also changes its
row and column totals and the MI values calculated from them. This thesis
derives that calculation and tests whether the adjustment helps.

The adjustment is not automatically better. It can reduce false positives
where Wald rejects too often, but it can also reduce detection where Wald
already rejects too rarely. In sparse samples, it may fail to return a usable
p-value when Wald succeeds. The experiment therefore examines false
positives, detection and the frequency of usable results together.

\section{Research questions}

The thesis asks four related questions. First, how can changes in the
estimated MI variance be used to choose the degrees of freedom for a Student
test? Second, does this choice improve false-positive rates without losing
too much detection or producing too many invalid results? Third, how does the
answer depend on table size, uneven cell probabilities, the amount and
location of association, and unequal sample sizes? Fourth, do the problems
disappear as samples grow, and how much extra computation does the method
require?

\section{Contributions to the field}

This thesis combines established ideas about MI and Welch--Satterthwaite
tests for a specific question: whether two populations have equal MI. Its
contributions are the calculation needed for that test and evidence about
whether the test is useful.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Mathematical contribution.} The thesis derives how changing
  one observation affects the estimated MI variance. The calculation includes
  the resulting changes in the row and column totals and in pointwise MI.

  \item \textbf{Methodological contribution.} Expanded Welch uses this
  calculation to choose the degrees of freedom for a two-sample Student test.
  The main experiment compares it with Wald; a supplementary analysis checks
  simpler sample-size choices for the degrees of freedom.

  \item \textbf{Empirical contribution.} A reproducible experiment measures
  false positives, detection, usable results and runtime on fixed populations
  with known MI. It covers \(2\times2\) through \(8\times8\) square tables,
  rectangular tables, unequal samples and very sparse settings. The companion
  atlas retains the result for each setting rather than hiding it in an
  overall average.

  \item \textbf{Practical contribution.} Expanded Welch can help when Wald
  rejects equal MI too often. It can also make an already-low rejection rate
  lower and return fewer usable results in sparse samples. The evidence shows
  why it should not replace Wald in general and points to problems that
  changing the reference distribution alone cannot solve.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and organisation}

The analysis concerns independent samples recorded in count tables with the
same row and column categories. The main derivation assumes every population
cell has positive probability and that its leading large-sample MI variance
is greater than zero. Exact independence is considered separately because
that variance becomes zero.
The experiment compares Normal Wald with Expanded Welch using a two-sided 5\%
test. It does not establish performance for paired data, continuous MI
estimators or a number of categories that grows with sample size.

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} supplies the statistical background.
Chapters~\ref{chap:baselines} and~\ref{chap:expanded} define the shared
statistic and derive the correction. Chapter~\ref{chap:design} explains how
the populations and samples are generated and how the results are measured.
Chapter~\ref{chap:results} shows the complete landscape before the focused
comparisons. Chapters~\ref{chap:discussion} and~\ref{chap:conclusion} discuss
the consequences and the resulting recommendation. The appendices provide
the detailed algebra, construction and source-to-result record.

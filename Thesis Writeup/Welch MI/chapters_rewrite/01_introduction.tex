\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Mutual information (MI) measures the average reduction in uncertainty about
one variable obtained by observing another \citep{bossomaier2016}.
For example, a categorical input feature
\(X\) may carry different amounts of information about a class label \(Y\) in two
populations. Comparing feature--label MI asks whether the feature provides
the same amount of information about the label in both populations,
even when their feature and class
frequencies differ. This thesis develops and evaluates a statistical test
for that comparison using two independently collected samples.

In practice, MI is estimated from observed data. Two samples can produce
different MI estimates even when the population MI values are equal,
because their observed counts vary from sample to sample. A statistical
test must account for this sampling variation before an estimated
difference can be interpreted as evidence of a population difference.

Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be the joint probability distributions of \(X\) and
\(Y\) in the two populations. The null and alternative hypotheses are
\begin{equation}
  H_0:I(P)=I(Q),
  \qquad H_1:I(P)\ne I(Q),
  \label{eq:introduction-null}
\end{equation}
Under \(H_0\), the populations have equal MI, but their joint probability
tables may differ and \(X\) and \(Y\) may be associated in both. The test
therefore compares population MI values rather than
testing independence within one population.

\section{Why the comparison is difficult}

The Normal Wald test \citep{wald1943,vanderVaart1998} divides the estimated
MI difference by its standard error and compares the resulting statistic
with a standard normal distribution. At a 5\% significance level, the
intended false-positive rate is 5\% when the population MI values are
equal. The normal
approximation can be inaccurate in small or sparse samples, causing the
test to reject equal MI too often or too rarely.

Both the MI difference and its standard error are estimated from count
tables, whose cells record how often each pair of categories occurs.
MI depends on the cell probabilities and their row and column totals.
When samples are small,
rare combinations of categories may occur only a few times or not at all.
The resulting probability estimates can distort both the MI difference
and its estimated uncertainty.

The proposed Expanded Welch--Satterthwaite test (Expanded Welch) retains
Wald's estimated MI difference and standard error but uses a Student
\(t\) distribution instead of the standard normal distribution. Choosing
its degrees of freedom requires estimating how much the MI variance
estimates change from sample to sample. For MI, this calculation must
account for the fact that changing a cell probability also changes its
row and column totals and the MI values calculated from them. This thesis
derives how these probability changes affect the estimated MI variance
and uses that calculation to obtain degrees of freedom for Expanded Welch.

The Student \(t\) distribution gives Expanded Welch a larger rejection
cutoff than Normal Wald, so it rejects less often. This can reduce false
positives, but it can also reduce statistical power: the probability of
rejecting equal MI when the population MI values genuinely differ.
The evaluation therefore examines both outcomes, together
with how often each method returns a usable p-value, to determine whether
the larger cutoff offers a useful improvement.

\section{Research questions}

The thesis addresses two research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item How can the sampling variability of the estimated MI variance
  determine MI-specific degrees of freedom for Expanded Welch?
  \item Under what conditions does Expanded Welch improve false-positive
  rates relative to Normal Wald, and what are the costs in power,
  usable results and computation?
\end{enumerate}

An analytical derivation addresses the first question. The second is
examined through controlled simulations with known population MI, varying
sample size, table size, category frequencies and population MI.
Estimation diagnostics explain how bias and uncertainty affect the test
results.

\section{Contributions to the field}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \begin{samepage}\textbf{Mathematical contribution.} The thesis derives how changing
  one observation affects the estimated MI variance. The calculation includes
  the resulting changes in the row and column totals and in pointwise MI.
  \par
  \end{samepage}

  \item \begin{samepage}\textbf{Methodological contribution.} Expanded Welch uses this
  variance sensitivity to choose the degrees of freedom for a two-sample
  test using a Student \(t\) distribution.
  \par
  \end{samepage}

  \item \begin{samepage}\textbf{Empirical contribution.} Three controlled studies compare
  false positives, power and the frequency of usable results across
  four table sizes and three category-frequency regimes. Diagnostics
  assess errors in the MI difference and its standard error, and directly
  check the predicted variability of the MI variance estimate. Supporting
  checks vary population construction and sample allocation, while a
  timing comparison measures computational cost.
  \par
  \end{samepage}

  \item \textbf{Practical contribution.} The evaluation identifies
  where Expanded Welch's larger cutoff helps and where estimation errors
  leave both tests unreliable. Near independence, both tests can reject
  equal MI too rarely. In sparse populations with different category
  frequencies, unequal MI bias can instead produce excessive false
  positives and reversed estimated ordering among rejections. These
  sparse-table findings persist under both tested population constructions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and organisation}

The analysis concerns categorical observations recorded in count tables
with the same fixed row and column categories. Observations are independent
within and between the two samples. The main derivation assumes positive
cell probabilities and positive MI in both populations. Exact independence
is considered separately because the first-order MI variance is then zero.
Normal Wald and Expanded Welch are compared using two-sided tests at the
5\% significance level.

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} reviews the background and literature.
Chapters~\ref{chap:baselines} and~\ref{chap:expanded} define the shared
statistic and derive Expanded Welch. Chapter~\ref{chap:design} describes
the experiment, and Chapter~\ref{chap:results} interprets its results and
the online-shopping application. Chapters~\ref{chap:discussion}
and~\ref{chap:conclusion} discuss the implications, limitations and
conclusions. The appendices provide supporting calculations, experimental
settings and additional results.

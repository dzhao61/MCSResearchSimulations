\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Mutual information (MI) measures the average reduction in uncertainty about
one variable obtained by observing another \citep{bossomaier2016}.
For example, a categorical input feature
\(X\) may carry different amounts of information about a class label \(Y\) in two
populations. This thesis develops and evaluates a statistical test
for that comparison using two independently collected samples.

Let \(P\) and \(Q\) be the joint probability distributions of \(X\) and
\(Y\) in the two populations. The null and alternative hypotheses are
\begin{equation}
  H_0:I(P)=I(Q),
  \qquad H_1:I(P)\ne I(Q).
  \label{eq:introduction-null}
\end{equation}
Under \(H_0\), the populations have equal MI even though their joint
probability tables can differ.

In practice, MI is estimated from observed counts that vary from sample
to sample, so two samples can produce different MI estimates even when
the population MI values are equal. A statistical
test must account for this sampling variation before an estimated
difference can be interpreted as evidence of a population difference.

\section{Why the comparison is difficult}

The Normal Wald test \citep{wald1943,vanderVaart1998} divides the estimated
MI difference by its standard error and compares the resulting statistic
with a standard normal distribution. At a 5\% significance level, the
intended false-positive rate is 5\% when the population MI values are
equal. The normal
approximation can be inaccurate in small or sparse samples, causing the
test to reject equal MI too often or too rarely.

Both the MI difference and its standard error are estimated from count
tables, whose cells record how often each pair of categories occurs.
MI depends on the cell probabilities and their row and column totals.
When samples are small and rare combinations of categories occur only a
few times or not at all, the resulting probability estimates can distort
both the MI difference and its estimated uncertainty.

The proposed Expanded Welch--Satterthwaite test (Expanded Welch) retains
Wald's estimated MI difference and standard error but uses a Student
\(t\) distribution instead of the standard normal distribution. Choosing
its degrees of freedom requires estimating how much the MI variance
estimates change from sample to sample. For MI, this calculation must
account for the fact that changing a cell probability also changes its
row and column totals and the MI values calculated from them. This thesis
derives how these probability changes affect the estimated MI variance
and uses that calculation to obtain degrees of freedom for Expanded Welch.

The Student \(t\) distribution gives Expanded Welch a larger rejection
cutoff than Normal Wald, so it rejects less often. This can reduce false
positives, but it can also reduce statistical power: the probability of
rejecting equal MI when the population MI values genuinely differ.
The evaluation reports the resulting false-positive rates, nominal power
and frequency of usable p-values. It then uses independent equal-MI
simulations to adjust each test's cutoff to target 5\% false positives
before comparing power. Changing the equal-MI setting used for this
calibration checks whether the power comparison is stable.

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The thesis addresses three research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*:},leftmargin=*]
 \item How accurately do the derived approximations predict the mean and
 sampling variance of the estimated MI variance, and what degrees of
 freedom do they imply near and away from independence?
 \item How do Expanded Welch's false-positive rates compare with those of
 Normal Wald under equal population MI?
 \item How do Expanded Welch's nominal and calibrated power compare with
 those of Normal Wald when population MI differs?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions to the field}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \begin{samepage}\textbf{Mathematical contribution.} The thesis derives how changing
  one observation affects the estimated MI variance, including the resulting
  changes in the row and column totals and in pointwise MI.
  \par
  \end{samepage}

  \item \begin{samepage}\textbf{Methodological contribution.} Expanded Welch uses this
  variance sensitivity to choose the degrees of freedom for a two-sample
  test using a Student \(t\) distribution.
  \par
  \end{samepage}

  \item \begin{samepage}\textbf{Empirical contribution.} Controlled simulations check
  the variance approximations and compare false-positive rates, power and
  usable results as sample size, table size, category frequencies and MI
  change. A supporting comparison checks whether sparse-table failures
  persist under another association construction.
  \par
  \end{samepage}

  \item \textbf{Practical contribution.} The evaluation identifies
  selected populations at common MI 0.02 nats where Expanded Welch brings
  false-positive rates closer to 5\% than Normal Wald, the dependence of calibrated power on
  the calibration setting, and estimation errors that make both tests
  unreliable.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and organisation}

The scope of this thesis is the comparison of MI between two populations
using categorical data. Each sample is summarised in a count table, and
both tables use the same fixed row and column categories. The two samples
are independent, and observations within each sample are also independent.
For the main derivation, both populations are assumed to have positive MI
and a positive probability in every cell. The case of zero MI, where
\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, is considered separately.
The main simulations compare Normal Wald and Expanded Welch using
two-sided tests at the 5\% significance level.

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} reviews the background and literature.
Chapters~\ref{chap:baselines} and~\ref{chap:expanded} define the statistic
and derive Expanded Welch. Chapters~\ref{chap:design} and~\ref{chap:results}
present the experiment, results and online-shopping application.
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} brings together the findings, limitations
and future work. Appendix~\ref{app:derivation-details} gives supporting
near-independence calculations, and Appendix~\ref{app:construction}
gives experimental construction, validity and uncertainty details.

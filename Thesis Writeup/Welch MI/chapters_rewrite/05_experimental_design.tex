\chapter{Experimental Design}
\label{chap:design}

The experiment evaluates whether Expanded Welch's larger rejection cutoff
improves performance compared with Normal Wald. It first measures false
positives when the two populations have equal MI, then examines the
estimation errors behind those results,
and finally measures detection when MI differs. Bias and variance diagnostics
link rejection rates to errors in the estimated MI difference and standard
error. Each comparison starts from fixed joint probability tables with
known MI values, so the
true difference is available when assessing the sampled estimates.

\section{Questions and controlled comparisons}

Three questions organise the study: how sample size and population structure
affect false positives, how common population MI changes Expanded Welch's
estimated degrees of freedom, and how its larger rejection cutoff affects
detection of a true MI difference. Table~\ref{tab:design-families}
summarises the comparisons. Holding the other stated factors fixed makes
each change easier to interpret. Specifying the questions, populations,
methods and performance measures follows the simulation-design guidance of
\citet{morris2019}.

The square table sizes are \(2\times2,4\times4,8\times8\) and
\(16\times16\), containing 4, 16, 64 and 256 cells. Each doubling of
the number of categories multiplies the number of cells by four. At a
fixed sample size, observations are therefore distributed over more
possible cells.

Three margin regimes compare uniform category frequencies, the same
uneven frequencies in both populations, and different uneven frequencies.
Under equal MI, the uniform and same-skew regimes provide identical-table
controls. The different-skew regime tests comparisons between distinct
population tables with equal MI.

\section{Constructing joint distributions with known MI}

The construction changes association while preserving the category
frequencies. For one population, draw \(X\) with category probabilities
\(a=(a_1,\ldots,a_k)\). With probability \(\lambda\), set \(Y=X\);
otherwise draw \(Y\) independently with the same probabilities \(a\).
Copying makes matching categories more likely than under independence.
Both branches give \(Y\) the same marginal probabilities, so changing
\(\lambda\) does not change the row or column totals.

For a diagonal cell, an observation can arrive through either branch. For
an off-diagonal cell, it must arrive through the independent branch.
Combining these probabilities gives
\begin{equation}
 p_{ij}(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)a_i a_j
                  +\lambda a_i\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}},
 \qquad 0\leq\lambda<1,
 \label{eq:copying-construction}
\end{equation}
where the indicator is one when the two categories match and zero otherwise.
At \(\lambda=0\), the variables are independent and MI is zero.
Increasing \(\lambda\) increases MI; the target MI determines the required
copying probability. Appendix~\ref{app:construction} gives the numerical
search and shows why the margins remain fixed.

\begin{samepage}
\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Constructing a \(2\times2\) joint distribution with positive MI.}

For \(P\), take \(a=(0.7,0.3)\) and a copying probability of
approximately 0.20347. Cell \((1,1)\) requires \(X=1\), which has
probability 0.7. Given \(X=1\), copying always gives \(Y=1\), whereas
an independent draw gives \(Y=1\) with probability 0.7. Thus
\begin{align*}
 p_{11}&\simeq0.7\{0.20347+(1-0.20347)(0.7)\}=0.53273,\\
 p_{12}&\simeq0.7(1-0.20347)(0.3)=0.16727.
\end{align*}
\end{samepage}
The second equation has no copying term because copying cannot produce
\(X=1,Y=2\). The remaining cells follow the same rule.
For \(Q\), use \(a=(0.8,0.2)\) and a copying probability of approximately
0.26091. These choices give \(I(P)=0.02\) and \(I(Q)=0.03\) nats:
\begin{equation}
 P\simeq\begin{pmatrix}0.53273&0.16727\\0.16727&0.13273\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 Q\simeq\begin{pmatrix}0.68175&0.11825\\0.11825&0.08175\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:design-example-pair}
\end{equation}
Both joint distributions have positive MI, and their true difference has
magnitude 0.01 nats. Sampling changes the estimated MI values, but the
population difference used to assess detection remains 0.01 nats.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Table sizes and margin regimes.}

Equation~\eqref{eq:copying-construction} applies to all four table sizes.
For uneven margins, the first category has probability \(\rho\), and the
remaining mass is shared equally:
\begin{equation}
 m_k(\rho)=\left(\rho,\frac{1-\rho}{k-1},\ldots,
                   \frac{1-\rho}{k-1}\right).
 \label{eq:skew-margin}
\end{equation}
Each population uses the same vector for its row and column margins.
Table~\ref{tab:margin-profiles} specifies the vectors for \(P\) and \(Q\).
The copying probabilities for \(P\) and \(Q\) are chosen separately to
achieve the targets \(I(P)\) and \(I(Q)\). Equal MI therefore gives identical
tables in the uniform and
same-skew regimes, but different tables in the different-skew regime.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Regime & \(P\): rows and columns & \(Q\): rows and columns\\
\midrule

Increasing table size under the uneven-margin rule spreads the remaining
mass over more categories. It changes both the number of cells and their
probabilities. This comparison shows what happens under a common margin
rule as the table grows. Uniform versus same skew instead compares even
and uneven category frequencies at a fixed size; different skew allows the
two populations' frequencies to differ as well.

\section{The three studies and supporting checks}

\subsection{Sample size, table size and margins}

The first study fixes \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\) nats and crosses all four sizes
with all three margin regimes. For each of the twelve fixed population
pairs, sample size varies over
\begin{equation}
 n_P=n_Q\in\{10,20,50,100,250,500,1000,2500,10000,50000\}.
 \label{eq:null-sample-grid}
\end{equation}
These 120 settings show whether the false-positive rate approaches 5\%
as more observations become available, and whether table size or margins
change that progression. Valid-result rates distinguish a genuinely low
false-positive rate from a failure to calculate the test.

The common target of 0.02 nats gives positive MI in all four table sizes
and three margin regimes. Fixing population MI does not fix the
difficulty of estimating it: tables with equal MI can have different
cell probabilities and different MI sampling variances. Keeping each
population pair fixed throughout the sample-size sweep separates
improvement from more observations from a change in the population.
The sweep includes small samples, intermediate samples and large-sample
checks so that both calculation failures and convergence are visible.

\subsection{Population MI and degrees of freedom}

The second study keeps \(4\times4\) tables and different-skew margins,
and changes the common positive MI:
\begin{equation}
 I(P)=I(Q)\in\{0.0001,0.0003,0.001,0.003,0.01,0.02,0.05\}.
 \label{eq:association-grid}
\end{equation}
Repeating this sweep at \(n=100\) and 1,000 separates the effect of
common population MI from a change in sample size. The estimated degrees
of freedom connect the observed rejection rates to the cutoff from the
Student \(t\) distribution. All targets are positive, so this study
approaches independence without evaluating its exact boundary.

Holding table size and margins fixed shows how rejection rates and
estimated degrees of freedom change with population MI. The small positive
targets examine where the first-order MI variance becomes small; the larger
targets show whether the same tests behave differently away from that
region. Two sample sizes show whether the pattern across MI values
persists when more data are available.

\subsection{Detection of a true MI difference}

The third study fixes \(I(P)=0.02\) and sets \(I(Q)=0.02+|\Delta|\),
using
\begin{equation}
 |\Delta|\in\{0,0.001,0.002,0.005,0.01,0.02,0.05,0.1,0.2\}
 \quad\text{nats}.
 \label{eq:detection-grid}
\end{equation}
At \(n=1{,}000\) and 10,000, compare all four sizes under different skew,
then all three margin regimes at \(4\times4\). Keeping the zero-difference
point in each curve makes detection interpretable alongside false positives.
The signed thesis parameter is \(\Delta=I(P)-I(Q)\), which is negative
when \(Q\) has the larger MI; figures use its known nonnegative magnitude.

The two sample sizes connect detection to the null study at the same
population settings. Keeping \(P\)'s MI fixed makes each curve start
from a common baseline as \(Q\)'s MI increases. Small differences show
the range in which the methods may disagree; the larger differences
show whether detection eventually becomes high. Reading each curve
from its zero-difference point prevents high rejection under alternatives
from being mistaken for useful sensitivity when false positives are
already excessive.

\subsection{A supporting change in construction}

An eight-setting check asks whether representative findings persist when
association is concentrated in four cells. Start from the independence
table, add the same amount to cells \((1,1)\) and \((2,2)\), and subtract
it from \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\). Each affected row and column gains and
loses the same amount. This transfer follows the sparse construction of
\citet[Section~3(b)]{berrett2021}.

Use \(4\times4\) and \(16\times16\) different-skew tables at
\(n=1{,}000\) and 10,000,
with MI pairs \((0.02,0.02)\) and \((0.02,0.03)\). Match the targets
and margins to the corresponding copying populations. In a binary table
the two constructions are equivalent; in a larger table copying changes
all diagonal and off-diagonal cells, whereas the transfer changes only four.
The smaller table checks a representative comparison; the larger table
examines whether the sparse-table bias and reversed estimated ordering
persist under a different arrangement of association. Compare rejection,
validity and estimation diagnostics at every matched setting, including
estimated direction under the alternative.

\subsection{A supporting unequal-sample comparison}

A second supporting check changes sample allocation while keeping the
total number of observations fixed at 2,000. It uses the copying
\(4\times4\) different-skew populations with MI pairs
\((0.02,0.02)\) and \((0.02,0.03)\) nats. For each pair, compare
\((n_P,n_Q)=(1000,1000),(400,1600)\) and \((1600,400)\).
Reversing the allocation leaves the populations unchanged: \(Q\) retains
its more uneven margins and has higher MI in the alternative.
This asks whether the same data budget gives different false-positive
and detection rates when one population has fewer observations.

Unequal sample sizes change both the bias corrections in
Equation~\eqref{eq:bias-corrected-difference} and the weights in the
standard error and degrees of freedom. The check applies the same
estimators and validity rules to every allocation. Its two balanced
comparisons are already part of the main studies, so the unequal
allocations add four settings.

The complete design contains 70 population pairs and 240 distinct settings.
Shared settings, such as the zero-difference points in the detection study,
use the same simulation results in each comparison. At 20,000 repetitions
per setting, the study samples 4,800,000 table pairs.

\section{Sampling and performance measures}

For each setting, draw 20,000 independent multinomial count-table pairs
from its fixed \(P\) and \(Q\). The samples from the two populations are
independent, and both methods are applied to the same pair. Copying creates
dependence between \(X\) and \(Y\) within an observation, not between
the two population samples. The MI estimator, bias correction and standard
error are those in Chapter~\ref{chap:baselines}; the Expanded Welch
calculation is given in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}.

All settings use a two-sided 5\% test. The primary rejection rate is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\#\{\text{valid p-values no larger than }0.05\}}{20{,}000}.
 \label{eq:unconditional-rate}
\end{equation}
At equal MI this estimates false positives; when MI differs it measures
detection. Invalid calculations contribute no rejection but are not
evidence for equal MI. The valid-result frequency is therefore reported
alongside rejection. Rates restricted to method-valid or common-valid
samples and their denominators are specified in
Appendix~\ref{app:construction}.

Wilson 95\% intervals quantify simulation uncertainty for individual rates
\citep{wilson1927}. Intervals for the difference between methods use paired
rejection indicators because the tests receive the same data.
For 20,000 repetitions and a rate near 0.05, the Monte Carlo standard error
is approximately 0.00154. Thus departures of only a few thousandths need to
be read alongside the intervals. Section~\ref{sec:simulation-uncertainty}
gives the calculations.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Diagnostics of the shared estimates.}

The known populations also let us measure estimation error. Difference
bias is the mean corrected estimate minus the true difference. The sample
variance across repetitions measures its observed spread, and the mean
squared standard error measures the uncertainty assigned by the tests.
Dividing the mean squared standard error by the observed variance indicates
whether the estimated uncertainty is too large or too small on average.
Comparing the observed variance with
\(V(P)/n_P+V(Q)/n_Q\) separately checks the first-order approximation
with its population inputs.

A supporting jackknife check changes the MI bias correction while keeping
the original standard errors and normal distribution cutoff. It uses the
same samples from four existing \(16\times16\) different-skew copying
settings, specified in Appendix Section~\ref{sec:jackknife-difference-check}.

Degrees-of-freedom summaries use the median and 5th--95th percentile range
among valid Expanded Welch outputs. This range describes differences
between sampled results, not uncertainty in the estimated median.
Empty-cell, row and column counts describe sparse sampling.
The diagnostics retain finite estimates even if the associated test is
invalid; Appendix~\ref{app:construction} specifies the subsets used in
each comparison. These measures help explain the curves without changing
the test or excluding sparse settings.

Finally, a small timing comparison measures the computational cost on
pre-generated \(2\times2\) through \(16\times16\) table pairs at
\(n=1{,}000\) under different skew. Sampling and population construction
are excluded from the timings. Both methods scan the cells, so their
operation counts grow linearly with the number of cells. The timing
procedure and measurement environment are reported with the results.

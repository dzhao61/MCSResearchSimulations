\chapter{Experimental Design}
\label{chap:design}

The simulations draw count tables from populations with known MI,
allowing us to measure errors in the estimated MI difference and its
standard error, and to count false positives when the population MI
values are equal. The evaluation addresses the two research questions in
Section~\ref{sec:research-questions}: whether the variance approximations
used by Expanded Welch are accurate, and how its false-positive rates
and power compare with those of Normal Wald. The design follows the simulation
framework of \citet{morris2019}, specifying the populations, quantities
being estimated, methods and performance measures.

One observation records an \((X,Y)\) outcome. One simulation repetition
draws a sample of observations from each population, producing two count
tables to which both tests are applied.

\section{Setup: Constructing joint distributions with known MI}

Before drawing samples, we construct population probability tables with
specified MI values. Equal MI values allow us to measure false-positive
rates, while different MI values allow us to measure power. For each table,
we first choose how frequently the categories occur, then change the
association between \(X\) and \(Y\) while keeping those frequencies fixed.

The main simulations use square tables in which both variables have
\(k\) categories, labelled \(1,\ldots,k\), giving \(k\times k\) cells
in the joint distribution. Let \(a=(a_1,\ldots,a_k)\) specify the category
frequencies: \(a_i\) is the probability of category \(i\), with
\(a_i>0\) and \(\sum_i a_i=1\). Within one population, we use the same vector for both variables,
so \(a\) specifies the row margins for \(X\) and the column margins
for \(Y\).

To obtain independent variables, we could draw \(X\) using \(a\), then
draw \(Y\) independently using \(a\). Cell \((i,j)\) would have
probability \(a_i a_j\), and MI would be zero. We introduce dependence by allowing \(Y\) to copy the category drawn
for \(X\) with probability \(\lambda\). For each
observation, first draw \(X\) using \(a\), then choose between two ways
of generating \(Y\), independently of the value of \(X\):
\begin{itemize}
 \item With probability \(\lambda\), set \(Y=X\).
 \item With probability \(1-\lambda\), draw \(Y\) independently using \(a\).
\end{itemize}
Because copying guarantees a match whereas an independent draw matches
\(X\) only by chance, increasing \(\lambda\) makes matching categories
occur more often.

Both choices preserve the category frequencies of \(Y\): copying
inherits the probabilities \(a\) used to draw \(X\), while an
independent draw of \(Y\) uses \(a\) directly. Consequently, changing how often we copy changes which
categories occur together, while the row and column margins remain fixed.
Sample row and column proportions fluctuate around these population
probabilities.

Each cell probability is the sum of the contributions from these two
choices. The independent choice, made with probability \(1-\lambda\),
produces \(X=i,Y=j\) with probability \(a_i a_j\) and therefore
contributes \((1-\lambda)a_i a_j\). The copying choice contributes only to diagonal
cells, where the categories match. Reaching cell \((i,i)\) requires both
copying and \(X=i\), so its contribution is \(\lambda a_i\).
Write \(\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}\) for an indicator that equals one when
\(i=j\) and zero otherwise. Adding the two contributions gives
\begin{equation}
 p_{ij}(\lambda)=(1-\lambda)a_i a_j
                  +\lambda a_i\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}},
 \qquad 0\leq\lambda<1,
 \label{eq:copying-construction}
\end{equation}
where the restriction \(\lambda<1\) keeps the independent choice possible
and therefore gives every cell positive probability. This copying
construction is a special case of the mixture model described by
\citet{bocci2010}.

At \(\lambda=0\), MI is zero. Increasing \(\lambda\) increases MI,
although the copying probability needed to reach a particular MI depends
on the category frequencies \(a\). To construct a table with the desired
MI, we hold \(a\) fixed and search numerically for \(\lambda\),
calculating MI from the population table at each candidate value.
Appendix~\ref{app:construction} gives the available MI range, the numerical
search and the fixed-margin calculation.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Constructing a \(2\times2\) joint distribution with positive MI.}

For population \(P\), take \(a=(0.7,0.3)\), so each variable has
probability 0.7 of taking category 1 and probability 0.3 of taking
category 2. With a target MI of 0.02 nats, the numerical search gives
a copying probability of approximately 0.20347.

Cell \((1,1)\) requires \(X=1\), which has probability 0.7. Given
\(X=1\), copying always gives \(Y=1\), while an independent draw gives
\(Y=1\) with probability 0.7. Cell \((1,2)\) also requires \(X=1\),
but copying cannot give \(Y=2\). It therefore requires the independent
choice followed by a draw of category 2, which has probability 0.3.
Multiplying these conditional probabilities by the probability of
\(X=1\) gives
\begin{align*}
 p_{11}&\simeq0.7\{0.20347+(1-0.20347)(0.7)\}=0.53273,\\
 p_{12}&\simeq0.7(1-0.20347)(0.3)=0.16727.
\end{align*}
Both off-diagonal cells have probability \((1-\lambda)(0.7)(0.3)\), so
\(p_{21}=p_{12}\). The second row totals 0.3, giving
\(p_{22}\simeq0.3-0.16727=0.13273\).

For population \(Q\), using \(a=(0.8,0.2)\) and a target MI of 0.03 nats
gives a copying probability of approximately 0.26091. The resulting
tables are
\begin{equation}
 P\simeq\begin{pmatrix}0.53273&0.16727\\0.16727&0.13273\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 Q\simeq\begin{pmatrix}0.68175&0.11825\\0.11825&0.08175\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:design-example-pair}
\end{equation}
These tables have \(I(P)=0.02\) and \(I(Q)=0.03\) nats, so the true
signed difference is \(\Delta=I(P)-I(Q)=-0.01\) nats. To construct an
equal-MI comparison, we instead choose the
same target MI for both populations and search for each copying
probability separately.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Table sizes and margin regimes.}

The experiment repeats this construction for square tables of sizes
\(2\times2,4\times4,8\times8\) and \(16\times16\), containing
4, 16, 64 and 256 cells. Each doubling of the number of categories multiplies the number of
cells by four, so a fixed sample must be distributed over more possible
cells.

At each table size, we compare three patterns of category frequencies,
called margin regimes. Uniform margins assign probability \(1/k\) to
every category. For uneven margins, the first category has probability
\(\rho\), and the remaining probability \(1-\rho\) is shared equally
among the other \(k-1\) categories. We write this vector as
\begin{equation}
 m_k(\rho)=\left(\rho,\frac{1-\rho}{k-1},\ldots,
                   \frac{1-\rho}{k-1}\right).
 \label{eq:skew-margin}
\end{equation}
For example, assigning probability 0.8 to the first of four categories gives
\begin{equation*}
 m_4(0.8)=\left(0.8,\frac{0.2}{3},\frac{0.2}{3},\frac{0.2}{3}\right).
\end{equation*}

In Table~\ref{tab:margin-profiles}, the same-skew regime uses the same
uneven frequencies in both populations, whereas the different-skew regime
uses a first-category probability of 0.7 in \(P\) and 0.8 in \(Q\).
Thus the row and column margins agree within each population, while the
margins can differ between populations.

\begin{table}[htbp]

\label{tab:margin-profiles}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Regime & \(P\): rows and columns & \(Q\): rows and columns\\
\midrule

Under equal MI, the uniform and same-skew regimes give identical joint
probability tables, \(P=Q\): both populations have the same margins and
the same copying probability. The different-skew regime instead gives
different tables with equal MI because the category frequencies differ.
Comparing the regimes therefore measures false positives under both
identical tables and distinct tables with equal MI.

\section{RQ1: Checking the variance approximations}

Expanded Welch chooses its component degrees of freedom from
\(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\) and \(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\), and likewise for \(Q\).
To check these quantities, we hold the
population table \(P\) and sample size \(n_P\) fixed, then draw repeated
count tables. From each table, we estimate the pointwise-MI variance \(V(P)\) by
\(\Vhat(P)\), then divide this estimate by \(n_P\) to obtain that
population's estimated contribution to the variance of the MI difference.
The average of the \(\Vhat(P)\) values estimates \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\);
their variation across draws estimates \(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\).

The observation-level sensitivity derived in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}
describes how the estimated MI variance responds to changes in the table.
Let \(\tau^2(P)\) be its population variance. The first-order predictions are
\begin{equation}
 \E\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx V(P),\qquad
 \Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx\frac{\tau^2(P)}{n_P}.
 \label{eq:redesign-moment-approximations}
\end{equation}
We compare \(V(P)\) with the observed mean of \(\Vhat(P)\), and
\(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) with its observed sampling variance. To examine the effect of estimating the sensitivity from each sample,
we also average the sample-based prediction \(\widehat\tau^2(P)/n_P\)
across the repeated tables and compare this average with the same observed
sampling variance.

We next compare the component degrees of freedom with the sampling-moment
target from Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded}:
\begin{equation}
 \nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}
 =\frac{2\{\E\Vhat(P)\}^{2}}{\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}}.
 \label{eq:design-moment-target}
\end{equation}
Using the repeated samples to estimate the mean and variance in this
target, we compare it with \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\), calculated from
the population probabilities, and the average of the sampled
\(\widehat\nu_P\) values. Averaging \(\widehat\nu_P\) need not recover
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\) because each estimate is a nonlinear ratio of
sample-based quantities, whereas the target uses the sampling mean and
variance of \(\Vhat(P)\). For the sampled estimates, the ratio
\(\operatorname{mean}(\widehat\nu_P)/\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\) equals one
when the average estimate matches the target; a ratio below one means
that the estimated degrees of freedom are lower on average. We make the same checks for \(Q\) and report the included fraction
whenever a summary uses only positive finite degrees of freedom.
Appendix~\ref{sec:moment-target-details} gives the definitions and algebra.

Table~\ref{tab:design-primary-grids} specifies two grids: the first varies
sample size at common MI 0.02 nats, while the second includes very small
positive MI to examine behaviour near independence. In this latter range,
the first-order variance becomes small and quadratic sampling terms can
matter. Both grids use all
three margin regimes and equal sample sizes \(n_P=n_Q=n\). Each row
combines every listed table size, MI value and sample size.

\section{RQ2.1: False-positive rates under equal MI}

To measure false positives, we draw a count table from each of two
populations with equal MI in every repetition, so every rejection is
a false positive. Normal Wald and Expanded Welch use the
same bias-corrected MI difference and estimated standard error from
Chapter~\ref{chap:baselines}, but compare the resulting statistic with
different reference distributions. Normal Wald uses the standard normal
distribution, while Expanded Welch uses a Student \(t\) distribution
with MI-specific degrees of freedom estimated from the two sampled count
tables.

Both tests use a two-sided 5\% nominal test. The broad null comparison
uses the first grid in Table~\ref{tab:design-primary-grids}: common MI
0.02 nats, four table sizes, three margin regimes and ten sample sizes,
giving 120 settings. A false-positive rate above 5\% means excessive rejection, while a rate
below 5\% means conservative rejection. Because a rule that never rejects
has zero false positives and zero power, we assess nominal calibration
by closeness to 5\% and evaluate power separately.

We also report the fraction of valid results. Because invalid calculations
contribute no rejection to the all-repetition rate, failures can lower that
rate without improving the decisions made on usable samples.

To check whether rejection behaviour depends on the association
construction, a supporting comparison replaces copying by a probability
transfer within four cells while preserving the margins and target MI.
Appendix~\ref{app:construction} gives the construction and settings, and
Section~\ref{sec:null-estimator-accuracy} reports the sparse-table comparison.

\section{RQ2.2: Nominal and calibrated power}

Power is the probability of rejecting equal MI when the population MI
values differ. We first compare power using the nominal 5\% cutoffs.
A test that rejects less often under equal MI can also reject less often
under alternatives, so we then compare power after adjusting the cutoffs
to match rejection at a specified equal-MI setting.

The focused comparison uses settings A--C in
Table~\ref{tab:redesign-focus-settings}. Earlier simulations identified
A and B as settings where Expanded Welch brought false-positive rates
closer to 5\%; their power is evaluated on new independent samples.
Setting C provides a larger-sample comparison.

To choose the MI differences for a power curve, we first measure the
sampling variation under equal MI. An independent null simulation estimates the standard deviation (SD) of
the corrected MI difference, giving the fitted value \(\sigma_0\). Measuring differences in units
of \(\sigma_0\) makes the three curves comparable relative to their null
sampling variation.

\begin{tabular}{@{}llrlr@{}}
\toprule Setting & Margins & \(n_P=n_Q\) & Size & \shortstack{Fitted null SD\\\(\sigma_0\) (nats)}\\\midrule
A & Uniform & 20 & \(4\times4\) & 0.154967\\
B & Different skew & 100 & \(8\times8\) & 0.065138\\
C & Different skew & 10,000 & \(4\times4\) & 0.003175\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{samepage}
For each curve, we hold \(I(P)=0.02\) nats and increase \(I(Q)\) so
that the population MI values are separated by \(h\) fitted null SDs.
The populations satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 I(P)&=0.02,\qquad I(Q)=0.02+h\sigma_0,\\
 h&\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5,3,3.5,4\},\\
 \Delta&=I(P)-I(Q)=-h\sigma_0.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:design-focused-populations}
\end{equation}
\end{samepage}
Thus \(h=0\) gives equal MI, while positive \(h\) gives larger MI in
\(Q\) and a negative signed difference. For example, at C, two null SDs are approximately
\(2(0.003175)=0.00635\) nats, giving an alternative with
\(I(P)=0.02\), \(I(Q)\simeq0.02635\) and
\(\Delta\simeq-0.00635\) nats. The fitted SD and the resulting population
tables stay fixed during evaluation. Appendix~\ref{app:construction}
gives their construction and the fixed SD values.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Power after matching null rejection.}

Empirical calibration chooses a separate p-value threshold for each
test through the following procedure for each full power curve:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item Use the independent fitting samples with \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\) nats,
 retaining the curve's margins, table size and sample sizes.
 \item Choose each rule's threshold to give at most 5\% rejection in
 that fitting batch, and hold it fixed across the curve.
 \item Apply the thresholds to separate equal-MI evaluation samples and
 to the alternative samples, measuring the achieved false-positive rates
 and power.
\end{enumerate}
The thresholds are fitted for these known simulation settings, where
calibrated power compares the tests after matching false-positive rates.

Normal Wald ranks simulated datasets by \(|T|\): larger \(|T|\) always
gives a smaller p-value and stronger evidence against equal MI.
Expanded Welch's p-value also depends on degrees of freedom that change
with the sampled tables, so it can rank the datasets differently.
At a common p-value threshold, its rejections are a subset of Wald's.
Calibration fits a separate threshold for each test, so that earlier
nesting no longer determines which test has greater power.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Changing the calibration setting.}

Equal MI can occur at different common MI values. To examine how that
choice affects the power comparison, we repeat calibration at \(h=2\)
and \(h=4\) for A--C using the two equal-MI settings in
Table~\ref{tab:design-calibration-populations}.

Both calibration nulls retain the alternative's margins and sample
sizes, but the lower null sets both MI values to the alternative's smaller
value, whereas the upper null sets both to its larger value.

rate elsewhere under equal MI. Appendix~\ref{sec:calibration-sensitivity-design} gives

\section{Shared evaluation: Sampling and uncertainty}

At one evaluation setting, each repetition draws a count table from
\(P\) and an independent count table from \(Q\). Both tests use these
same two tables, so their rejection decisions
can be compared on the same sampled data. Each batch contains 100,000 independent repetitions to improve the
precision of the reported rates, while each test uses only \(n_P\)
and \(n_Q\) observations within a repetition. The study uses three types of batch:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item \textbf{Base evaluation:} approximation checks, null comparisons,
 power alternatives and the supporting construction comparison, measuring
 rejection and estimation accuracy.
 \item \textbf{Independent fitting:} the equal-MI populations for A--C,
 used to estimate the null SDs and fit calibration cutoffs.
 \item \textbf{Calibration sensitivity:} six upper-null settings, each
 with separate fitting and validation batches.
\end{enumerate}
Fitting and evaluation use separate random streams, with settings shared
between comparisons sampled once within each role.

Table~\ref{tab:design-performance-measures} defines the measures for each
evaluation setting.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Performance measures calculated from repeated samples.}
\label{tab:design-performance-measures}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{.28\textwidth}X@{}}
\toprule
Measure & Calculation\\
\midrule
Rejection rate &
\(\dfrac{\text{number of valid rejections}}{\text{number of simulation repetitions}}\)\\
\addlinespace
Valid-result fraction &
\(\dfrac{\text{number of valid outputs}}{\text{number of simulation repetitions}}\)\\
\addlinespace
Difference bias &
\(\operatorname{mean}(\Deltahat)-\Delta\)\\
\addlinespace
Variance ratio &
\(\dfrac{\operatorname{mean}(\SEhat^2)}{\text{observed variance of }\Deltahat}\)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Bias uses finite MI differences even when a test is invalid. The variance ratio uses repetitions with both finite \(\Deltahat\)
and finite \(\SEhat^2\), with one indicating agreement. Ratios below
one mean that the squared standard error understates the observed
difference variance on average, while ratios above one indicate
overstatement. Two-sided power includes rejections in either direction.
Appendix~\ref{sec:estimation-diagnostics} specifies the inclusion rules
and complete calculations.

Rejection rates have Wilson 95\% confidence intervals \citep{wilson1927},
which describe uncertainty from the finite number of simulation repetitions.
Intervals for method differences account for both methods being applied
to the same simulated data. For
calibrated power, we resample fitting and evaluation outputs and refit
the cutoffs, so the intervals also include cutoff-fitting uncertainty.
These intervals apply pointwise to one rate or comparison at a specified
setting, with the power-difference intervals assessed against a practical
tolerance of \(\pm1\) percentage point.
Appendix~\ref{app:construction} gives the interval calculations.

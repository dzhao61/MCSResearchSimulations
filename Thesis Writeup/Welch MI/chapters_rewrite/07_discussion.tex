\chapter{Implications and Limitations}
\label{chap:discussion}

\section{Implications for using Expanded Welch}

Normal Wald and Expanded Welch use the same estimated MI difference and
standard error. Expanded Welch can only make a valid two-sided p-value
larger. This is helpful when Wald rejects an equal-MI null too often;
it is harmful when Wald already rejects too rarely. Lower rejection is
therefore not by itself evidence of better calibration.
In sparse samples, Expanded Welch's extra component requirements can prevent
it from returning a p-value. This also lowers the reported rejection rate,
so calibration must be read alongside the frequency of usable results.

The trade-off varies with table size, margins, starting MI and sampling
allocation, including when equal-MI populations have different margins.
Neither method is well calibrated across all tested settings.
For applications resembling a tested regime, Normal
Wald remains the simpler baseline. Expanded Welch can be a
supplementary check if a separate calibration study shows
that Wald rejects equal MI too often and the expanded calculation is usually valid.
Method choice should be set before examining the p-values.

The extra computation is not the principal obstacle. In the measured
implementation, an Expanded Welch call takes about 1.75 times as long
as a Wald call, but both have the same \(O(rc)\) operation growth for
an \(r\times c\) table. The statistical limitations matter more than
this timing difference.

\begin{samepage}
\section{Limits of the evidence}

The rejection rates and Monte Carlo intervals apply to the constructed
population pairs. The intervals quantify uncertainty from the finite number
of simulated samples; they do not describe how performance varies across
untested populations.
\par
\end{samepage}

One specific gap concerns the association patterns in equal-MI pairs.
The main construction changes four cells. Rare-cell, spread and
log-linear constructions test other patterns, but the study does not
pair equal-MI populations whose association is placed in different
cells. Such pairs would test whether the method comparison changes when
the populations differ in association location as well as their margins.

The methods are studied with two independent samples from fixed
categorical alphabets, a two-sided 5\% threshold and the stated sample
ladders. The analysis does not establish performance for paired
observations, continuous MI estimators, growing alphabets or a real-data
workflow.
Exact independence also needs a different mathematical approximation. The
first-order MI variance vanishes there.
Section~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} derives the second-order
approximation needed there.

\chapter{Implications and Limitations}
\label{chap:discussion}

\section{Implications for using Expanded Welch}

Normal Wald and Expanded Welch use the same estimated MI difference and
standard error. Expanded Welch can only make a valid two-sided p-value
larger, reducing rejection for the same sampled tables. When
Wald's false-positive rate exceeds 5\%, reducing it towards 5\% can
help. When Wald already rejects too rarely, the same adjustment moves
the rate further from the target and can reduce detection. A comparison
of the two methods therefore needs both measures: false positives show
whether the reduction improves calibration, and detection shows its
cost when population MI differs.

The bias and direction findings show why a lower rejection rate can leave
the main estimation problem unresolved. Unequal biases can create an
apparent change in association when population MI is equal, as
Equation~\eqref{eq:focused-null-bias-shift} demonstrates. They can also
reverse the estimated ordering when the population MI values differ,
including among rejected comparisons. Increasing the cutoff changes
neither the centre nor the sign of the estimated difference. A significant
comparison with the wrong estimated direction can therefore lead to the
opposite substantive conclusion. Reliable interpretation requires
accurate MI estimates as well as a suitable rejection cutoff.

A usable calculation and a well-calibrated test are also different
requirements. Validity here means that the quantities needed for the
calculation are finite and satisfy the method's positivity conditions.
It does not mean that the resulting p-values achieve the intended
false-positive rate. The fully valid large-table comparison in
Figure~\ref{fig:focused-null-sizes} demonstrates that distinction.
Conversely, sparse samples
can prevent Expanded Welch from returning a p-value, lowering its
reported rejection rate without providing a decision about equal MI.
Rejection and valid-result frequency therefore need to be read together.

For a categorical feature--label comparison, the estimated MI difference
reports the size and direction of the observed change. Its standard
error describes the sampling uncertainty assigned by the method, and
the p-value supplies the test decision. Reporting these quantities
together makes the comparison more informative than reporting a
p-value alone. If Expanded Welch is used, its estimated degrees of
freedom explain the additional cutoff adjustment. A small p-value is
evidence against equal MI within the test's approximation; a large
p-value does not establish that the population MI values are equal.

Normal Wald remains the simpler baseline, but neither method is reliable
in every studied setting. An application-specific comparison should match
category definitions, sample sizes and margin patterns, and include
plausible association strengths and distinct equal-MI tables. Judge Expanded
Welch by whether its lower rejection brings excessive false positives closer
to 5\% without an unacceptable loss of detection or usable results. Choose
the method using that comparison before examining the observed p-values.

\begin{samepage}
\section{Limits of the evidence}

The rejection rates and Monte Carlo intervals apply to the constructed
population pairs. The intervals quantify uncertainty from the finite number
of simulated samples; they do not describe how performance varies across
untested populations.
\par
\end{samepage}

The primary populations use a copying mixture with identical row and
column margins within each population. This gives a clear way to vary
association without changing category frequencies. The four-cell check
supports the representative \(4\times4\) method comparison and
reproduces the main \(16\times16\) estimation failures at its matched
settings, although detection changes numerically with the construction.
Copying favours matching categories in both
populations, whereas the four-cell transfer concentrates association
in the same chosen block. The different-skew regime changes category
frequencies while keeping those association locations aligned. Its
results therefore demonstrate that distinct equal-MI tables can be
difficult to compare, but do not describe every way two tables with
equal MI can differ. A broader assessment would vary association
locations as well as their strengths and margins, including unequal
row and column margins within a population.

Equal sample sizes simplify the main studies by making the two leading
bias corrections cancel in \(\Deltahat\). The remaining difference
bias in the experiment consequently comes from estimation errors not
removed by that common correction. With unequal sample sizes, the
corrections no longer cancel, and the two contributions to the standard
error and degrees of freedom can become less balanced. The derivation
allows unequal sample sizes. The supporting fixed-total check assesses
both allocation directions in two \(4\times4\) different-skew pairs.
There, imbalance changes bias, uncertainty and detection, while the
Expanded Welch adjustment lowers already-conservative null rates.
This extends the comparison beyond equal samples, but a wider allocation
study would be needed for other table sizes and population structures.

The sampling assumptions also limit transfer to other applications.
Paired observations would require a covariance term in the variance of
the difference, whereas the present samples are independent. Continuous
MI estimators have different estimation errors from fixed categorical
tables. Similarly, increasing the number of categories with sample size
changes the fixed-alphabet setting of the derivation. The empirical
recommendation concerns the independent categorical comparisons studied
here, at a two-sided 5\% threshold. The online-shopping application
illustrates interpretation on observed data, but its unknown population
MI values do not allow a direct measurement of false positives or detection.

Exact independence needs a different mathematical approximation because
the first-order MI variance vanishes there. The association-strength
study also shows that being positive is not enough to make that
approximation accurate at a particular sample size: small positive MI
can remain close to the problematic region. This explains why improving
the first-order cutoff and improving the approximation near independence
are separate tasks.
Section~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} derives the second-order
approximation needed there.

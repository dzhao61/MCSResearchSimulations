\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

The proposed correction models how the estimated MI variance changes across
samples. The experiments show why this alone is insufficient: Wald and
Expanded Welch use the same observed MI difference and plug-in standard
error. Changing the reference distribution leaves any finite-sample error
in those quantities untouched.

\section{Answers to the research questions}

The first question was how to choose degrees of freedom for an MI test.
Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} shows how one extra observation changes the
estimated MI variance. That observation changes not only its cell but also
the row and column totals used in other MI calculations. Measuring how much
the variance estimate changes provides the degrees of freedom used by
Expanded Welch.

The second question was whether Expanded Welch offers a useful improvement
over Normal Wald. Table~\ref{tab:null-paired-examples} shows both sides of
the false-positive trade-off. In one case Wald rejects equal MI too often,
and Expanded Welch moves the rate toward 0.05. In another, Wald already
rejects too rarely, and the same adjustment moves the rate farther from
0.05. In very sparse settings, some low rejection rates arise because the
method fails to return a p-value. Thus a smaller false-positive rate in one
case is not evidence that Expanded Welch is better overall. Under unequal
MI, it usually detects fewer differences, although the methods are not being
compared at matched false-positive rates.

The answer also depends on the populations and samples being compared. The
experiment varies table size, uneven probabilities, starting MI, the cells
carrying the association, and the allocation of samples between the groups.
These features can change rejection rates even when the true MI difference
is held fixed. In particular, tables with the same row totals, column totals
and MI can still have different cell probabilities and behave differently
when sampled.

For the tested populations with MI 0.02, both methods reject equal MI at
close to the target 5\% rate when each sample contains 50,000 observations.
They still reject too rarely for the tested populations with very small MI.
In the measured implementation, one Expanded Welch calculation takes about
1.75 times as long as one Wald calculation. These results do not support
Expanded Welch as a general replacement for Wald.

\section{What the correction can and cannot change}

Both methods compute the same \(\Deltahat\), \(\SEhat\) and \(T\). With
valid positive degrees of freedom, Expanded Welch has a heavier-tailed
reference and a two-sided p-value no smaller than Wald's for the same table
pair. This gives a direct explanation for many plots: the correction lowers
rejection whether the Wald rate is too high or already too low. It is not an
adaptive calibration method that can move the threshold in both directions.
It also does not alter the leading bias correction or re-estimate the
numerator's finite-sample distribution.

The estimated MI difference and its standard error come from the same count
tables. Independence between the two groups lets us add their variance
contributions, but it does not make the MI difference independent of its
estimated standard error. The diagnostic in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} shows
that both can differ from their large-sample approximations. Adjusting the
reference for the standard error alone therefore cannot guarantee an exact
Student distribution or correct every small-sample error.

Near independence, the calculation in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} gives an
approximate component degree of freedom of \(nI\). When MI is small, that
number can remain small even with a sizeable sample. The resulting Student
reference requires a larger observed difference to reject, just where the
large-sample approximation to MI is also least reliable.

At exact independence, the usual first-order MI variance is zero. An MI
estimate from a finite sample can still vary, but a different, second-order
approximation is then needed. Comparing two positive MI values and testing
one table for independence are therefore different problems. Appendix~\ref{app:independence-boundary}
gives the mathematical distinction.

\section{Interpretation of invalid samples}

The validity diagnostics matter most where expected counts are very small.
Wald needs a usable total estimated variance. The expanded implementation
additionally requires each component sensitivity variance and effective
degree of freedom to be positive and finite. A sample can therefore yield a
valid Wald p-value but no Expanded Welch p-value. Part of this extra
invalidity comes from the implementation rule rather than the combined
formula, as shown in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation-details}. The primary
unconditional rate records a failed calculation as a non-rejection.

That rate answers an operational question: how often does the implemented
procedure produce a valid rejection? It should not be mistaken for the
conditional rejection probability among valid tables. Nor does a failure to
return a p-value constitute a true negative. The saved validity and paired
results allow readers to distinguish fewer rejections from fewer usable
tests.

\section{Practical conclusion and limits of the evidence}

For the tested task, Normal Wald remains the simpler general baseline.
Expanded Welch can be useful as a conservative sensitivity analysis in a
regime where its validity is high and Wald's null rejection is demonstrably
liberal. It should not be installed as a universal replacement merely
because one null table shows a smaller false-positive rate. In applications,
the population truth is unknown, so the most relevant calibration study is
one matched to the anticipated alphabet, sample size and plausible margins.
Selecting between the methods after inspecting a single p-value is not a
validated selection rule.

Several limitations bound this conclusion. The population families are
deliberately constructed fixed examples, not a probability sample of
all joint distributions. Twenty thousand replicates make a rate at a fixed
population relatively precise; they do not make the 534 selected population
pairs representative of every application. The tables are at most
\(8\times8\) square and \(3\times5\) rectangular, with the stated finite
sample ladders and only a two-sided nominal level of 0.05. The main
construction emphasises a local four-cell association; spread, rare-cell and
log-linear checks probe but do not exhaust other possibilities. The study
does not validate paired observations, continuous estimators, a real-data
workflow or growing alphabets.

The protocol was frozen before the final simulation, after exploratory
development of the method and design. It is a reproducible final run without
external preregistration. Configurations
displayed in several sections are the same sampled run. Repeating their
figure appearances does not provide independent confirmation. The recorded
Monte Carlo intervals also address repeated sampling of each fixed pair, not
the broader uncertainty of generalising to an untested population.

The alternative curves use the common nominal 0.05 threshold. They show
practical detection under the tested procedures, not power after giving each
method a simulation-specific critical value.

Future work should address the joint finite-sample behaviour of the
numerator and denominator or use a calibrated reference tailored to sparse
tables. The transition near independence is a separate problem
because the first-order variance tends to zero. A likelihood-ratio or
resampling alternative must be assessed when \(I(P)=I(Q)\) but \(P\ne Q\),
not imported from a test of full distributional equality or one-table
independence without checking its target. These are directions for new
validation, not remedies established by the present results.

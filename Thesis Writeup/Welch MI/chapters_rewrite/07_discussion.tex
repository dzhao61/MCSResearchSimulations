\chapter{Implications and Limitations}
\label{chap:discussion}

Chapter~\ref{chap:results} interprets the figures as each question is
answered. This chapter draws together what those results mean for using the
test and how far the conclusions can be taken.

\section{Implications for using Expanded Welch}

Normal Wald and Expanded Welch use the same estimated MI difference and
standard error. Expanded Welch can only make a valid two-sided p-value
larger. This is helpful when Wald rejects an equal-MI null too often;
it is harmful when Wald already rejects too rarely. Its extra component
validity requirements can also reduce the number of samples for which a
p-value is available. Lower rejection is therefore not by itself evidence
of better calibration.

The tested populations include equal-MI pairs with different margins,
so the comparison goes beyond the special case \(P=Q\). They show that
table size, margin shape, starting MI and sampling allocation can change
the trade-off. No single numerical adjustment worked well across all
those settings. For applications resembling a tested regime, Normal
Wald remains the simpler baseline. Expanded Welch can be a
conservative sensitivity check if a separate calibration study shows
that Wald is too liberal and the expanded calculation is usually valid.
Choosing the method after seeing which p-value is more favourable would
not be a calibrated test.

The extra computation is not the principal obstacle. In the measured
implementation, an Expanded Welch call takes about 1.75 times as long
as a Wald call, but both have the same \(O(rc)\) operation growth for
an \(r\times c\) table. The statistical limitations matter more than
this timing difference.

\section{Limits of the evidence}

The study evaluates fixed, deliberately constructed population pairs,
not a random sample from every possible joint distribution. Its 20,000
replicates per configuration make the rate for each fixed pair precise;
they do not make the selected pairs representative of every application.
The main construction changes four cells. Rare-cell, spread and
log-linear constructions test other patterns, but the study does not
pair equal-MI populations whose association is placed in different
cells. That is a specific remaining design limitation.

The methods are studied with two independent samples from fixed
categorical alphabets, a two-sided 5\% threshold and the stated sample
ladders. The analysis does not establish performance for paired
observations, continuous MI estimators, growing alphabets or a real-data
workflow. Exact independence is a separate boundary because the
first-order MI variance vanishes there. The dedicated check and
Appendix~\ref{app:independence-boundary} describe that boundary but
do not turn the positive-MI test into a general independence test.

The pointwise Monte Carlo intervals quantify simulation uncertainty for the
chosen populations, not uncertainty about performance in untested populations.

These limits point to two distinct next steps: studying the joint
finite-sample behaviour of the estimated MI difference and its standard
error, and validating any alternative test under the equal-MI null
when \(P\ne Q\). A method for the stronger null \(P=Q\) cannot be
adopted for that question without checking its assumptions.

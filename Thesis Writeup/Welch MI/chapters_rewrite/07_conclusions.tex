\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusion}

\section{Contributions and main findings}

This thesis develops and evaluates a test for comparing mutual information
(MI) between two categorical variables using independent samples from two
populations. The proposed Expanded Welch--Satterthwaite test (Expanded
Welch) retains Normal Wald's estimated MI difference and standard error but
uses a Student \(t\) reference distribution. The mathematical contribution
is a first-order approximation to how the variance estimates underlying
the standard error change between samples. Their mean and sampling
variability determine the degrees of freedom, which control the rejection
cutoff.

Across the tested populations, Expanded Welch brings some excessive
false-positive rates closer to the 5\% target, but lowers rejection further
where Wald already rejects too rarely. Its larger cutoff can reduce power,
the probability of detecting a real MI difference, while leaving errors in
the estimated MI difference and standard error used by both tests unchanged.
In the online-shopping application, both tests give evidence of a stronger
month--purchase association among returning visitors than new visitors,
including after adjustment for the six exploratory comparisons. Neither
test rejects equal weekend--purchase MI, although this non-rejection does
not establish equality.

\subsection{RQ1: Accuracy of variance approximations}

In the tested populations, the moment approximations improve as samples
grow, although larger tables require more observations. The population
first-order formula squares the predicted mean of each pointwise-MI
variance estimate, so underestimating this mean magnifies the downward
error in the numerator. In the experiments, the resulting degrees of
freedom fall below the target calculated from repeated samples even where
the sampling variance of these estimates is predicted fairly accurately.
Replacing population probabilities with sample
proportions introduces further error into this variance prediction.

When the two variables are nearly independent, sampling noise creates
apparent association in the observed tables, producing substantial variation
that the first-order approximation misses. The sample-based calculation
retains this apparent association, so the average estimated component
degrees of freedom remain several units above zero in the \(4\times4\)
experiment with 1,000 observations per population. The population
first-order prediction instead approaches zero as MI decreases at this
fixed sample size. The calculation in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} includes the additional sampling
variation and explains this separation.

\subsection{RQ2: False-positive rates under equal MI}

In the uniform \(4\times4\) setting in
Table~\ref{tab:balanced-null-diagnostics}, an underestimated standard error
contributes to excessive rejection, as in the regression examples reviewed
in Section~\ref{sec:welch-background} \citep{bell2002,kauermann2001}.
Expanded Welch's larger cutoff brings rejection closer to 5\%.
In the same-skew \(16\times16\) setting, however, the standard error is too
large and bias in the estimated MI difference is small, so Wald already
rejects too rarely and the larger cutoff reduces rejection further. The
different-skew \(16\times16\) setting instead shows how bias moves the
estimated difference away from zero even when the population MI values are
equal.

Under different-skew \(16\times16\) margins at common MI 0.005 nats
and 1,000 observations per population, false-positive rates are 36.38\%
with Wald and 34.38\% with Expanded Welch, far above the 5\% target.
Both methods return usable results in every repetition, so these high
rates reflect inaccurate inference rather than failed calculations. At
the same common MI, both methods remain below the
5\% target in all three \(16\times16\) margin regimes even with
50,000 observations per population. Similar rejection rates at larger
samples therefore do not by themselves establish accurate false-positive
rates.

\subsection{RQ3: Power across MI differences}

At the usual 5\% significance level, Expanded Welch cannot reject where
Wald would not, so it cannot have higher power under these same testing
rules. At the smallest samples, some Expanded Welch calculations also fail,
contributing to its lower reported power, whereas with 10,000 observations
per population the power difference is below 0.32 percentage points across
all tested settings.

In sparse tables, bias can make the estimated difference point to the wrong
population as having greater MI, even when the test rejects equality.
This bias can also oppose the population difference so that increasing the true
MI gap initially moves the average estimate closer to zero and reduces
rejection. With sufficiently large samples, the bias diminishes and larger
MI gaps become easier to detect.

\section{Limitations}

The main derivation assumes independent categorical observations, a fixed
set of categories, positive probability for every category combination,
and nonzero population variance terms in the degrees-of-freedom
calculation. Paired samples would require accounting for dependence between
them, while continuous variables or a growing number of categories would
require extending the present derivation. Independence between the two
variables within a population also requires separate treatment, using the
additional sampling approximation in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}.

The experiments use one population construction, which increases the
probability of matching categories while keeping category frequencies fixed
and equal for rows and columns within each table. Although the experiments
vary selected table sizes and MI values, they do not examine other
association patterns or unequal row and column frequencies. The power study
further restricts the comparisons by fixing one population's MI and only
increasing the other's, so other starting MI values and the reverse
direction of change remain untested.

Because all simulations use equally sized samples, the same leading bias
correction is subtracted from each MI estimate and cancels from their
difference, leaving the correction's performance with unequal sample sizes
untested. The two MI estimates can nevertheless have different remaining
biases, so their difference can still be biased even when sample sizes are
equal.

Both tests are evaluated at the usual 5\% significance level, with
confidence intervals quantifying simulation uncertainty at individual
population and sample-size settings. Because the tests' actual
false-positive rates can differ, the power comparison describes detection
under these usual testing rules rather than at a common observed
false-positive rate. These rates can be assessed in the simulations because
the population MI values are known, whereas the online-shopping application
has unknown population MI values and therefore illustrates interpretation
on observed data without assessing false-positive accuracy.

The empirical comparison is restricted to Normal Wald, so it does not
establish how Expanded Welch performs against resampling procedures or
tests using other MI estimators.

\section{Future work}

Future work should first examine whether improved estimates of the MI
difference and standard error reduce the departures observed here.
Changing one estimator at a time would help distinguish their effects on
false-positive rates, power and the estimated direction of the MI
difference.

Retaining terms omitted by the first-order approximation could improve the
predicted average of the variance estimates used in the degrees-of-freedom
calculation. Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4} shows that the error in this
predicted average changes with MI and category frequencies, so a correction
should account for the population table and be evaluated through its effects
on false-positive rates and power. Near independence, the reference
distribution for the MI difference could also retain the variation from
sampling noise that the first-order approximation misses. Any resulting
test must preserve the equal-MI hypothesis even when the populations have
different probability tables.

Broader experiments could test whether the findings persist across other
starting MI values, either population having greater MI, unequal sample
sizes, and other patterns of category frequencies and association. Within
these comparisons, simulations based on plausible application populations
could also examine sensitivity to rare category combinations and behaviour
when distinct populations have equal MI.

The derived degrees of freedom bring some excessive false-positive rates
closer to 5\%, at the cost of reduced power. The remaining failures show
why changing the cutoff alone is insufficient: reliable MI comparisons in
the affected sparse settings also require better estimates of the
difference and its standard error.

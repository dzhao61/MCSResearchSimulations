\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusion}

\section{Contributions and main findings}

This thesis derives an Expanded Welch--Satterthwaite test for comparing
categorical MI across independent populations by calculating the
observation-level sensitivity of the estimated pointwise-MI variance. This
sensitivity accounts for changes in cell probabilities, margins and
pointwise MI. The variance of this sensitivity gives the first-order approximation
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\approx\tau^2(P)/n_P\), which moment matching uses
to choose MI-specific degrees of freedom for a Student \(t\) distribution.

\subsection{RQ1: Accuracy of variance approximations}

The predicted sampling variance of \(\Vhat(P)\) can be close to its
observed value even when the sampling mean \(\E\{\Vhat(P)\}\) remains
above \(V(P)\). Because the moment target in Equation~\eqref{eq:design-moment-target}
squares the mean in its numerator, replacing that sampling mean by the
smaller population value lowers \(\nu_{P,\mathrm{FO}}\) relative to
\(\nu_{P,\mathrm{mom}}\), even when the sampling-variance approximation
is accurate. The sampling mean \(\E(\widehat\nu_P)\) can also fall
below the moment target, with the size of both discrepancies depending
on the population and sample size.

Near independence, the first-order prediction \(\tau^2(P)/n_P\) for
\(\Var\{\Vhat(P)\}\) is small and quadratic sampling terms remain
substantial. The calculation in
Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} explains the observed scale of
the estimated component degrees of freedom. These results
explain why the first-order approximation becomes inadequate near
independence and why substituting sample probabilities requires its own
evaluation, even when the population approximation is accurate.

\subsection{RQ2: False-positive rates under equal MI}

The balanced null study covers common MI values of 0.005, 0.02 and 0.30
nats across the same table sizes, margin regimes and sample sizes.
Expanded Welch lowers rejection throughout, bringing some excessive rates
closer to 5\% while making conservative rates lower still. The effect
on calibration depends on the population as well as sample size. In
uniform \(4\times4\) at \(n=20\), its rate is 4.90\% at common MI
0.02 but 13.11\% at common MI 0.30, so the improvement at the lower MI
does not extend unchanged to stronger association.

The diagnostic comparison in Table~\ref{tab:balanced-null-diagnostics}
shows how different errors in the shared statistic accompany these
outcomes. Underestimated sampling variance tends to enlarge \(|T|\),
and the larger cutoff can offset some excessive rejection. Overestimated
variance without appreciable difference bias instead suppresses rejection,
which Expanded Welch reduces further. When the population tables differ,
residual bias can shift the estimated MI difference away from zero even
under equal MI; increasing the cutoff leaves that displacement unchanged.

The severe different-skew \(16\times16\) failure persists in the
balanced grid. At common MI 0.005 and \(n=1{,}000\), Wald rejects in
36.38\% of repetitions and Expanded Welch in 34.38\%, despite complete
validity. At the other extreme, both tests remain below 4\% in every
\(16\times16\) margin regime at common MI 0.005 and
\(n=50{,}000\). Agreement between methods at large sample sizes
therefore does not by itself establish accurate false-positive rates.

\subsection{RQ3: Power across MI differences}

The power study repeats the full grid at MI-gap magnitudes of 0.005,
0.01 and 0.02 nats, giving 360 settings. At the usual p-value threshold of
0.05, Expanded Welch's rejections remain a subset of Wald's, so its lower
rejection probability also reduces power. The size of that loss depends
on the population, MI gap and sample size, with failed calculations
contributing at the smallest samples. At \(n=10{,}000\), the method
power gap is below 0.32 percentage points throughout the study. Both
methods exceed 99.9\% power at \(n=50{,}000\) for the two larger gaps,
while detection of the 0.005-nat gap ranges from 79.87\% to 96.47\%.

High rejection in a sparse alternative does not necessarily identify the
direction of the MI difference reliably. In the different-skew
\(16\times16\) case at \(n=1{,}000\) and the 0.01-nat gap, positive estimation bias
outweighs the true negative difference, and almost all rejections have
the opposite estimated direction. As that bias diminishes, the sampling
centre first approaches zero, reducing rejection, before moving towards
the negative population difference and giving higher power. Expanded
Welch's cutoff changes the frequency of rejection but leaves this movement
of the shared numerator unchanged. At a fixed sample size in this
biased range, making the population difference more negative can also
move the centre towards zero and reduce rejection. Larger MI gaps become
easier to detect once the bias diminishes sufficiently.

The observation-level sensitivity supplies an MI-specific basis for
choosing the cutoff from the Student \(t\) distribution, while the
simulations distinguish errors in the degrees-of-freedom approximations
from errors in the shared statistic. Expanded Welch brings false-positive
rates closer to 5\% in selected tested settings and reduces detection
probability at the same nominal threshold. Where Wald is already
conservative, the larger cutoff instead lowers both null rejection and
power. These findings support improvements in particular settings while
showing the detection cost and estimation errors that limit the method.

\section{Limitations}

The derivation assumes independent categorical observations, fixed
categories, positive population cell probabilities, and positive
\(V(P)\), \(V(Q)\), \(\tau^2(P)\) and \(\tau^2(Q)\). Paired samples would
require a covariance term
in the difference variance. Growing alphabets and continuous MI estimators
have different approximation errors. Exact independence requires the
second-order argument in Appendix~\ref{sec:independence-boundary}.

The findings concern the tested copying populations. The null study
covers three positive common MI values, and the power study holds
\(I(P)=0.02\) nats while giving \(Q\) greater MI at three gap magnitudes.
The copying construction uses identical row and column margin profiles
within each table and places its added association on matching diagonal
cells in both populations. Other starting MI values, the opposite direction of MI change, other
association constructions, unequal row and column margins within a table
and other locations of dependence remain untested by the power study.

All reported simulations use equal sample sizes, for which the leading
bias corrections cancel from the MI difference. They therefore do not
evaluate the correction's performance under unequal sample sizes, and
residual biases can still differ between populations in the tested
equal-sample settings.

The power comparison uses the proposed tests' nominal thresholds,
so their false-positive rates need not match. A larger rejection
probability under the alternative therefore cannot establish superior
power at a common achieved false-positive rate. The pointwise confidence
intervals quantify simulation uncertainty for each specified population
and sample size.

The online-shopping application illustrates how to report the estimated
MI difference and its direction, standard error, p-value and Expanded Welch
degrees of freedom. Its unknown population MI values prevent a direct
check of false-positive rates. The application therefore demonstrates
interpretation of the test outputs, while the simulation evidence
establishes their behaviour under known populations.

\section{Future work}

A first extension is to derive the second-order mean contribution to the
variance estimator and evaluate a corresponding degrees-of-freedom
correction. Figure~\ref{fig:redesign-a4} shows that the mean approximation's error
changes with MI and differs between the populations, so the correction
should account for the population table. Any estimated correction should then be evaluated through its effect
on the test's false-positive rates and power.

Improved MI bias and standard-error estimators could address the sparse-table
failures. Their effects should be assessed separately by changing one
estimate while holding the others fixed, then measuring null rejection,
power and estimated direction. Near independence, the quadratic sampling
approximation may support a more accurate comparison of MI differences.
Any likelihood-ratio or resampling procedure must preserve the equal-MI
null even when the complete population tables differ.

Reversed power paths, stronger dependence at small sample sizes,
alternative association constructions, unequal margins and unequal sample
sizes would measure how much the false-positive rates and power change
across a wider range of populations. Simulations from plausible application populations could
also examine sensitivity to rare cells and distinct equal-MI tables,
with conclusions tied to the populations chosen for that assessment.

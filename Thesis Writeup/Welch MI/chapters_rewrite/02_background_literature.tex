\chapter{Background and Literature Review}
\label{chap:background}

Testing whether two populations have equal MI requires an estimate from each
sample and a measure of its sampling error. This chapter develops those
inputs and reviews methods for comparing them.

\section{Information-theoretic foundations}

\subsection{Entropy and information content}

For a discrete random variable \(X\) with probability mass function
\(p_X(x)\), the information content of outcome \(x\) is
\(-\log p_X(x)\).
Rare outcomes have larger information content because they are less expected.
Using natural logarithms gives information in nats. The Shannon entropy is the
expected information content,
\begin{equation}
  H(X)=-\sum_x p_X(x)\log p_X(x).
  \label{eq:entropy}
\end{equation}
Entropy summarises the uncertainty of one random variable before its value is
observed \citep{shannon1948}.

For a pair \((X,Y)\), the joint entropy \(H(X,Y)\) is defined from the joint
probabilities in the same way. The conditional entropy
\(
H(X\mid Y)=H(X,Y)-H(Y)
\)
measures the uncertainty remaining in \(X\) after observing \(Y\).

\subsection{Pointwise and average mutual information}

Let \(P=(p_{ij})\) be the joint distribution of categorical variables
\(X\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\) and \(Y\in\{1,\ldots,c\}\). Write
\begin{equation}
  p_{i+}=\sum_{j=1}^{c}p_{ij},
  \qquad
  p_{+j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{ij}
\end{equation}
for the row and column margins. The pointwise mutual information associated
with cell \((i,j)\) is
\begin{equation}
  \pmi_P(i,j)
  =\log\left(\frac{p_{ij}}{p_{i+}p_{+j}}\right).
  \label{eq:background-pmi}
\end{equation}
The denominator is the cell probability under independence with the same
margins. A positive value means that the cell occurs more often than
independence predicts; a negative value means that it occurs less often.

Mutual information is the probability-weighted average of these pointwise
values \citep{shannon1948,bossomaier2016}:
\begin{equation}
  I(P)
  =\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{c}p_{ij}\pmi_P(i,j).
  \label{eq:background-mi}
\end{equation}
Here the summations are nested: for each row \(i\), the inner sum runs over
all columns \(j\). Equivalently, the expression sums once over every cell in
the table and may be written with the shorthand \(\sum_{i,j}\).

Because \(P\) is the joint distribution of \((X,Y)\), we also write
\(I(P)\) as \(I(X;Y)\). Its entropy form is
\begin{equation}
  I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y).
  \label{eq:mi-entropy-form}
\end{equation}
The quantity is nonnegative and equals zero exactly when
\(p_{ij}=p_{i+}p_{+j}\) for every cell, including zero-probability cells
\citep{bossomaier2016}.

Although MI measures the overall departure from independence, it does not
specify the shape of association. Two populations can have the same MI while assigning
probability to different cells, having different margins, or exhibiting
different association patterns. Equality of MI is therefore weaker than
equality of distributions. Our null requires only this one number to agree,
not that the two tables match cell by cell.

The logarithm base determines the reporting unit. This thesis uses natural
logarithms, so MI is measured in nats; dividing by \(\log 2\) converts to bits.
This conversion rescales MI and its standard error together but leaves the
degrees of freedom unchanged, so the test result is identical in either unit.

\section{Estimating discrete mutual information}
\label{sec:background-mi-bias}

Suppose a sample of size \(n\) produces counts \(N_{ij}\). The empirical cell
probabilities are
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{ij}=\frac{N_{ij}}{n}.
\end{equation}
Substitution into Equation~\eqref{eq:background-mi} gives the plug-in estimator
\begin{equation}
  \Ihat(P)
  =\sum_{i,j:\widehat p_{ij}>0}
  \widehat p_{ij}
  \log\left(
  \frac{\widehat p_{ij}}
       {\widehat p_{i+}\widehat p_{+j}}
  \right).
  \label{eq:plugin-mi-background}
\end{equation}
The zero-cell convention follows from
\(\lim_{x\downarrow0}x\log x=0\). Empty cells contribute no direct term, but
they still affect the empirical margins and the finite-sample behaviour of the
estimator.

Under multinomial sampling, the cell counts vary between samples but must
sum to \(n\). An extra observation in one cell therefore leaves fewer
observations for the others. For a fixed set of categories, the plug-in
estimate approaches population MI as the sample grows, but its small-sample
behaviour depends on the complete probability table. In particular, rare
cells can change this behaviour even when the categories and sample size are
unchanged
\citep{paninski2003}.

For a fixed positive \(r\times c\) support, the plug-in estimator has a
positive leading bias as the sample size grows:
\begin{equation}
  \Bias\{\Ihat(P)\}
  =\frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2n}+O(n^{-2}).
  \label{eq:mi-leading-bias}
\end{equation}
The leading term grows with table size and shrinks as the sample grows. It
does not describe every small or sparse sample. The result is closely related
to Miller's entropy correction \citep{miller1955}, and the same histogram-MI
term appears in \citet{moddemeijer1989}. Sparse sampling can produce further
error beyond this leading approximation \citep{paninski2003}.

Bias matters directly when comparing two populations. If
\(I(P)=I(Q)\) but \(n_P\ne n_Q\), the uncorrected difference has leading bias
\begin{equation}
  \frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2}
  \left(\frac{1}{n_P}-\frac{1}{n_Q}\right).
  \label{eq:two-sample-mi-bias}
\end{equation}
For a \(2\times2\) table with \(n_P=50\) and \(n_Q=100\), Equation
\eqref{eq:two-sample-mi-bias} gives \(0.005\) nats despite equal population MIs. Chapter
\ref{chap:baselines} corrects this sample-size effect by subtracting
\((r-1)(c-1)/(2n)\) from each estimate.

\section{Sampling variance of plug-in MI}
\label{sec:first-order-mi-background}

Comparing two MI estimates requires knowing how much each varies across
repeated samples. We derive the large-sample approximation
\begin{equation}
  \Var\{\Ihat(P)\}\approx\frac{V(P)}{n}.
  \label{eq:first-order-mi-var-background}
\end{equation}
Here \(V(P)\), defined below, measures how much the contribution of one
observation varies across the population. The derivation assumes that the
population cell probabilities are positive, so the logarithms and their
derivatives are well defined. This first-order variance is established for
histogram and discrete MI \citep{moddemeijer1989,brillinger2004}.

The argument proceeds in three steps. First, sampling changes the empirical
cell probabilities. Second, a first-order Taylor expansion translates those
probability errors into an MI error. Third, that error can be written as a
sample average, whose variance is straightforward to calculate.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Step 1: From probability errors to MI error.}

Using the entropy identity in Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-entropy-form}, MI can be
written directly in terms of the joint and marginal probabilities:
\begin{equation}
  I(P)
  =
  \sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\log p_{ij}
  -\sum_i p_{i+}\log p_{i+}
  -\sum_j p_{+j}\log p_{+j}.
  \label{eq:mi-probability-sums}
\end{equation}
Each sum contains the same function, \(f(u)=u\log u\). Expanding this
function lets us translate probability errors into changes in MI. The
derivative needed for its first-order Taylor
expansion follows directly from the product rule:
\begin{equation}
  f'(u)
  =\frac{d}{du}\{u\log u\}
  =\log u+u\frac{1}{u}
  =\log u+1.
  \label{eq:ulog-derivative-background}
\end{equation}
The general first-order Taylor formula is
\begin{equation}
  f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a),
  \label{eq:general-first-order-taylor-background}
\end{equation}
where \(a\) is the value around which the function is expanded and \(x-a\)
is the distance from that value. Here the new value is the estimated
probability \(x=\widehat u\), the expansion point is the population probability
\(a=u\), and \(f(t)=t\log t\). Therefore, \(x-a=\widehat u-u\), which is the
sampling error in the estimated probability. Substituting these quantities
into the general formula gives
\begin{equation}
  \widehat u\log\widehat u
  \approx
  \underbrace{u\log u}_{\text{population value}}
  +
  \underbrace{(\log u+1)(\widehat u-u)}_{\text{first-order change}}.
  \label{eq:first-order-taylor-background}
\end{equation}
Subtracting the population value from both sides makes the change explicit:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat u\log\widehat u-u\log u
  \approx
  (\log u+1)(\widehat u-u).
  \label{eq:ulog-change-background}
\end{equation}

Applying this expansion to the three sums in
Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-probability-sums} gives
\begin{align}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  &\approx
  \sum_{i,j}(\log p_{ij}+1)(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
  \notag\\
  &\quad
  -\sum_i(\log p_{i+}+1)(\widehat p_{i+}-p_{i+})
  \notag\\
  &\quad
  -\sum_j(\log p_{+j}+1)(\widehat p_{+j}-p_{+j}).
  \label{eq:mi-first-order-three-sums}
\end{align}
The margin errors are sums of the corresponding cell errors:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{i+}-p_{i+}
  =\sum_j(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}),
  \qquad
  \widehat p_{+j}-p_{+j}
  =\sum_i(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}).
  \label{eq:margin-errors-from-cells}
\end{equation}
For example, the row-margin sum can now be written as
\begin{align}
  \sum_i(\log p_{i+}+1)(\widehat p_{i+}-p_{i+})
  &=\sum_i\left[
    (\log p_{i+}+1)
    \left\{\sum_j(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})\right\}
    \right]
  \notag\\
  &=\sum_{i,j}(\log p_{i+}+1)(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}).
\end{align}
The inner sum over \(j\) is the probability error for a fixed row \(i\). It
is multiplied by that row's coefficient \(\log p_{i+}+1\); the outer sum then
adds these products over all rows. The column-margin sum can be rearranged in
the same way.

After this rearrangement, the same cell error \(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}\)
appears in the joint term, its
row-margin term and its column-margin term. Its combined coefficient is
\begin{align}
  &(\log p_{ij}+1)
  -(\log p_{i+}+1)
  -(\log p_{+j}+1)
  \notag\\
  &\qquad
  =\log\left(\frac{p_{ij}}{p_{i+}p_{+j}}\right)-1
  \notag\\
  &\qquad
  =\pmi_P(i,j)-1.
  \label{eq:mi-error-cell-coefficient}
\end{align}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  \approx
  \sum_{i,j}\{\pmi_P(i,j)-1\}(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}).
  \label{eq:mi-error-before-constant-cancellation}
\end{equation}
The empirical and population cell probabilities both sum to one, so
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i,j}(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
  =\sum_{i,j}\widehat p_{ij}-\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}
  =1-1=0.
  \label{eq:cell-errors-sum-zero}
\end{equation}
The constant \(-1\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-cell-coefficient} therefore
contributes zero. Explicitly,
\begin{align}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  &\approx
  \sum_{i,j}\{\pmi_P(i,j)-1\}(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
  \notag\\
  &=\sum_{i,j}\pmi_P(i,j)(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
    -\sum_{i,j}(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
  \notag\\
  &=\sum_{i,j}
  \pmi_P(i,j)(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}).
  \label{eq:mi-error-from-cell-errors}
\end{align}
The complicated changes in the joint table and its two margins have therefore
reduced to a weighted sum of cell-probability errors. The weight for a cell is
its population pointwise MI.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Step 2: From cell errors to observation-level errors.}

Let \(Z_a=(X_a,Y_a)\) be observation \(a\). An empirical cell probability is
the proportion of observations in that cell, so
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}
  =
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
  \left\{\mathbf 1(Z_a=(i,j))-p_{ij}\right\}.
  \label{eq:cell-error-as-average}
\end{equation}
Substituting this expression into Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-from-cell-errors}
first gives
\begin{equation}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  \approx
  \sum_{i,j}\pmi_P(i,j)
  \left[
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
    \{\mathbf 1(Z_a=(i,j))-p_{ij}\}
  \right].
  \label{eq:mi-error-double-sum-background}
\end{equation}
The finite sums can be exchanged, so the contribution from observation \(a\)
is gathered inside one pair of brackets:
\begin{align}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  &\approx
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
  \left[
    \sum_{i,j}\pmi_P(i,j)\mathbf 1(Z_a=(i,j))
    -\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\pmi_P(i,j)
  \right]
  \notag\\
  &=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
  \left\{\pmi_P(Z_a)-I(P)\right\}.
  \label{eq:mi-error-as-average-background}
\end{align}
The first sum inside the brackets selects the single cell containing
\(Z_a\), giving \(\pmi_P(Z_a)\). The second is the probability-weighted
average of all pointwise-MI values, which is \(I(P)\).

To first order, Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-as-average-background} expresses
the MI error as an average of \(n\) observation-level contributions.
The contribution of an observation in cell \((i,j)\) is simply that cell's
pointwise MI minus the population average, \(\pmi_P(i,j)-I(P)\). Its mean is
zero because
\begin{align}
  \E_P\{\pmi_P(X,Y)-I(P)\}
  &=\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\{\pmi_P(i,j)-I(P)\}
  \notag\\
  &=\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\pmi_P(i,j)}_{I(P)}
    -I(P)\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}}_{1}
  =0.
  \label{eq:mi-if-mean-zero-background}
\end{align}
The centred function \(\pmi_P(i,j)-I(P)\) is called the influence function
of MI\@. Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-as-average-background} uses it to
approximate the sampling error \citep{hampel1974,vanderVaart1998,kandasamy2015}.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Step 3: Calculate the sampling variance.}

Define the population variance of these centred contributions by
\begin{equation}
  V(P)
  =\Var_P\{\pmi_P(X,Y)-I(P)\}
  =\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\{\pmi_P(i,j)-I(P)\}^2.
  \label{eq:mi-variance-background}
\end{equation}
Under independent sampling, the \(n\) terms in
Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-as-average-background} are independent and each
has variance \(V(P)\). The covariance between any two different observations
is therefore zero. Hence
\begin{align}
  \Var\{\Ihat(P)\}
  &\approx
  \Var\left\{
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
    \{\pmi_P(Z_a)-I(P)\}
  \right\}
  \notag\\
  &=\frac{1}{n^2}
    \sum_{a=1}^{n}\Var\{\pmi_P(Z_a)-I(P)\}
  \notag\\
  &=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{a=1}^{n}V(P)
  =\frac{nV(P)}{n^2}
  =\frac{V(P)}{n}.
  \label{eq:mi-variance-derived-background}
\end{align}
Averaging \(n\) independent contributions therefore divides their variance
by \(n\). For dependent observations, covariance terms must also be included
in Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-variance-derived-background}, as studied by
\citet{moddemeijer1999}. The present experiment uses independent observations.

When \(V(P)>0\), the central limit theorem also gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Ihat(P)-I(P)}{\sqrt{V(P)/n}}
  \xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).
  \label{eq:mi-clt-background}
\end{equation}
This gives a large-sample normal approximation for a fixed table with
positive population cell probabilities. The \(1/n\) leading bias from
Section~\ref{sec:background-mi-bias} decreases faster than the
\(1/\sqrt n\) sampling error, so it does not change this limiting
distribution.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{The independence boundary.}
\nopagebreak[4]

At exact independence, \(p_{ij}=p_{i+}p_{+j}\), so
\(\pmi_P(i,j)=\log 1=0\) in every cell. Hence \(I(P)=0\), \(V(P)=0\), and the
first-order average in Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-as-average-background}
vanishes.
A finite sample can still have positive MI. Its leading variation comes from
the squared cell-probability errors in a second-order Taylor expansion, which
produces the usual chi-squared limiting distribution
\citep{brillinger2004,marinescu2025}. Testing independence therefore uses a
chi-squared distribution rather than the normal approximation for comparing
positive MI values.

\section{Approaches to Testing MI Differences}
\label{sec:welch-background}

This section reviews methods for estimating uncertainty in MI and testing
differences between two MI values. The question in this thesis is whether two
populations have the same MI, \(I(P)=I(Q)\). Their cell probabilities may
differ even when their MI values are equal.

\thesisminiheading{Normal Wald inference.}

A Wald test estimates a parameter difference, divides it by an estimated
standard error, and compares the result with a standard normal distribution
\citep{wald1943,vanderVaart1998}.
For two independent MI estimates, the two first-order variance contributions
add:
\begin{equation}
  \Var\{\Ihat(P)-\Ihat(Q)\}
  \approx \frac{V(P)}{n_P}+\frac{V(Q)}{n_Q}.
  \label{eq:background-two-sample-variance}
\end{equation}
Each term is the sampling variance of one MI estimate. Independence of the
two samples makes their covariance zero. Replacing the population variances
by estimates gives the standardisation
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Ihat(P)-\Ihat(Q)}
       {\sqrt{\Vhat(P)/n_P+\Vhat(Q)/n_Q}}
  \xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)
  \qquad\text{under }I(P)=I(Q).
  \label{eq:background-wald-standardisation}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Vhat(P)\) and \(\Vhat(Q)\) estimate the pointwise-MI variances
\(V(P)\) and \(V(Q)\). This limit assumes the regular positive-support
conditions of Section~\ref{sec:first-order-mi-background} and increasing
sample sizes. Chapter~\ref{chap:baselines} applies the leading bias correction
to the numerator and specifies the full Normal Wald test.
With smaller samples, the MI difference may not follow the normal
approximation closely, and the estimated standard error can vary between
samples. The Wald test still uses a normal distribution; it makes no separate
small-sample adjustment for that standard-error variability.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{The Welch--Satterthwaite principle.}

Welch's test compares two means when their population variances may differ
\citep{welch1947}. For two independent samples with means
\(\bar X_P,\bar X_Q\) and variances \(s_P^2,s_Q^2\), the statistic for
testing equal population means and its effective degrees of freedom are
\begin{align}
  T_{\mathrm{Welch}}
  &=\frac{\bar X_P-\bar X_Q}
          {\sqrt{s_P^2/n_P+s_Q^2/n_Q}},\\
  \nu
  &=\frac{\left(s_P^2/n_P+s_Q^2/n_Q\right)^2}
          {\dfrac{(s_P^2/n_P)^2}{n_P-1}
           +\dfrac{(s_Q^2/n_Q)^2}{n_Q-1}}.
  \label{eq:background-ordinary-welch}
\end{align}

The denominator of the statistic contains one estimated variance contribution
from each sample. Under normal sampling, their component degrees of freedom
are \(n_P-1\) and \(n_Q-1\). If \(s_P^2/n_P\) dominates, the formula makes the
combined \(\nu\) close to \(n_P-1\); when both contributions matter, both
component values affect \(\nu\). Satterthwaite obtains this form by matching
the mean and variance of their sum to a scaled chi-squared variable
\citep{satterthwaite1946}.

Welch compares the statistic with a Student \(t\) distribution using the calculated
\(\nu\). At the same significance level, this requires a larger absolute
statistic to reject the null than a standard normal distribution does.
The difference between the rejection thresholds from the Student \(t\)
and standard normal distributions becomes
smaller as \(\nu\) increases.
For MI, the estimated variance contributions are \(\Vhat(P)/n_P\) and
\(\Vhat(Q)/n_Q\). Their component degrees of freedom need their own
calculation because \(\Vhat(P)\) and \(\Vhat(Q)\) are nonlinear functions of
the probability tables. Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} derives these
MI-specific inputs to the same moment-matching structure.

The same principle has been used in regression to account for
uncertainty in estimated variances
\citep{panwall2002,bell2002,kauermann2001,fay2001,imbens2016}.
For example, \citet{panwall2002} calculate approximate degrees of freedom
by examining how a regression variance estimate varies between samples.
Expanded Welch applies this approach to the estimated MI variance.
These regression precedents address variance estimates that are too small
or confidence intervals that cover too rarely
\citep{bell2002,kauermann2001}. A larger cutoff from the Student \(t\)
distribution can widen the intervals and reduce excessive rejection in such
settings. The MI comparison in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism-results} has the
opposite problem: the average squared plug-in standard error exceeds the
measured variance of the estimated difference. The larger cutoff leaves
that variance error unchanged and makes rejection less likely still.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Prior MI inference and related comparisons.}

\citet{roulston1999} derives entropy and MI error estimates
using error propagation and examines them numerically. These calculations
describe the uncertainty of an individual estimate, a necessary input to a
comparison of two estimates.
A Bayesian approach describes uncertainty about population MI given an
observed table. \citet{hutter2002} and \citet{hutterzaffalon2005} model the
unknown cell probabilities to calculate the variance and shape of the
resulting MI distribution. Our repeated-sampling comparison instead asks
how often each test rejects when new samples are drawn from fixed
populations.

Hutcheson's test is a direct information-theoretic Welch predecessor. It
compares two Shannon diversity estimates with an estimated variance and a
Student \(t\) distribution, using
\begin{equation}
  \nu_H=
  \frac{(s_{H,P}^2+s_{H,Q}^2)^2}
       {(s_{H,P}^2)^2/n_P+(s_{H,Q}^2)^2/n_Q}
  \label{eq:hutcheson-df}
\end{equation}
as its effective degrees of freedom \citep{hutcheson1970}. Here each of
\(s_{H,P}^2\) and \(s_{H,Q}^2\) is the estimated sampling variance of the
corresponding entropy estimator \(\widehat H\), and each variance component
is assigned its sample size, not \(n_i-1\).
Hutcheson's rule assigns degrees of freedom from sample sizes. Expanded
Welch instead calculates how the estimated MI variance changes with the
table, including the effects of a cell change on its row and column totals.

In a segregation application, \citet{mora2009} develop inference for an MI index and discuss
pairwise comparisons across groups and periods; the later peer-reviewed account
places that MI index within the wider segregation literature \citep{mora2011}.
By contrast, \citet{guha2014} test whether two continuous distributions are identical
using MI between pooled observations and their sample labels.
For discrete data, \citet{drake2014} develop an MI-based \(k\)-sample test
for equality of distributions. These tests compare complete distributions,
not the MI of two variables within each population.

\medskip
\thesisminiheading{Resampling alternatives.}

A bootstrap would draw many new samples from the observed tables to estimate
how much the test statistic varies. This requires repeating the calculation
many times \citep{efron1979}. A simple permutation test instead combines the
two samples and shuffles their group labels. That shuffle is justified when
the populations have the same complete distribution, \(P=Q\). Our hypothesis
requires only \(I(P)=I(Q)\): the tables can differ while their MI values are
equal. Shuffling labels could therefore test a stronger claim than this thesis
asks about \citep{chung2013}. Specialised permutation methods allow distinct
populations in some parameter comparisons \citep{janssen1997,chung2013};
their validity for equal MI would need to be established.

\section{Previous work and this thesis's contribution}

Existing work provides the leading bias and sampling variance of MI
\citep{miller1955,moddemeijer1989,brillinger2004,paninski2003}.
Hutcheson's entropy comparison assigns degrees of freedom from sample sizes
\citep{hutcheson1970}, while \citet{panwall2002} use the variability of a
regression variance estimate to choose degrees of freedom. Applying the
latter approach to MI requires calculating how its estimated variance
changes with the observed table.

A new observation changes the empirical cell probabilities, row and column
totals, and pointwise MI values. The remaining calculation is how these
changes affect the estimated MI variance. This thesis uses that calculation
to choose the degrees of freedom for a Student \(t\) distribution, then
evaluates whether it improves false positives and detection.
Both tests start with the same estimated MI difference and standard error.
Chapter~\ref{chap:baselines} defines this shared statistic before
Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} derives the additional variance calculation.

\chapter{Background and Literature Review}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter develops the concepts needed to compare MI between two
categorical populations. It begins with entropy, pointwise MI, and population
MI\@. It then reviews plug-in estimation, first-order inference, the
Welch--Satterthwaite approximation, and prior comparisons of information
quantities. The final section identifies the gap addressed by the expanded
method.

\section{Information-theoretic foundations}

\subsection{Entropy and information content}

For a discrete random variable \(X\) with probability mass function
\(p_X(x)\), the information content of outcome \(x\) is
\(-\log p_X(x)\).
Rare outcomes have larger information content because they are less expected.
Using natural logarithms gives information in nats. The Shannon entropy is the
expected information content,
\begin{equation}
  H(X)=-\sum_x p_X(x)\log p_X(x).
  \label{eq:entropy}
\end{equation}
Entropy summarises the uncertainty of one random variable before its value is
observed \citep{shannon1948}.

For a pair \((X,Y)\), the joint entropy \(H(X,Y)\) is defined from the joint
probabilities in the same way. The conditional entropy
\(
H(X\mid Y)=H(X,Y)-H(Y)
\)
measures the uncertainty remaining in \(X\) after observing \(Y\).

\subsection{Pointwise and average mutual information}

Let \(P=(p_{ij})\) be the joint distribution of categorical variables
\(X\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\) and \(Y\in\{1,\ldots,c\}\). Write
\begin{equation}
  p_{i+}=\sum_{j=1}^{c}p_{ij},
  \qquad
  p_{+j}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}p_{ij}
\end{equation}
for the row and column margins. The pointwise mutual information associated
with cell \((i,j)\) is
\begin{equation}
  \pmi_P(i,j)
  =\log\left(\frac{p_{ij}}{p_{i+}p_{+j}}\right).
  \label{eq:background-pmi}
\end{equation}
The denominator is the cell probability under independence with the same
margins. A positive value means that the cell occurs more often than
independence predicts; a negative value means that it occurs less often.

Mutual information is the probability-weighted average of these pointwise
values \citep{shannon1948,bossomaier2016}:
\begin{equation}
  I(P)
  =\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{c}p_{ij}\pmi_P(i,j).
  \label{eq:background-mi}
\end{equation}
Here the summations are nested: for each row \(i\), the inner sum runs over
all columns \(j\). Equivalently, the expression sums once over every cell in
the table and may be written with the shorthand \(\sum_{i,j}\).
Because \(P\) is the joint distribution of \((X,Y)\), the same quantity is
conventionally written as \(I(X;Y)\). Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
  I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y).
  \label{eq:mi-entropy-form}
\end{equation}
The quantity is nonnegative and equals zero exactly when
\(p_{ij}=p_{i+}p_{+j}\) for every cell, including zero-probability cells.

Equation~\eqref{eq:background-mi} is also the Kullback--Leibler divergence
between the joint distribution and the product of its margins. MI therefore
measures the overall departure from independence, not one particular
shape of association.

This scalar summary deliberately discards much of the information in the
joint distribution. Two populations can have the same MI while assigning
probability to different cells, having different margins, or exhibiting
different association patterns. Equality of MI is therefore weaker than
equality of distributions. This distinction is central to the present test:
the null fixes one scalar functional while leaving the remaining features of
each population unrestricted.

The logarithm base determines the reporting unit. This thesis uses natural
logarithms, so MI is measured in nats; dividing by \(\log 2\) converts to bits.
This conversion rescales MI and its standard error together but leaves the
degrees of freedom unchanged, so the test result is identical in either unit.

\section{Estimating discrete mutual information}
\label{sec:background-mi-bias}

Suppose a sample of size \(n\) produces counts \(N_{ij}\). The empirical cell
probabilities are
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{ij}=\frac{N_{ij}}{n}.
\end{equation}
Substitution into Equation~\eqref{eq:background-mi} gives the plug-in estimator
\begin{equation}
  \Ihat(P)
  =\sum_{i,j:\widehat p_{ij}>0}
  \widehat p_{ij}
  \log\left(
  \frac{\widehat p_{ij}}
       {\widehat p_{i+}\widehat p_{+j}}
  \right).
  \label{eq:plugin-mi-background}
\end{equation}
The zero-cell convention follows from
\(\lim_{x\downarrow0}x\log x=0\). Empty cells contribute no direct term, but
they still affect the empirical margins and the finite-sample behaviour of the
estimator.

Under multinomial sampling, the cell counts are random but constrained to sum
to \(n\), so they are dependent. The plug-in estimator remains consistent for
a fixed alphabet, but its finite-sample behaviour depends on the complete
probability table. In particular, rare cells can change the sampling
behaviour even when the alphabet and sample sizes are unchanged.

The plug-in estimator is upward biased: random variation in the cell counts
tends to make the empirical table appear more dependent than the population
table. For a fixed positive \(r\times c\) support, the leading bias is
\begin{equation}
  \Bias\{\Ihat(P)\}
  =\frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2n}+O(n^{-2}).
  \label{eq:mi-leading-bias}
\end{equation}
The factor \((r-1)(c-1)\) increases with table size, while division by \(n\)
shows that the bias decreases as the sample grows. The bias is therefore
largest for larger tables with small samples. The result is closely related
to Miller's entropy correction \citep{miller1955}, and the same histogram-MI
term appears in \citet{moddemeijer1989}. Sparse sampling can produce further
error beyond this leading approximation \citep{paninski2003}.

Bias matters directly when comparing two populations. If
\(I(P)=I(Q)\) but \(n_P\ne n_Q\), the uncorrected difference has leading bias
\begin{equation}
  \frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2}
  \left(\frac{1}{n_P}-\frac{1}{n_Q}\right).
  \label{eq:two-sample-mi-bias}
\end{equation}
For a \(2\times2\) table with \(n_P=50\) and \(n_Q=100\), Equation
\eqref{eq:two-sample-mi-bias} gives \(0.005\) nats despite equal population MIs. Chapter
\ref{chap:baselines} corrects this sample-size effect by subtracting
\((r-1)(c-1)/(2n)\) from each estimate.

\section{Sampling variance of plug-in MI}
\label{sec:first-order-mi-background}

To compare two estimated MI values, it is necessary to know how much each
estimate would vary across repeated samples. The goal of this section is to
derive the large-sample approximation
\begin{equation}
  \Var\{\Ihat(P)\}\approx\frac{V(P)}{n}.
  \label{eq:first-order-mi-var-background}
\end{equation}
Here \(V(P)\), defined below, measures how much the contribution of one
observation varies across the population. The derivation assumes that the
population cell probabilities are positive, so the logarithms and their
derivatives are well defined. This first-order variance is established for
histogram and discrete MI \citep{moddemeijer1989,brillinger2004}.
The argument proceeds in three steps. First, sampling changes the empirical
cell probabilities. Second, a first-order Taylor expansion translates those
probability errors into an MI error. Third, that error can be written as a
sample average, whose variance is straightforward to calculate.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 1: From probability errors to MI error.}

Using the entropy identity in Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-entropy-form}, MI can be
written directly in terms of the joint and marginal probabilities:
\begin{equation}
  I(P)
  =
  \sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\log p_{ij}
  -\sum_i p_{i+}\log p_{i+}
  -\sum_j p_{+j}\log p_{+j}.
  \label{eq:mi-probability-sums}
\end{equation}
Let \(f(u)=u\log u\). The derivative needed for its first-order Taylor
expansion follows directly from the product rule:
\begin{equation}
  f'(u)
  =\frac{d}{du}\{u\log u\}
  =\log u+u\frac{1}{u}
  =\log u+1.
  \label{eq:ulog-derivative-background}
\end{equation}
The general first-order Taylor formula is
\begin{equation}
  f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a),
  \label{eq:general-first-order-taylor-background}
\end{equation}
where \(a\) is the value around which the function is expanded and \(x-a\)
is the distance from that value. Here the new value is the estimated
probability \(x=\widehat u\), the expansion point is the population probability
\(a=u\), and \(f(t)=t\log t\). Therefore, \(x-a=\widehat u-u\), which is the
sampling error in the estimated probability. Substituting these quantities
into the general formula gives
\begin{equation}
  \widehat u\log\widehat u
  \approx
  \underbrace{u\log u}_{\text{population value}}
  +
  \underbrace{(\log u+1)(\widehat u-u)}_{\text{first-order change}}.
  \label{eq:first-order-taylor-background}
\end{equation}
Subtracting the population value from both sides makes the change explicit:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat u\log\widehat u-u\log u
  \approx
  (\log u+1)(\widehat u-u).
  \label{eq:ulog-change-background}
\end{equation}

Applying this expansion to the three sums in
Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-probability-sums} gives
\begin{align}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  &\approx
  \sum_{i,j}(\log p_{ij}+1)(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
  \notag\\
  &\quad
  -\sum_i(\log p_{i+}+1)(\widehat p_{i+}-p_{i+})
  \notag\\
  &\quad
  -\sum_j(\log p_{+j}+1)(\widehat p_{+j}-p_{+j}).
  \label{eq:mi-first-order-three-sums}
\end{align}
The margin errors are sums of the corresponding cell errors:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{i+}-p_{i+}
  =\sum_j(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}),
  \qquad
  \widehat p_{+j}-p_{+j}
  =\sum_i(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}).
  \label{eq:margin-errors-from-cells}
\end{equation}
For example, the row-margin sum can now be written as
\begin{align}
  \sum_i(\log p_{i+}+1)(\widehat p_{i+}-p_{i+})
  &=\sum_i\left[
    (\log p_{i+}+1)
    \left\{\sum_j(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})\right\}
    \right]
  \notag\\
  &=\sum_{i,j}(\log p_{i+}+1)(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}).
\end{align}
The inner sum over \(j\) is the probability error for a fixed row \(i\). It
is multiplied by that row's coefficient \(\log p_{i+}+1\); the outer sum then
adds these products over all rows. The column-margin sum can be rearranged in
the same way. Consequently,
the same cell error \(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}\) appears in the joint term, its
row-margin term and its column-margin term. Its combined coefficient is
\begin{align}
  &(\log p_{ij}+1)
  -(\log p_{i+}+1)
  -(\log p_{+j}+1)
  \notag\\
  &\qquad
  =\log\left(\frac{p_{ij}}{p_{i+}p_{+j}}\right)-1
  \notag\\
  &\qquad
  =\pmi_P(i,j)-1.
  \label{eq:mi-error-cell-coefficient}
\end{align}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  \approx
  \sum_{i,j}\{\pmi_P(i,j)-1\}(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}).
  \label{eq:mi-error-before-constant-cancellation}
\end{equation}
The empirical and population cell probabilities both sum to one, so
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i,j}(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
  =\sum_{i,j}\widehat p_{ij}-\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}
  =1-1=0.
  \label{eq:cell-errors-sum-zero}
\end{equation}
The constant \(-1\) in Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-cell-coefficient} therefore
contributes zero. Explicitly,
\begin{align}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  &\approx
  \sum_{i,j}\{\pmi_P(i,j)-1\}(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
  \notag\\
  &=\sum_{i,j}\pmi_P(i,j)(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
    -\sum_{i,j}(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij})
  \notag\\
  &=\sum_{i,j}
  \pmi_P(i,j)(\widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}).
  \label{eq:mi-error-from-cell-errors}
\end{align}
The complicated changes in the joint table and its two margins have therefore
reduced to a weighted sum of cell-probability errors. The weight for a cell is
its population pointwise MI.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 2: From cell errors to observation-level errors.}

Let \(Z_a=(X_a,Y_a)\) be observation \(a\). An empirical cell probability is
the proportion of observations in that cell, so
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p_{ij}-p_{ij}
  =
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
  \left\{\mathbf 1(Z_a=(i,j))-p_{ij}\right\}.
  \label{eq:cell-error-as-average}
\end{equation}
Substituting this expression into Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-from-cell-errors}
first gives
\begin{equation}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  \approx
  \sum_{i,j}\pmi_P(i,j)
  \left[
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
    \{\mathbf 1(Z_a=(i,j))-p_{ij}\}
  \right].
  \label{eq:mi-error-double-sum-background}
\end{equation}
The finite sums can be exchanged, so the contribution from observation \(a\)
is gathered inside one pair of brackets:
\begin{align}
  \Ihat(P)-I(P)
  &\approx
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
  \left[
    \sum_{i,j}\pmi_P(i,j)\mathbf 1(Z_a=(i,j))
    -\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\pmi_P(i,j)
  \right]
  \notag\\
  &=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
  \left\{\pmi_P(Z_a)-I(P)\right\}.
  \label{eq:mi-error-as-average-background}
\end{align}
The first sum inside the brackets selects the single cell containing
\(Z_a\), giving \(\pmi_P(Z_a)\). The second is the probability-weighted
average of all pointwise-MI values, which is \(I(P)\).
This equation is the key step. To first order, the error in the plug-in MI
estimate behaves like the average of \(n\) observation-level contributions.
The contribution of an observation in cell \((i,j)\) is simply that cell's
pointwise MI minus the population average, \(\pmi_P(i,j)-I(P)\). Its mean is
zero because
\begin{align}
  \E_P\{\pmi_P(X,Y)-I(P)\}
  &=\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\{\pmi_P(i,j)-I(P)\}
  \notag\\
  &=\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\pmi_P(i,j)}_{I(P)}
    -I(P)\underbrace{\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}}_{1}
  =0.
  \label{eq:mi-if-mean-zero-background}
\end{align}
In formal asymptotic terminology, the centred function
\(\pmi_P(i,j)-I(P)\) is the influence function of MI; Equation
\eqref{eq:mi-error-as-average-background} is its first-order linear
representation \citep{hampel1974,vanderVaart1998,kandasamy2015}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 3: Calculate the sampling variance.}

Define the population variance of these centred contributions by
\begin{equation}
  V(P)
  =\Var_P\{\pmi_P(X,Y)-I(P)\}
  =\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}\{\pmi_P(i,j)-I(P)\}^2.
  \label{eq:mi-variance-background}
\end{equation}
Under independent sampling, the \(n\) terms in
Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-as-average-background} are independent and each
has variance \(V(P)\). The covariance between any two different observations
is therefore zero. Hence
\begin{align}
  \Var\{\Ihat(P)\}
  &\approx
  \Var\left\{
    \frac{1}{n}\sum_{a=1}^{n}
    \{\pmi_P(Z_a)-I(P)\}
  \right\}
  \notag\\
  &=\frac{1}{n^2}
    \sum_{a=1}^{n}\Var\{\pmi_P(Z_a)-I(P)\}
  \notag\\
  &=\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{a=1}^{n}V(P)
  =\frac{nV(P)}{n^2}
  =\frac{V(P)}{n}.
  \label{eq:mi-variance-derived-background}
\end{align}
This proves the approximation stated in
Equation~\eqref{eq:first-order-mi-var-background}. It is the familiar result
that the variance of an average of \(n\) independent contributions is the
variance of one contribution divided by \(n\).

When \(V(P)>0\), the central limit theorem also gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Ihat(P)-I(P)}{\sqrt{V(P)/n}}
  \xrightarrow{d}N(0,1).
  \label{eq:mi-clt-background}
\end{equation}
This does not claim that every finite-sample MI estimate is normally
distributed. It is a local, large-sample approximation for a fixed table with
positive population cell probabilities. The \(1/n\) leading bias from
Section~\ref{sec:background-mi-bias} decreases faster than the
\(1/\sqrt n\) sampling error, so it does not change this limiting
distribution.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The independence boundary.}

At exact independence, \(p_{ij}=p_{i+}p_{+j}\), so
\(\pmi_P(i,j)=\log 1=0\) in every cell. Hence \(I(P)=0\), \(V(P)=0\), and the
first-order average in Equation~\eqref{eq:mi-error-as-average-background}
vanishes.

A finite sample can still have positive MI. Its leading variation comes from
the squared cell-probability errors in a second-order Taylor expansion, which
produces the usual chi-squared limit. The normal limit for comparing positive
MI values and the chi-squared limit for testing independence therefore apply
in different settings.

\section{Related inferential methods}
\label{sec:welch-background}

\noindent\textbf{Normal Wald inference.}

A Wald test estimates a parameter difference, divides it by an estimated
standard error, and compares the result with its asymptotic normal reference.
For two independent MI estimates, the two first-order variance contributions
add:
\begin{equation}
  \Var\{\Ihat(P)-\Ihat(Q)\}
  \approx \frac{V(P)}{n_P}+\frac{V(Q)}{n_Q}.
  \label{eq:background-two-sample-variance}
\end{equation}
Each term is the sampling variance of one MI estimate. Independence of the
two samples makes their covariance zero. Replacing the population variances
by estimates gives the standardisation
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Ihat(P)-\Ihat(Q)}
       {\sqrt{\Vhat(P)/n_P+\Vhat(Q)/n_Q}}
  \xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)
  \qquad\text{under }I(P)=I(Q).
  \label{eq:background-wald-standardisation}
\end{equation}
Here \(\Vhat(P)\) and \(\Vhat(Q)\) estimate the pointwise-MI variances
\(V(P)\) and \(V(Q)\). This limit assumes the regular positive-support
conditions of Section~\ref{sec:first-order-mi-background} and increasing
sample sizes. Chapter~\ref{chap:baselines} applies the leading bias correction
to the numerator and specifies the full Normal Wald test.

Wald inference is asymptotically valid under regularity conditions, but its
finite-sample calibration depends on both the quality of the linear expansion
and the stability of the estimated standard error. A normal reference does not
separately represent the uncertainty of the two estimated variance
contributions.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The Welch--Satterthwaite principle.}

Welch's test compares two means when their population variances may differ
\citep{welch1947}. For two independent samples with means
\(\bar X_P,\bar X_Q\) and variances \(s_P^2,s_Q^2\), the statistic for
testing equal population means and its effective degrees of freedom are
\begin{align}
  T_{\mathrm{Welch}}
  &=\frac{\bar X_P-\bar X_Q}
          {\sqrt{s_P^2/n_P+s_Q^2/n_Q}},\\
  \nu
  &=\frac{\left(s_P^2/n_P+s_Q^2/n_Q\right)^2}
          {\dfrac{(s_P^2/n_P)^2}{n_P-1}
           +\dfrac{(s_Q^2/n_Q)^2}{n_Q-1}}.
  \label{eq:background-ordinary-welch}
\end{align}
The denominator of the statistic contains two independently estimated
variance contributions. The degrees-of-freedom formula weights their
uncertainty: under normal sampling, each sample variance has component
degrees of freedom \(n_P-1\) or \(n_Q-1\). Satterthwaite obtains the combined value by
representing the sum as a scaled chi-squared variable with matching mean
and variance \citep{satterthwaite1946}.

Welch evaluates the statistic against a Student distribution with \(\nu\)
degrees of freedom. The connection to the normal reference can be seen from
the representation
\begin{equation}
  t_\nu=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{\chi^2_\nu/\nu}},
  \qquad Z\sim N(0,1),
  \label{eq:background-student-representation}
\end{equation}
where the normal and chi-squared variables are independent. The denominator
is random, which produces heavier tails than the normal reference. As
\(\nu\) increases, \(\chi^2_\nu/\nu\) approaches one and the Student
reference approaches \(N(0,1)\).

For MI, the estimated variance contributions are \(\Vhat(P)/n_P\) and
\(\Vhat(Q)/n_Q\). Their component degrees of freedom need their own
calculation because \(\Vhat(P)\) and \(\Vhat(Q)\) are nonlinear functions of
the probability tables. Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} derives these
MI-specific inputs to the same moment-matching structure.

For a single scalar restriction, normal and chi-squared Wald notation are
equivalent. If \(T\) is asymptotically standard normal, then \(T^2\) is
asymptotically \(\chi^2_1\). The signed form is retained here because the
parameter is the scalar difference \(I(P)-I(Q)\), its sign is meaningful, and
the Welch refinement replaces the normal reference for \(T\) by a Student
reference. The \((r-1)(c-1)\)-degree chi-squared law used for one-table
independence is a different second-order result.

The same principle extends beyond ordinary sample variances.
\citet{panwall2002} account for variability in sandwich variance estimates
using approximate Student and $F$ references. \citet{bell2002} use
Satterthwaite moment matching for regression variance estimates under
multi-stage sampling after showing that traditional linearisation estimators
can have substantial negative bias. \citet{kauermann2001} examine the
variability and efficiency of sandwich covariance estimation and propose an
adjustment for the resulting confidence-interval undercoverage. Related
adjustments and practical guidance are given by \citet{fay2001} and
\citet{imbens2016}. These are close
methodological precedents: estimating the variability of a variance estimator
and using it in a finite-sample reference are established strategies. The
present task is to derive the required inputs for the discrete MI
functional and assess whether the resulting reference is useful.

The direction of the finite-sample problem is nevertheless different here.
The cited regression corrections respond to underestimated uncertainty or
anti-conservative coverage, so a heavier-tailed reference can move inference
in the required direction. Near independence, the plug-in MI variance can
instead be biased upward. A heavier-tailed Student reference may then
reinforce, rather than offset, the resulting conservatism. This possibility is
derived more precisely in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} and evaluated in the
experiments.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Prior MI inference and related comparisons.}

MI uncertainty also has a broader literature than its leading normal
approximation. \citet{roulston1999} derives entropy and MI error estimates
using error propagation and examines them numerically. \citet{hutter2002}
and \citet{hutterzaffalon2005} derive moments of the posterior distribution
of MI under a Dirichlet model, including higher-order variance and shape
information. Their posterior uncertainty has a different conditioning from
the repeated-sampling distribution studied here. Their results establish
that higher-order MI uncertainty is not a new topic, but do not directly
validate the two-sample Student reference in this thesis.

Hutcheson's test is a direct information-theoretic Welch predecessor. It
compares two Shannon diversity estimates with an estimated variance and a
Student reference, using
\begin{equation}
  \nu_H=
  \frac{(s_{H,P}^2+s_{H,Q}^2)^2}
       {(s_{H,P}^2)^2/n_P+(s_{H,Q}^2)^2/n_Q}
  \label{eq:hutcheson-df}
\end{equation}
as its effective degrees of freedom \citep{hutcheson1970}. Here each of
\(s_{H,P}^2\) and \(s_{H,Q}^2\) is the estimated sampling variance of the
corresponding entropy estimator \(\widehat H\), and each variance component
is assigned its sample size, not \(n_i-1\). Applying the same assignment to the
two MI variance contributions gives a clear historical baseline. It does not,
however, derive how the plug-in MI variance itself fluctuates: MI depends on a
joint distribution and both margins, so moving mass into one cell changes
pointwise values elsewhere in its row and column. The expanded calculation
retains these changes.

The question of comparing MI values also has predecessors. In a segregation
application, \citet{mora2009} develop inference for an MI index and discuss
pairwise comparisons across groups and periods; the later peer-reviewed account
places that MI index within the wider segregation literature \citep{mora2011}.
They do not derive the
variance-sensitivity degrees of freedom used here, but the general aim of
comparing MI values is not new. \citet{guha2014} use MI for a different two-sample
question: whether two continuous distributions are identical. Their MI is
between a pooled observation and its sample label; their null is equality of
distributions, not equality of two within-population MI values. For discrete
data, \citet{drake2014} develop an MI-based \(k\)-sample test for the same
distributional-equality question.

The exact-independence boundary has likewise been studied separately.
\citet{marinescu2025} use a second-order delta-method argument for MI under
independence. More generally, their expansion combines a normal term with a
linear combination of chi-squared terms; the normal term vanishes at exact
independence. That setting explains why a first-order positive-MI comparison
must not be presented as a replacement for a one-table independence test and
suggests why second-order effects can remain relevant near that boundary.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Resampling and the weak null.}

Bootstrap methods can approximate a statistic's repeated-sampling behaviour
by drawing from fitted empirical populations, at greater computational cost
than a direct analytic table scan \citep{efron1979}. Sparse empirical support
can still be a problem. A label permutation instead pools the observations and
reallocates their group labels; it targets a different symmetry assumption.

Permutation requires a separate distinction. Pooling two samples and
permuting their group labels is exact under the strong null \(P=Q\), because
the observations are exchangeable. Under the weak null \(I(P)=I(Q)\), the two
complete distributions may differ, so raw label permutation generally targets
the wrong null. Studentized permutation can recover asymptotic validity for
some weak-null parameter comparisons \citep{janssen1997,chung2013}, but it is not the
standard one-table MI shuffle used to test independence.

The final comparison uses Normal Wald and the proposed Expanded Welch method,
which share the same effect estimate and standard error. Resampling is relevant
future work, but is not an exact finite-sample solution for the unrestricted
weak null without additional assumptions.

\section{Literature synthesis and methodological gap}

The literature establishes all of the broad ingredients used in this thesis:
plug-in MI estimation and bias correction, first-order MI variance, Wald
comparison of smooth functionals, and Welch--Satterthwaite moment matching.
It also contains prior comparisons of entropy and MI-related indices.

This thesis makes three contributions to two-sample inference for mutual
information. First, it derives an explicit observation-level sensitivity for
the estimated MI variance. Second, it uses this sensitivity to construct a
plug-in Welch--Satterthwaite degrees-of-freedom correction. Third, it provides
a controlled empirical evaluation across distinct equal-MI multinomial
populations. Together, these contributions clarify when Welch--Satterthwaite
correction is useful for MI comparison and, importantly, why it can become
overly conservative.

The remaining task is specific to MI. Hutcheson's direct information-theoretic
predecessor assigns each component its sample size; an ordinary-Welch analogue
uses \(n-1\). Both are fixed assignments. By contrast, established sandwich
adjustments derive effective degrees of freedom from the estimated variability
of the variance estimator itself. The plug-in MI variance requires its own
version of that calculation because it is a nonlinear function of the same
empirical joint table used to calculate MI. A change in one observation
modifies the cell probabilities, margins, pointwise-MI values, and their
weighted variance.

Chapters~\ref{chap:baselines} and~\ref{chap:expanded} develop the MI-specific
variance sensitivity and its Welch--Satterthwaite reference.
Chapter~\ref{chap:results} evaluates when that adjustment helps.

### A. Establish the evidence before drafting

Make an internal checklist with one row per important feature: figure/panel, varying parameter, observed trend, proposed explanation, evidence available, and disposition. Separate an algebraic consequence from a mechanism supported by diagnostics and from an unresolved cause. Do not print this administrative checklist in the thesis.

Start with saved population tables and summary files: moment means and variances, zero-estimate counts, positive-finite df counts, difference bias relative to observed SD, mean squared-SE/observed-variance ratios, rejection tails and valid-output fractions. Inspect a sequence of settings on both sides of a turning region; one isolated setting cannot establish the explanation of an entire hump.

Where summaries are insufficient, inspect the saved scalar repetition records for the joint behaviour of the difference, standard error, statistic and estimated df. These records do not retain the full count tables. If a support/occupancy explanation needs observed counts, first use population probabilities to calculate expected occupancy or reconstruct a small, purpose-specific set of count-table draws from the recorded sampling streams. Verify reconstruction against saved scalar outputs before using it as evidence. Keep any additional diagnostic separate from the published experiment and preserve all existing outputs. New simulation is a last step for a specific unresolved question, not a default rerun of the experiment.

A diagnostic association does not isolate a causal contribution. For example, mean squared-SE accuracy alone does not determine rejection: the distribution of the statistic and dependence between the difference and standard error can also matter. Likewise, an increase in expected occupancy is not, by itself, proof of why a plotted estimate peaks.

### C. Rules for clear explanations without excessive detail

1. **Lead with a recognisable feature.** Say which curve rises, flattens, dips or separates, and over which part of the parameter range. Avoid starting with a catalogue of coordinates or a vague statement that the method is affected by several factors.
2. **Name the intermediate quantity.** Connect sample size or MI to a change in the estimation bias, sampling variation, empirical support or cutoff that actually matters. Words such as “sparsity”, “nonlinearity” and “finite-sample effects” are incomplete explanations unless the next clause says what they change and how that changes the plotted result.
3. **Keep one main mechanism in a paragraph.** Use a flexible sequence of observation, reason, consequence and connection to the research question. Do not force four sentences or repeat a roadmap. Separate genuinely different mechanisms when necessary; do not create a subsection for each small feature.
4. **Use numbers selectively.** Keep benchmarks such as a ratio of one, the nominal 5% rate, required experimental settings and material tolerances. Add a numerical anchor only when magnitude changes the interpretation, a before/after comparison is needed, or a claim would otherwise be vague. There is no quota requiring a number in every results paragraph. Leave full ranges, coordinates and repeated rates in their displays.
5. **Replace duplicated recaps.** Reconsider even the numerical examples added in the preceding revision. Preserve their underlying evidence, but shorten a prose recital when the figure/table already shows it and a qualitative explanation is more useful. Keep a detailed worked example where it teaches the squared-mean/df relationship; do not remove necessary reasoning merely to reduce the word count.
6. **Do not repeat P and Q unnecessarily.** Explain their shared mechanism together, then identify a difference between them only if it changes the interpretation. Apply the same rule to repeated table sizes and the nominal/calibrated versions of a power trend.
7. **Use established symbols where they reduce ambiguity.** For example, use V(P), V-hat(P) and Var(V-hat(P)) consistently rather than switching among loosely named variances. Put enough words beside the expression to identify what varies. Add no new acronym or symbol solely to shorten an explanation.
8. **Use mathematics to expose a relationship, not decorate it.** Reuse the existing moment-matching and T equations. One short relationship may be useful to connect Figures 6.1 and 6.2; avoid a second derivation in the results chapter. Explain the direction of each effect in words.
9. **Match confidence to evidence.** State an exact algebraic implication directly. For a mechanism supported by diagnostics, identify the observed intermediate change and say it helps explain the curve. If an important shape remains unresolved, state the specific missing explanation once beside that result. Do not cover every paragraph in generic caveats or imply that every wiggle needs a separate causal story.
10. **Keep effects distinct.** Under equal MI, closer to 5% is the calibration target; under unequal MI, higher rejection measures power. A lower false-positive rate may reflect conservatism or invalid outputs. A small Monte Carlo interval establishes precision of the simulated summary, not accuracy of the statistical approximation. Do not swap these meanings while interpreting shapes.
11. **Protect reading order.** Introduce the comparison and its benchmark before the graph, interpret it nearby, and defer diagnostic machinery until its role is clear. Use short captions. Put additional diagnostic arrays or technical calculations in internal records or the existing supplement unless the manuscript needs them to support a material new claim.
12. **Use connected, direct academic prose.** Keep familiar subjects, explicit verbs, complete distribution names, and varied sentence lengths. Preserve the existing paragraph formatting. Exclude project paths, identifiers and workflow explanations from the manuscript.

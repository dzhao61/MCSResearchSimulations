\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Mutual information (MI) measures dependence between two variables. This
thesis tests whether two independent categorical populations have equal MI,
without requiring their joint distributions or margins to be the same.

The Normal Wald test divides the estimated MI difference by its estimated
standard error and judges the result against a normal curve. Expanded
Welch--Satterthwaite uses the same difference and standard error but judges
the result against a Student \(t\) distribution. It chooses the degrees of
freedom by estimating how much the MI variance estimate changes across
samples. The calculation
accounts for changes to cell probabilities, row and column totals, and the
MI values of individual cells. The Student reference is an approximation, not an
exact small-sample result.

The experiment uses fixed population tables from \(2\times2\) to
\(8\times8\), including unequal sample sizes and very sparse tables. Each of
3,111 settings uses 20,000 repeated pairs of samples and a 5\% test
threshold. The study reports false positives, detection, how often a method
returns a usable result, and runtime for each setting. It includes both
identical populations and different populations with equal MI.

Expanded Welch reduces excessive false positives in some settings but makes
already-low rejection rates lower still. It also produces fewer usable
results in sparse samples under the implemented validity rule. Wald is closer
to the target 5\% rate in all 24 main null settings at sample sizes 500 or
1,000. When both samples have size 50,000 and population MI is 0.02, both
methods are near 5\% in the tested populations;
very small MI remains difficult. Expanded Welch takes about 1.75 times as
long as Wald per test in the measured implementation.

Near independence, the correction can become very cautious and reject too
rarely. A separate diagnostic shows why changing the reference curve alone
cannot fix errors in the estimated MI difference or its standard error.

The evidence supports Wald as the simpler general baseline. Expanded Welch
may be useful as an additional check where Wald is known to reject too often.

\chapter{Experimental Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results}

The figures plot how often each test rejects across 20,000 sampled table
pairs. A point at a true MI difference of zero measures false positives;
points to its right measure detection when the population MIs differ.
The horizontal coordinate is fixed by the population construction, not
estimated from the random samples. Every plotted rate divides valid
rejections by all 20,000 replicates.

Blue solid circles show Normal Wald and
magenta dashed squares show Expanded Welch. The dotted line marks the
target 0.05 null rejection rate. A hollow marker warns that fewer
than 90\% of calculations were valid.
Where a zoom is shown, the upper panels use a 0--1 rejection axis and the
lower panels enlarge the 0--0.15 range.

We first ask whether lower rejection improves false-positive rates, then
examine its effect on detection. Sparse-sample validity shows when a low
rate instead reflects failed calculations. The final comparisons examine
errors shared by both methods and whether larger samples reduce them.
Additional matched figures are in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

\section{Calibration across table size and margins}

The first comparison uses a \(2\times2\) table with uniform margins in
both populations. Figure~\ref{fig:main-2x2-uniform} shows three equal sample sizes
and asks whether the lower Expanded Welch rejection rate is closer to
the target 0.05 at each size.

\mainlandscapefigure{2x2}{\(2\times2\)}{uniform}{both \(P\) and \(Q\) have uniform row and column margins \((1/2,1/2)\)}{uniform}{The calibration effect depends on sample size. At zero difference, both methods reject below 0.05 at \(n=100\) and 1,000; Expanded Welch is lower still. At \(n=10\), Wald is slightly above 0.05 and Expanded Welch below it. Validity is high in all three panels, so the low null rates at the larger sizes reflect conservative rejection rather than missing outputs.
Detection also changes with sample size. Rejection grows most clearly with MI difference at \(n=1{,}000\), where the two curves are close. At the smaller sizes, the lower Expanded Welch curve represents less detection as well as fewer false positives.}

In the \(8\times8\) row, Expanded Welch moves Wald's above-target
rate closer to 0.05. In the \(2\times2\) row, it moves an already-low
rate farther below the target. Invalid outputs cannot explain either
difference. Both methods use the same statistic, but Expanded Welch's
Student \(t\) distribution gives a p-value at least as large as Wald's.
It can therefore only lower rejection. We next examine what this means
for detection when MI differs.

\section{Detection as the true MI difference grows}

The main grid shows only differences up to 0.02 nats. Figure~
\ref{fig:broad-focus} extends a uniform \(2\times2\) comparison
to 0.2 nats to check whether the low rejection over small effects
persists as the true MI difference grows.
Both populations have row and column margins \((0.5,0.5)\), use the first
\(2\times2\) change block, and have sample sizes \(n_P=n_Q=100\).
The population MI values are \(I(P)=0.02\) and
\(I(Q)=0.02+|\Delta|\). The difference grid is
\(|\Delta|=0,0.001,0.002,0.005,0.01,0.02,0.05,0.1,0.2\) nats.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{broad_2x2_uniform.pdf}
\caption[Wider MI differences, uniform \(2\times2\)]{Rejection rates over
wider MI differences for uniform \(2\times2\) tables.}
\label{fig:broad-focus}
\end{figure}

Both methods detect the larger MI differences much more often. At zero
difference, both are below the 0.05 line; Expanded Welch is lower, and validity
is high. Rejection then rises markedly as the true MI
difference reaches the larger targets: by 0.2 nats both methods reject more
than 90\% of samples. Expanded Welch remains below Wald at intermediate
differences, but the gap narrows when detection is already high.

Starting closer to independence does not improve calibration in this

\FloatBarrier

The local calculation in Chapter~\ref{chap:expanded} explains why the
adjustment can be particularly restrictive near independence. For a fixed
sample size, smaller population MI can give fewer degrees of freedom and
a higher cutoff from the Student \(t\) distribution. The test then needs
a larger absolute statistic to reject, while the first-order MI
approximation is also less reliable.
These detection rates use the common nominal 5\% threshold. The methods
have different false-positive rates, so the curves compare detection under
the stated tests, not at matched false-positive rates.

\section{Validity under sparse sampling}

An observed table can omit many cells even when every population
cell has positive probability. Figure~\ref{fig:extreme-focus}
pushes the margins toward one dominant category while holding
\(n_P=n_Q=100\). It asks whether near-zero rejection in very sparse
tables means good calibration or simply a failure to calculate a test.
From left to right, the dominant row and column probabilities for
\((P,Q)\) are \((0.9,0.95)\), \((0.99,0.995)\), and
\((0.999,0.9995)\). Each remaining margin entry divides the leftover
probability equally between the two other categories. Both populations use
the first \(2\times2\) change block, with \(I(P)=0.00001\) and
\(I(Q)=I(P)+|\Delta|\). The difference grid is
\(|\Delta|=0,0.00001,0.00005,0.0001,0.0002\) nats.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{extreme_3x3.pdf}
\caption[Extreme-skew \(3\times3\) tables]{Rejection rates for
\(3\times3\) tables with increasingly dominant categories. The lower
panels enlarge the 0--0.15 range.}
\label{fig:extreme-focus}
\end{figure}

The adjustment can reduce false positives too far even before the most
extreme sparsity is reached. For dominant margins 0.9 and 0.95, the null rates are
0.0701 for Wald and 0.0088 for Expanded Welch: the adjustment moves an
excessive rate past 0.05.
At the most extreme margins, zero rejection mostly reflects failed
calculations. With dominant probabilities 0.999 and 0.9995,
both rates are zero, but only 0.0118 of Wald and 0.00005 of Expanded Welch
null calculations are valid. Rejection does not meaningfully grow over the
displayed alternatives there. The hollow markers show why near-zero
unconditional rejection cannot be read as good calibration in sparse data.

\FloatBarrier

Wald needs a positive usable total estimated variance. Expanded Welch also
needs positive finite degrees of freedom for both components and their
combination. Some count tables therefore
produce a Wald p-value but no Expanded Welch p-value. The primary
rate counts such failures as non-rejections. The exact
numerical validity criteria are given in Appendix~\ref{sec:validity-rules}.

\section{Checks on population and sampling choices}

Margins and MI alone do not determine finite-sample performance.
Moving association between the first block, rare cells and a spread pattern
changes rejection rates even though each population's margins and MI stay
fixed (Figure~\ref{fig:patterns-focus}). Changing from a four-cell transfer
to an ordinal log-linear construction also changes rates under matched
margins and MI (Figure~\ref{fig:construction-focus}). The cell probabilities
therefore matter beyond these summaries. The corresponding plots and
specifications are in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

Sampling allocation also changes the detection comparison. Reversing which
population has the larger sample or higher MI changes
the alternative curves (Figures~\ref{fig:imbalance-q} and
\ref{fig:imbalance-p}). The direction of the MI difference therefore matters
alongside the number of observations assigned to each population.
The low null rates are not confined to square tables.
Rectangular tables also show conservative rejection
(Figure~\ref{fig:rectangular-focus}). Calibration and validity therefore
depend on the particular populations and sampling allocation.

At exact independence, the first-order MI variance is zero.
Figure~\ref{fig:independence-focus} reports this boundary check.
Section~\ref{sec:independence-boundary} explains why a one-table
independence test has a different reference distribution.

\section{Limits of changing the reference distribution}
\label{sec:mechanism-results}

Changing the reference distribution leaves errors in the shared statistic
untouched. Both methods use the same estimated MI difference and the same
plug-in standard error, so any error in either quantity affects both tests.
Different small-sample biases can shift the estimated MI difference away
from zero even when the population MIs are equal. At equal sample sizes,
the leading bias corrections cancel in the difference. The remaining
biases need not cancel when \(P\) and \(Q\) have different margins.
The study does not separately measure how much this residual bias
contributes to rejection.

A supplementary check shows that replacing the shared standard error can
bring Wald's false-positive rate closer to 0.05.
For a \(3\times3\) different-skew null with \(I(P)=I(Q)=0.02\) and
\(n=100\), Wald rejects 0.0166 of independently simulated table pairs.
Keeping the same estimated MI differences but dividing each by one
fixed standard deviation estimated in a separate pilot simulation
raises rejection to 0.0551. The MI differences and normal rejection cutoff
are unchanged, so the increase comes from replacing the standard error.
The size of the plug-in standard error helps explain this increase.
In this setting, the average squared plug-in standard error is about
17\% larger than the measured variance of the MI difference. A larger
standard error tends to make the absolute test statistic smaller and rejection
less likely.

This replacement changes two properties at once: the size of the standard
error and its dependence on the estimated MI difference. A fixed standard
error cannot rise or fall with the difference across samples, so the check
does not separate these two effects.
Appendix~\ref{sec:mi-variance-covariance} gives the first-order covariance
between each population's MI and variance estimates.

\section{Larger samples and computational cost}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-focus} keeps each population pair fixed
under equal MI and increases the sample size. This tests whether the
poor null rates are temporary small-sample effects for the chosen
populations, and whether the answer changes with their common MI.
The rows compare \(2\times2\) and \(8\times8\) tables, and the columns use
common population MI values of 0.0001 and 0.02 nats. For each shape,
\(P\)'s row and column margins are \(m_k(0.7)\) and \(Q\)'s are
\(m_k(0.8)\), with \(k=2\) or 8. Both populations use the first
\(2\times2\) change block. The equal sample sizes are
\(n_P=n_Q=1{,}000,2{,}500,10{,}000,50{,}000\), plotted on a labelled
logarithmic scale. Every point has equal population MI.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{convergence.pdf}
\caption[Large-sample null behaviour]{False-positive rates as sample size
increases for \(2\times2\) and \(8\times8\) tables. The vertical axis
ranges from 0 to 0.10.}
\label{fig:convergence-focus}
\end{figure}

In the displayed \(2\times2\) and \(8\times8\) settings, larger samples
improve calibration much more clearly at MI 0.02 than at MI 0.0001.
All displayed calculations are valid. Across all 12
first-block shape--margin combinations at \(n=50{,}000\), the 0.02-MI
null rates range from 0.0470 to 0.0527 for Wald and 0.0466 to 0.0526 for
Expanded Welch. At MI 0.0001, the ranges remain 0.0089--0.0280 and
0.0076--0.0138. Convergence can therefore be slow near independence.
The sample size needed for acceptable calibration therefore depends on the
population setting.

Across the 60 timing regimes, the median call time is about

This extra cost is modest compared with the differences in calibration,
detection and validity. Expanded Welch can help where Wald rejects equal MI
too often, but its lower detection and validity elsewhere make it unsuitable
as a general replacement.
